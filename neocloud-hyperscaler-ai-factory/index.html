Running a GPU cloud

Neocloud, hyperscaler, AI factory: what the words mean

Three market words, the standard they sit beside, and how to look underneath each one

This site plays out on a GPU cloud and inside a scene it calls the factory. Customers and vendors use three words for places like these: neocloud, hyperscaler and AI factory. None of the three is a standard. No standards body defines them, and each vendor draws the boundary in a slightly different place. That matters to an engineer, because the word on a proposal tells you little about what you will actually get. This lesson starts from the one definition that is a standard, NIST's definition of cloud computing. It then takes each word in turn: what it usually means, what it leaves out, and real numbers for the hardware and the software underneath. It ends with two things that are often confused. An AI factory is a way of producing AI. A cloud is a way of selling compute. Our model, Qwen3-32B served by vLLM from its FP8 build of 34.3 GB, runs through every example.

Chapter 1 of 16

Three words that no standard defines

Market terms sit on top of one older definition, and only that one is fixed

Neocloud, hyperscaler and AI factory are all market terms. They show up in requests for proposal, analyst reports and vendor pages, and each source scopes them a little differently. An engineer who takes one of these words at face value can end up comparing offers that have little in common. The way out is to separate two questions. The first is who sells the service and what they concentrate on. The three words answer that one. The second is how the service behaves. A standard answers that one.

The standard is NIST Special Publication 800-145, The NIST Definition of Cloud Computing, published in September 2011. It defines cloud computing as a model for on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources, such as networks, servers, storage, applications and services, that can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort or service provider interaction. It says the model has five essential characteristics, three service models and four deployment models. The service models are Software as a Service (SaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS) and Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS). The deployment models are private, community, public and hybrid cloud.

None of the three words appears in that list. Neocloud is not a NIST service model or deployment model. Hyperscaler describes size and breadth. AI factory describes a way of producing AI, and many factories are never sold as a cloud at all. So the useful habit is to describe any offer twice. A neocloud that rents whole H100 servers through an API is, in NIST terms, a public cloud offering IaaS. A company that runs Qwen3-32B for its own staff on its own servers may call that an AI factory, and in NIST terms it may be a private cloud, or no cloud at all if nobody can order capacity for themselves.

The rest of this lesson takes the words one at a time and shows what sits underneath each, on the GPU cloud scene here and on the factory scene.

Chapter 2 of 16

Five characteristics on a GPU cloud

What makes rented GPUs a cloud is the behaviour around them

NIST's five essential characteristics are a good test for any GPU offer, whatever it is called. On-demand self-service means a consumer can provision capabilities such as server time and network storage as needed, automatically, without human interaction with each provider. On Northlight's scene that is the portal and its API. Tenant A orders gpu-srv-1 and nobody at Northlight reads an email. Broad network access means the capabilities are reached over the network through standard mechanisms. Resource pooling means the provider's resources serve many consumers in a multi-tenant model, assigned and reassigned as demand changes, and the customer usually knows the location only at a higher level, such as a country or a data center. Northlight's three servers of four H100 GPUs each are one pool of 12 GPUs that moves between tenants.

Rapid elasticity means capabilities can be provisioned and released, sometimes automatically, to scale outward and inward with demand. Measured service means the cloud meters use at a level that suits the service, such as storage, processing or bandwidth, so use can be monitored, controlled and reported to both sides. A footnote in the standard adds that this is typically done on a pay-per-use or charge-per-use basis.

The IaaS model fits a bare-metal GPU server well. NIST says the consumer can provision processing, storage, networks and other fundamental resources and run arbitrary software, including operating systems. The underlying cloud infrastructure stays with the provider. The consumer controls operating systems, storage and deployed applications, and possibly some networking components such as host firewalls. That is exactly what a tenant has on a rented gpu-srv-1: its own operating system, its own driver, its own vLLM.

The test also shows what is missing from plain hosting. A server rented for a year, set up by hand after an email, with an invoice that never changes, has no self-service and no measured service. It may be a fine product. It is GPU hosting, and the lesson From an order to a server and back shows how much machinery the self-service version needs.

Chapter 3 of 16

A neocloud is a cloud built around the GPU

A recent market term for providers that concentrate on accelerated computing

Neocloud is an industry term for specialised cloud infrastructure built mainly around GPUs and AI workloads. Thaki Cloud's insights series lists CoreWeave, Lambda, Nebius and Crusoe as providers the word is often used for. It overlaps heavily with GPU cloud and AI cloud. The owner's GRID deck uses it for the NVIDIA Cloud Partners, the providers that build to NVIDIA's reference designs. The term is recent, and its exact scope varies across providers and sources.

Neoclouds emerged because large-scale training and inference need things that most enterprise applications do not. The series lists nine: large GPU capacity, GPU-to-GPU interconnect, high-speed networking, high-throughput storage, bare-metal access, cluster topology, AI software stacks, GPU scheduling and reliable capacity. Each has a concrete form. NVIDIA's DGX H100 is a common building block. One 8U system holds 8 H100 GPUs with 640 GB of GPU memory in total. The GPUs talk to each other over fourth-generation NVLink at 900 GB/s per GPU. Eight ConnectX-7 cards of up to 400 Gb/s each carry the cluster network between servers, 3.2 Tb/s per system. Two more dual-port ConnectX-7 cards carry storage and in-band management, and a separate 1 GbE port carries the BMC with Redfish. Six power supplies of 3.3 kW in a 4+2 arrangement feed a system that draws up to 10.2 kW.

A general-purpose cloud can rent you a GPU. A neocloud is built so that dozens or thousands of these systems can be rented as one tightly connected cluster, with the topology known and the capacity reserved. NVIDIA's reference guide for these partners describes a software stack in three layers, IaaS for bare metal and virtual machines, CaaS with managed Kubernetes, and an AI PaaS for tenant workloads, and says the layered design lets partners offer multi-tenant AI services competitive with hyperscale providers.

The scene here is smaller than a DGX. Northlight's servers have four H100 GPUs each, and the qwen3 Deployment uses one GPU per Pod. The reasons are the same at any size.

Chapter 4 of 16

Hyperscaler: breadth, neocloud: depth

Hundreds of services on one side, a deep AI compute stack on the other

Hyperscaler is the market word for the largest general-purpose clouds: AWS, Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud. These providers run hyperscale data centers. IBM describes such sites as huge sites with at least 5,000 servers on at least 10,000 square feet, built for extreme scalability and used by those same three providers. The services built on top are what define a hyperscaler to a customer. They span general compute, databases, storage, security, analytics, serverless platforms, networking, AI and machine learning services, and integrations with enterprise applications. GPU instances are one product among hundreds.

A neocloud's catalogue is narrower and goes deeper into one area. Thaki Cloud's insights series lists what it usually centres on: high-end GPUs, bare-metal GPU servers, GPU clusters, high-speed interconnect, AI-optimised storage, training and inference infrastructure, and AI platform services. The series sums it up in one line. Hyperscalers emphasise breadth across cloud services, and neoclouds emphasise depth in AI compute.

The owner's GRID deck puts the history in one line of its own. The hyperscalers spent twenty years building three big clouds for standard web traffic, while telecom operators built ten thousand small ones for mobile access. Its next line, Guess who's selling tokens now?, points at the telecom operators as new sellers of tokens. NVIDIA's partner reference guide says its layered stack lets neoclouds offer multi-tenant AI services competitive with hyperscale providers. The same deck lists what the neoclouds had to solve that hyperscalers solved over decades. Multi-tenancy on bare metal was an immature research project, and power interconnection delays slowed site build-outs.

Neither model is better in general. Breadth matters when the AI work leans on everything else in the same cloud. Depth matters when the AI work itself is the hard part. The next chapter turns that into a decision with numbers.

Chapter 5 of 16

When each one fits

Where the data and the identities already live often decides it

The choice is rarely about the GPU alone. Thaki Cloud's insights series puts it this way. Organisations already deep in a hyperscaler's databases, identity, security and managed services may want their AI workloads close to those services. AI teams that put dedicated GPU capacity, cluster performance or AI-specific infrastructure first may prefer a specialised provider. If an application already depends heavily on a hyperscaler, the hourly GPU price alone may not decide the platform.

Data movement is one reason. Take a 20 TB training dataset that sits in a hyperscaler's object store. Over a 10 Gb/s link it takes 20 × 10^12 × 8 bits ÷ 10^10 bits per second = 16,000 seconds, about 4.4 hours, to move once. Over 100 Gb/s it takes 1,600 seconds, about 27 minutes. The Qwen3-32B FP8 weights, 34.3 GB, cross a 10 Gb/s link in about 27 seconds. So a model can move freely. A dataset that changes every day pulls the training toward wherever it lives. Inference traffic is the other extreme. A chat request and its answer are a few kilobytes, so the model server can sit in another cloud from the application that calls it, at the cost of one more network path and one more set of credentials.

The hourly price is the other reason. Total cost also includes storage, networking, data transfer, idle reserved hours, commitments and support. Three cases show how the answer changes. An internal assistant whose users sign in through the hyperscaler's directory, and whose documents sit in its managed database, probably stays there. A fine-tuning run on 64 GPUs that needs eight servers on one 400 Gb/s fabric for three weeks fits a neocloud's reserved cluster. The qwen3 Deployment, two Pods with one GPU each, fits either, and the deciding factors are price, region and the network path to its callers.

Chapter 6 of 16

Cloud GPU, GPU cloud, GPUaaS and neocloud

Four overlapping words, each pointing at a different thing

Four words get used almost interchangeably, and no taxonomy enforces a difference. A working set of meanings from Thaki Cloud's insights series is still useful. A cloud GPU is the resource itself, one GPU or a GPU-enabled instance reached through a cloud. GPUaaS, GPU as a Service, is the consumption model: GPU capacity used as a service instead of owned. GPU cloud is the broader environment that delivers and operates those resources. Neocloud is the provider category, an AI-focused provider whose environment may include GPUaaS along with Kubernetes, managed training and inference platforms.

GPUaaS is not a virtualisation technology. The same service model can deliver a GPU virtual machine, a shared or partitioned GPU, a dedicated GPU, a bare-metal server or a multi-node cluster. Bare metal can be GPUaaS when it is ordered, provisioned, metered and released like any other cloud resource. The line between GPUaaS and plain GPU hosting is the lifecycle: self-service requests, standard provisioning, an API or console, metering, and release and reuse. Remote access to a fixed server alone does not give that.

Neoclouds are not bare-metal-only either. Depending on the provider they sell bare-metal servers, GPU VMs, dedicated GPUs, reserved clusters, on-demand GPUs, Kubernetes, managed AI platforms and inference services. Nor are they for training only. Training drove the first demand for large clusters, and production AI now depends more and more on inference, which runs for as long as the model is in use.

The delivery form decides what fits. An H100 SXM has 80 GB and can be split into up to seven MIG instances of 10 GB each. Qwen3-32B in its FP8 build needs 34.3 GB for weights alone, so it cannot run in a 10 GB slice. On a whole H100 it leaves 80 - 34.3 = 45.7 GB for the KV cache and runtime. The BF16 build, 65.5 GB, leaves only 14.5 GB. The lesson A tenant is a boundary shows how slices are cut.

Chapter 7 of 16

Three layers: infrastructure, operations, workloads

A neocloud is more than GPUs for rent, and the middle layer is the cloud

Thaki Cloud's insights series gives a three-layer model for a neocloud. The bottom layer is AI-optimised infrastructure: GPUs, bare metal, high-speed networking and high-performance storage. The middle layer is cloud operations: provisioning, scheduling, allocation, metering, APIs, observability and lifecycle management. The top layer is the AI workloads that customers run: training, fine-tuning, inference, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), agents and high-performance computing (HPC). The flow goes upward. Infrastructure is turned into a service by the operations layer, and workloads run on that service.

Every part of the scene sits in one of these layers. gpu-srv-1, gpu-srv-2 and gpu-srv-3, the network fabric and storage are the first layer. The portal and API, identity, catalogue and orders, scheduler, provisioner, metering and billing are the second. Tenant B's qwen3 Deployment running vLLM is the third. Several lessons on this site open up the middle layer. From an order to a server and back follows provisioning and release. Why a job waits while GPUs are free follows scheduling. Metering is not billing follows metering. A tenant is a boundary follows isolation.

NVIDIA's reference guide for its cloud partners cuts the same stack another way, by service model. Its IaaS layer provisions bare metal and virtual machines. Its CaaS layer, containers as a service, offers managed Kubernetes. Its AI PaaS layer runs tenant-facing AI workloads. That guide also says compute, storage and networking are allocated on demand instead of statically provisioned.

The middle layer is what separates a pile of GPUs from a cloud. Two providers with identical servers can differ completely here. One may take days and a phone call to hand over a server, and the other may hand it over in minutes through an API, with metering that starts and stops on its own.

Chapter 8 of 16

What a neocloud offers, and what to check

Five advantages to expect and a longer list to verify

Depending on the provider, a neocloud may offer five things that a general cloud often does not. AI-specialised infrastructure means GPU clusters, networking and storage built around accelerated work. Dedicated capacity means reserved or dedicated GPUs and clusters. Bare-metal access means physical GPU servers without a virtualisation layer. AI-specific operations means GPU scheduling, health monitoring and cluster management. Flexible consumption means on-demand, reserved, dedicated or contract capacity.

None of that is automatic, so Thaki Cloud's insights series lists what to evaluate: regions and data-center locations, GPU models and available capacity, network and storage architecture, service availability, security and compliance, the scope of managed services, enterprise support, API and ecosystem integration, pricing and contract structure, and operational maturity. A more technical list, which this lesson draws from the nine needs above, adds the required GPU model, support for multi-node training, interconnect, storage throughput, whether bare metal, VMs and clusters are all offered, provisioning workflows, API and console access, metering and billing, the SLA and the AI software stack.

Bare metal changes where security sits. When a neocloud rents whole servers, the tenant has root on the host. The owner's NeoCloudCon brief makes this the central point. Anything enforced on that host, a firewall, a network policy, an agent, sits inside the tenant's own trust domain and can be switched off by root. Isolation between tenants then has to live below the host, in the switch fabric, the server's BMC and a DPU that the host cannot reach. The lessons A server nobody has to trust and A tenant is a boundary show how. When checking a neocloud, ask where its tenant isolation is enforced, and ask how a server is wiped between tenants.

The same GPU model can also perform differently at two providers. Interconnect, network bandwidth and latency, storage throughput, CPU and memory, cluster topology and the software stack all change the result for the same workload.

Chapter 9 of 16

Bare metal, then GPUaaS: selling hours

The first two steps of a value progression sell time on hardware

Thaki Cloud's insights series names a value progression in three steps: Bare Metal → GPUaaS → Token Factory. The series shows it apart from its NeoCloud OS architecture, which is Thaki's own product flow from customer infrastructure through NeoCloud OS to a customer neocloud service. That product flow is a different picture from the three-layer neocloud model in the chapter before this one. The series gives the three names and the order. It does not spell out what each step sells. The reading in this chapter and the next is this lesson's own: each step sells something different, and each adds a layer on top of the one before it.

Bare metal is raw, dedicated capacity. The provider sells a whole physical server, here gpu-srv-1 with four H100 GPUs, for a period. The unit is a server-hour or a GPU-hour. The tenant installs and runs everything above the hardware. The provider's revenue depends on how many hours are sold, and the tenant carries the risk of leaving GPUs idle. Metering is not billing works through such a day, where a reserved server earns the full price while its GPUs sit idle.

GPUaaS turns that capacity into a service a customer can select and consume. The unit is still mostly time, a GPU-hour, but now the customer picks a GPU model and count from a catalogue, receives it through automated provisioning, and releases it when done. Smaller units become possible. Tenant B can take two GPUs on gpu-srv-2 instead of the whole server. Billing can be on demand or reserved. Whoever orders still decides what runs on the GPU, and still carries the risk of idle time on what they ordered.

NIST's service models fit the first two steps with one distinction. Bare metal is IaaS, because the consumer controls the operating system. GPUaaS is IaaS when it hands over a whole server or a virtual machine, where the consumer again controls the operating system. When it hands over only containers on a GPU, the consumer no longer controls the operating system, and the offer sits closer to the CaaS layer in NVIDIA's partner stack, or to PaaS in NIST's terms. Either way, the difference between them is the operations layer. Bare metal can be sold with very little of it. GPUaaS needs all of it: catalogue, provisioning, scheduling, metering, release and reuse. The third step, the token factory, changes the unit itself, and that is the subject of the next chapter.

Chapter 10 of 16

A token factory sells what the GPU produces

The third step changes the unit from hours to tokens

In the third step the provider runs the model itself and sells its output. The customer sends a prompt to an API and pays per token in and per token out. The customer never sees a GPU, a Pod or a server. In NIST's terms this is close to SaaS, where the consumer uses the provider's application and controls none of the infrastructure. On this site the factory scene is exactly such a service. Callers reach the BNK data plane at 203.0.113.80, F5 AI Gateway checks keys and budgets, the endpoint picker chooses a vLLM worker, and the answer streams back as tokens. The site's lesson A node for every Pod mentions one real product named this way, Nebius Token Factory.

The risk moves with the unit. When the provider sells tokens, every idle GPU-hour is the provider's own cost. Earnings now depend on how many tokens each GPU-hour produces, which depends on the model, the batch size, the cache hit rate and the routing. That is why the picker and the cache lessons on this site matter to a business.

The arithmetic for our model shows the scale. In decode every step reads all the weights from GPU memory. An H100 SXM reads 3.35 TB/s. The FP8 build is 34.3 GB, so one GPU can do at most 3,350 ÷ 34.3 ≈ 97.7 decode steps a second, about 10.2 ms each. With batching one step produces a token for every sequence in the batch. With 32 sequences the ceiling is 32 × 97.7 ≈ 3,125 tokens a second, or 3,125 × 3,600 ≈ 11.25 million tokens an hour on one GPU. The two qwen3 Pods together top out near 22.5 million. Suppose, as an illustration, a GPU-hour costs the provider 100 units and the service reaches 40% of that ceiling, 4.5 million tokens an hour. The break-even price is then 100 ÷ 4.5 ≈ 22.2 units per million tokens. Better batching or more cache hits lower that number. Idle hours raise it.

Metering changes with the unit as well. The owner's AIGW deck contrasts a traditional load balancer, which counts requests per minute and bytes, with an AI gateway that counts tokens per minute and tokens per day.

Chapter 11 of 16

An AI factory produces AI again and again

A definition with no standard behind it, and the factory metaphor

AI factory is the third market word. Thaki Cloud's insights series defines it as an integrated infrastructure and operating environment built to develop, train, deploy and run AI models and services repeatedly, at scale. It brings accelerated computing, networking, storage, data pipelines, AI software, orchestration, security and monitoring together, so that the AI lifecycle runs as a production system. NVIDIA's glossary describes it as specialised computing infrastructure that creates value from data by managing the whole AI life cycle, from data ingestion through training and fine-tuning to high-volume inference. It names the primary product as intelligence, measured by token throughput.

The term is not standardised. NVIDIA, Dell, Cisco and infrastructure operators all use it, each with a somewhat different boundary. The common idea is that a factory is more than a data center full of GPUs. It connects that hardware to the software and the working routines that turn data and compute into production AI, again and again.

The word comes from manufacturing. A physical factory takes raw materials and energy, applies a repeatable process, and ships goods. The AI version has inputs, processes and outputs too. The inputs are data, energy, compute capacity, and models and algorithms. The processes are data preparation, training, fine-tuning, evaluation, deployment and inference. The outputs are trained models, predictions, recommendations, generated content, AI services, and tokens and other inference results.

Because it is a production line, it gets measured like one. Throughput, utilisation, reliability, automation and unit economics become the numbers that matter. NVIDIA's own site now presents its DSX platform with the tagline Build AI factories optimized for lowest cost tokens per megawatt, and the owner's GRID deck calls tokens per megawatt the metric that both a finance team and a power utility understand. This site calls its main scene the factory for this reason. The scene shows the inference end of the line, from a caller through the border, the gateway and the picker to a GPU worker.

Chapter 12 of 16

AI data center, AI factory, traditional data center

A facility, the production system on it, and the general-purpose site it replaced

IBM defines an AI data center as a facility that houses the IT infrastructure needed to train, deploy and deliver AI applications and services, with advanced compute, network and storage architectures and the energy and cooling to handle AI workloads. Thaki Cloud's insights series lists its typical needs: high-density GPU compute, high-bandwidth networking, high-performance storage, large-scale power delivery, advanced cooling including liquid cooling, and physical security and facility operations. The series draws the line this way. An AI data center provides AI-ready infrastructure. An AI factory turns that infrastructure into a repeatable AI production system with data ingestion and preparation, training, fine-tuning, evaluation, deployment, inference, monitoring, feedback, orchestration, security and governance. It calls this a practical distinction and says vendors may draw it elsewhere.

A traditional data center hosts a wide mix of enterprise work: databases, virtual machines, ERP systems, web applications and storage. IBM notes that conventional data centers are designed mostly around CPUs, while AI-ready ones need GPUs, and that NPUs and TPUs are appearing too. The network changes because distributed training moves large volumes between GPUs and servers. One DGX H100 has eight 400 Gb/s cluster ports. Storage changes because datasets, checkpoints and weights must be read fast.

Power and cooling change the most visibly. One DGX H100 draws up to 10.2 kW. Four of them in one rack, 32 GPUs in 32U, can draw up to 40.8 kW. NVIDIA's GB300 NVL72 is a fully liquid-cooled rack with 72 Blackwell Ultra GPUs and 36 Grace CPUs joined by 130 TB/s of NVLink. The owner's GRID deck sorts rack designs into three power tiers, below 15 kW, below 25 kW and above 25 kW, and lists air cooling and water cooling as separate items. A rack of four DGX H100 systems at 40.8 kW falls in the top tier. IBM adds that liquid cooling handles dense heat better and improves power usage effectiveness (PUE).

The largest difference is still software and operations. A factory needs scheduling, orchestration, data pipelines, training, serving, monitoring, security, governance and lifecycle management on top of the hardware.

Chapter 13 of 16

The GPU cluster is the factory's engine

Compute alone, against compute with data, platform, lifecycle and operations

A GPU cluster connects several GPU servers through high-speed networking so they act as one larger compute resource. It is the engine of an AI factory. Thaki Cloud's insights series puts the difference as two short formulas. A GPU cluster is the compute engine. An AI factory is compute plus data, platform, AI lifecycle and operations.

The extra parts are concrete. A factory also has data pipelines, storage, resource scheduling, training frameworks, fine-tuning, model registries, evaluation, model serving, inference, monitoring, governance, security and lifecycle automation. On the factory scene the cluster is gpu-1, gpu-2 and gpu-3, each running vLLM. Everything around them is the rest of the factory. Object storage and BIG-IP hold and serve the model weights, the subject of The road to the weights. The endpoint picker is part of serving: it chooses the worker for each request using load and cache state. F5 AI Gateway and the key service are governance: who may call, which model, how many tokens. F5 AI Guardrails is security for the prompts and answers. The BNK data plane at the border is where all of that is enforced on the wire. The semantic cache answers repeated questions without a GPU.

The owner's AIGW deck calls BNK the F5 AI factory application and delivery platform, which places it in this outer part of the factory and not in the cluster. The Distributed Inference deck lists an AI factory team among the people who run platform operations and AI infrastructure. So in many companies the word names a team as well as a system.

A useful test follows from the formulas. Count the GPUs, and you have measured the engine. Ask whether the site can repeatedly take data through training, evaluation and deployment to inference, and you have asked about the factory.

Chapter 14 of 16

The lifecycle that makes it a factory

Six steps that repeat, with feedback starting the next round

What defines a factory is that the lifecycle repeats. Thaki Cloud's insights series gives it six steps. First, raw data is collected, cleaned, transformed and made available. Second, accelerated compute trains a model or adapts an existing one to specific data and tasks. Third, the model is evaluated for quality, safety, latency, accuracy and business requirements. Fourth, approved models are deployed and serve inference. Fifth, teams monitor infrastructure utilisation, model performance, latency, errors, cost and capacity. Sixth, new data and production feedback start the next round of training, fine-tuning or model update.

Each step has its own lesson on this site. The text before training covers data preparation. A training run at scale covers training. From base model to your model covers fine-tuning. Replacing a model server covers deployment. Keeping GPUs busy and healthy covers monitoring. The factory joins them into one loop with the same storage, the same scheduler and the same records.

A deployment on our cluster shows the hand-off in real terms. A fine-tuned Qwen3-32B is evaluated and approved. Its FP8 weights, 34.3 GB, are written to object storage. A new revision of the qwen3 Deployment points at them. Kubernetes gives the new revision a new pod-template hash, so the new Pods get new names and replace qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-x7k2p and qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-m9q4t one at a time. Each new Pod reads the 34.3 GB and takes about three minutes to load before it reports ready. Monitoring then watches time to first token, errors and tokens per second, and logs of real use become data for the next round.

An AI factory is not for training only. The series lists foundation model training, fine-tuning, RAG, evaluation, batch and real-time inference, generative and agentic AI, computer vision, speech and language, and HPC and simulation. In production, inference runs continuously long after training ends, so the serving end of the line keeps using GPU hours for as long as the model is in use.

Chapter 15 of 16

A factory can span buildings and owners

It is a way of operating, and it can run on premises, in a cloud or across sites

An AI factory is not tied to one building. Thaki Cloud's insights series lists where one can run: an enterprise data center, a colocation facility, a private cloud, public cloud infrastructure, a hybrid environment, or several connected sites. It is closer to an AI production system and its operating routines than to a type of building. NVIDIA's glossary describes on-premises, cloud-based and hybrid AI factories. Its Enterprise AI Factory validated design is a full-stack design for companies that build their own on premises. Its cloud-based option puts the factory on cloud capacity instead of the company's own building. IBM notes that most businesses will not build an AI data center from scratch, and that hybrid cloud and colocation lower the barrier.

NIST's deployment models help here too. A private cloud, in SP 800-145, is for one organisation's exclusive use and may be owned and run by that organisation, a third party or both, on or off premises. So a company can run its AI factory as a private cloud inside a neocloud's building, on servers it rents whole.

The compute is not only GPUs either. Large factories rely mostly on GPUs, but the compute can include CPUs, NPUs and other accelerators. IBM lists NPUs and TPUs among the accelerators that AI-ready data centers increasingly use.

This site's scene of three factories shows the multi-site case. Frankfurt answers at 203.0.113.80, Amsterdam at 192.0.2.80 and Madrid at 192.0.2.160. Each has its own GPUs and serves the same models. F5 DNS answers for the one name and sends each user to a factory by health, capacity and distance, as One name, three factories explains. The owner's GRID deck describes a larger layout of three regions with nine AI factories, and Many sites, one platform covers tiers from a central factory down to edge sites.

Chapter 16 of 16

An AI factory is not yet a cloud service

Producing AI and selling capacity are separate jobs with separate layers

An AI factory and a GPU cloud are related, and they are different things. Thaki Cloud's insights series puts it plainly. An AI factory is an infrastructure and operating model for producing and running AI. A GPU cloud is a service model for delivering GPU resources through a cloud experience. A company can run a factory only for its own teams, with no customers and no catalogue. A telecom operator, a data-center operator or another infrastructure owner can instead put its factory capacity in front of outside users as a self-service GPU or AI cloud.

The path from one to the other is a layer. Factory infrastructure goes through a cloud operations layer and comes out as a multi-customer GPU or AI cloud service. That layer has a service catalogue, provisioning, multi-tenancy, GPU scheduling, metering, billing, SLA management, observability and lifecycle automation. The series lists the questions an owner faces at that point: product definition, ordering, provisioning, tenancy, usage metering, billing, the SLA, lifecycle automation, and recovery and reuse. It calls this cloud business enablement. Building the factory and operating a cloud service are different stages, and the second does not follow automatically from the first.

The owner's blog draft describes the network side of the same move in four tiers. First, where: choosing the site, with GSLB and DNS. Second, the provider border: bare metal handed to strangers, with BNK on BlueField DPUs, per-tenant fabric VRFs and firewall and DDoS policy. Third, the tenant gateway: a VRF and VNI per tenant, per-tenant egress, keys, budgets and model routing. Fourth, inference: choosing the GPU with F5 EPP. The draft notes that NVIDIA's cloud partner reference architecture leaves the tenant access network to partners, which is the slot BNK fills.

Two tenants, one factory and What leaves the factory show that tenant layer on this site.

What to keep

Ask what is underneath the word

Neocloud, hyperscaler and AI factory are useful words that no standard pins down. A neocloud is a cloud provider built around GPUs and AI work, deep where a hyperscaler is broad. Its value is in the operations layer between the hardware and the customer as much as in the GPUs. Bare metal, GPUaaS and a token factory are three ways to sell the same hardware, by server-hour, by GPU-hour and by token, and each moves more of the idle risk to the provider. An AI factory is infrastructure plus the routines that take data through training, evaluation and deployment to inference, over and over. A GPU cluster is its engine, an AI data center is the building it may sit in, and a cloud service is something it can become once a cloud operations layer is added. When a proposal uses any of these words, describe it again in NIST's terms, and check the GPU model, the capacity, the network, where isolation is enforced, and the unit you pay for.
