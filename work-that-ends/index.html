Kubernetes underneath

Work that ends

Jobs and CronJobs, and what a retry costs when the Pod holds a GPU

A Deployment keeps its Pods running for as long as it exists. Some work has an end: a fine-tuning run that trains for five hours and stops, or an evaluation that scores the model once a night. Kubernetes runs that kind of work as a Job. A Job creates Pods, counts the ones that finish with success, replaces the ones that fail, and then stops. A CronJob creates a new Job each time a schedule comes due.

This lesson follows one fine-tuning Job that holds a GPU on gpu-node-1 through a crash and a preemption, then a second one that waits in a queue, and then the nightly evaluation that runs in four parts. It shows how the Job controller keeps its count, each field that decides how often a Job retries and when it gives up, and what an operator reads in events, conditions and metrics to tell a healthy schedule from a broken one.

Chapter 1 of 14

A Pod that is meant to finish

A Deployment restarts whatever stops. A Job counts what succeeded

The team wants to teach Qwen3-32B the wording of its own support tickets. The training run needs one GPU for about five hours, and then it is done. A Deployment is the wrong object for this. Its Pod template only accepts restartPolicy Always, so when the training process exits with code 0 the kubelet starts it again. A Job is built for work that ends. Its Pods must use restartPolicy Never or OnFailure, and a Pod that exits with code 0 counts as one completion.

Two fields set the target. completions is how many Pods must succeed, and parallelism is how many may run at once. Both default to 1. Those two fields give three kinds of Job. With neither set, one Pod runs until it succeeds, as here. With completions set, the Job wants that many successes. With completions left out and parallelism set, the Job is a work queue: the Pods share a queue, and once any one of them succeeds no new Pods are created. Setting parallelism to 0 pauses a Job until it is raised again.

The template is the only required field of the spec. The Job's name must be a valid DNS subdomain of at most 63 characters, and the documentation advises the stricter rules of a DNS label because the name is copied into labels and Pod names. The Job controller, one of the loops in kube-controller-manager, reads the Job and creates one Pod from its template. The scheduler places that Pod on gpu-node-1, which has a free card. Even with parallelism 1, completions 1 and restartPolicy Never, the Jobs page warns that the same program may sometimes be started twice, so training code should cope with finding its own earlier output.

Chapter 2 of 14

How the Job keeps count

A generated selector, two labels and a finalizer on every Pod

A Job has to find its own Pods among all the others in the namespace. When the Job is created, the API server fills in spec.selector with a value that cannot overlap with any other Job: the label batch.kubernetes.io/controller-uid set to the Job's own UID. The documentation says that in almost all cases you should leave the selector alone. The controller puts that label, and batch.kubernetes.io/job-name, on every Pod it creates, so kubectl get pods with the selector batch.kubernetes.io/job-name=qwen3-finetune lists them, and kubectl logs jobs/qwen3-finetune reads the logs of one of them.

Counting must survive a Pod that is deleted before anyone looked at it. So every Job Pod is created with the finalizer batch.kubernetes.io/job-tracking, which stops the Pod object from disappearing. When a Pod ends, the controller counts it in three steps through status.uncountedTerminatedPods. It adds the Pod's UID to that field, removes the finalizer from the Pod, then removes the UID while it adds one to succeeded or failed. If the controller restarts halfway, the UIDs in that field tell it what was already in progress. The field is empty on a finished Job. Each of its two lists holds at most 500 UIDs, to keep the field under about 20 KB. This mechanism is stable since 1.26.

A finished Pod that still shows Terminating usually means the controller has not counted it yet. The controller also creates or deletes at most 500 Pods in one pass, so a Job with parallelism 2,000 fills up over several passes. kubectl describe job prints those counters on one line, Pods Statuses, as Active with the number Ready in brackets, then Succeeded and Failed. The example output on the Jobs page shows an older form of that line, with Running in place of Active.

Chapter 3 of 14

The container fails: Never or OnFailure

One crash, and two different parts of the cluster that can answer it

Two hours and twelve minutes in, at step 4,300 of 10,000, the training process hits a CUDA out of memory error and exits with code 1. What happens next depends on restartPolicy. With Never, the kubelet leaves the container alone. The Pod's phase becomes Failed, and the Job controller creates a new Pod with a new name. With OnFailure, the Pod stays on the node and the kubelet runs the container again inside it.

The kubelet restarts it at once the first time. After later crashes it waits 10 seconds, then 20, then 40, and at most five minutes. Once a container has run for 10 minutes without trouble, the kubelet resets that delay. Two feature gates change these numbers. ReduceDefaultCrashLoopBackOffDecay, alpha since 1.33 and off by default, starts the delay at 1 second and caps it at 60 on clusters that enable it. KubeletCrashLoopBackOffMax, alpha from 1.32 to 1.34 and beta and on by default since 1.35, lets each node set its own cap between 1 and 300 seconds, with the field crashLoopBackOff.maxContainerRestartPeriod in the kubelet's configuration file.

Both kinds of retry count toward the Job's limit. With OnFailure the count is the number of Failed Pods plus the container restarts inside Pods that are still Pending or Running. This Job uses Never for two reasons. The failed Pod is kept, so its logs can still be read with kubectl logs on that Pod. And the pod failure policy in a later chapter only works with Never. The cost of Never is that the new Pod starts from an empty container filesystem on whatever node the scheduler picks, while an OnFailure restart reuses the same Pod, the same node and the same volumes.

Chapter 4 of 14

Six retries, a growing wait and a deadline

Three settings decide when a Job gives up

The Job controller sees the Failed Pod and adds one to status.failed. Then it checks backoffLimit, the number of retries allowed before the whole Job is marked as failed. The default is 6. With Never, the Job fails when the number of failed Pods goes above the limit, so the seventh failed Pod ends it with the reason BackoffLimitExceeded. When that happens, every Pod of the Job that is still Pending or Running is terminated, and the controller records a Warning event with the same reason and message as the condition.

Replacement Pods do not come at once. The controller waits 10 seconds after the first failure, 20 after the second, 40 after the third, and never more than ten minutes. In the source the delay is 10 seconds doubled for each further failure, so the waits run 10, 20, 40, 80, 160 and 320 seconds, and the seventh would be 640 and is cut to 600. Only failures since the last success count, so a Job with many Pods is not slowed by failures from long ago. The wait is measured from when the failed Pod finished. The controller takes that time from the last container to finish. If it cannot, it uses when the Pod's Ready condition last turned False, then the deletion time minus the grace period, then the Pod's creation time.

The other limit is a clock. activeDeadlineSeconds caps the whole Job, however many Pods it has used. When it passes, the running Pods are terminated and the Job fails with the reason DeadlineExceeded. The deadline takes precedence over backoffLimit. A Pod template has an activeDeadlineSeconds field of its own, which limits one Pod, so the documentation warns to set it at the level you mean. A failed Job stays failed, and nothing in Kubernetes starts it again.

Chapter 5 of 14

A preemption is not a bug

A pod failure policy reads exit codes and Pod conditions

By default every failed Pod counts toward backoffLimit, whatever the cause. That is wrong in two directions. Suppose the training script exits with code 42 when its configuration is invalid. Six more tries will fail the same way, and each one loads the 65.5 GB BF16 build of the weights first. And when the scheduler removes the Pod to make room for a more important one, the training code did nothing wrong.

The Job's podFailurePolicy sorts these cases. Its rules are checked in order and the first match decides. A rule matches on container exit codes or on Pod conditions and names an action. FailJob ends the Job at once. Ignore replaces the Pod without counting it. Count is the default handling. FailIndex fails only one index of an Indexed Job. An exit code rule may name a container, including an init container, and applies to all containers when it names none. A policy holds at most 20 rules, a rule at most 255 exit codes and at most 20 condition patterns.

The condition DisruptionTarget marks a Pod that is going away for reasons outside its own code. Its reason says which: PreemptionByScheduler, DeletionByTaintManager for a NoExecute taint the Pod does not tolerate, EvictionByEvictionAPI for an eviction such as kubectl drain, DeletionByPodGC when the Pod's node no longer exists, and TerminationByKubelet for node pressure or a graceful node shutdown. A Pod evicted for going over its own container limits gets no such condition, because the cause would come back on a retry. Rules can also match conditions you add yourself. The task page patches a condition of type ConfigIssue onto a Pod stuck pulling an image that does not exist, which backoffLimit would never see because that Pod never reaches Failed.

At three hours the cluster is full, a third model server with a higher priority needs a GPU, and the scheduler preempts the training Pod.

Chapter 6 of 14

When the replacement is made

At the first sign of deletion, or only once the old Pod has really ended

A Pod that is being deleted still runs until its grace period is over, and while it runs it still holds its GPU. The field podReplacementPolicy decides whether the Job controller waits for that. It has two values. With TerminatingOrFailed, a replacement is created as soon as the old Pod has a deletion timestamp. With Failed, the replacement waits until the old Pod's phase is Failed. The field is stable since 1.34.

Which one applies depends on the policy from the last chapter. A Job with no pod failure policy defaults to TerminatingOrFailed, and it also counts a terminating Pod as failed at once, even if that Pod later exits with success. A Job that has a pod failure policy defaults to Failed, and no other value is allowed. The policy needs the final phase to judge the failure, and since 1.27 the kubelet moves deleted Pods to a terminal phase so that the phase exists to read.

For a GPU Job this matters more than it looks. With TerminatingOrFailed, the preempted training Pod and its replacement would exist at the same time. The replacement would ask for the same kind of card while the old Pod was still releasing it, so the number of Pods could go above parallelism for a while and the replacement would sit Pending anyway. With Failed, the replacement appears only once the card is really free. status.terminating counts the Job's Pods that are terminating at any moment, so an operator can see the overlap when it happens.

Here the policy is set, so the replacement qwen3-finetune-t9w3c is created only when the preempted Pod has reached Failed. Every GPU is taken by then, so it stays Pending until one is free.

Chapter 7 of 14

The GPU is held until the Pod ends

Checkpoints save the hours. A deadline frees the card

A Job's Pod holds its GPU from the moment the kubelet assigns the card until the Pod reaches Succeeded or Failed. Nothing else can be scheduled onto that card in between, even when the process inside is stuck. This is the reason a GPU Job should set activeDeadlineSeconds. Every retry is also a new Pod with an empty filesystem. Left alone, the second Pod would have started at step 0 and thrown away 129 minutes of GPU time. This Job mounts a volume at /checkpoints and saves there every 500 steps. Each new Pod loads the latest checkpoint, so the crash cost 300 steps, about nine minutes.

Six hours after the Job started, the third Pod exits with code 0. The Job controller records succeeded 1. It first adds the condition SuccessCriteriaMet with the reason CompletionsReached, and once every Pod of the Job has terminated it adds Complete. In Kubernetes 1.30 and earlier, Complete or Failed could be set while some Pods were still terminating. Tools that start the next step of a pipeline should wait for Complete, or accept that a Pod of the old Job may still be shutting down.

The controller records a Normal event with the reason Completed and the message Job completed, and the counter job_controller_jobs_finished_total goes up with result succeeded and reason CompletionsReached. kubectl describe job shows Completed At and a Duration. The Pods and the Job object stay, so their logs and status can still be read until the time to live set on the Job runs out.

Chapter 8 of 14

A Job that waits its turn

spec.suspend holds a Job back, and a queue decides when it runs

The team prepares a second fine-tuning run, qwen3-finetune-v2, before anyone knows when a GPU will be free. It creates the Job with spec.suspend set to true. A suspended Job exists and is valid, and the controller creates no Pods for it. The Job gets a condition of type Suspended with status True. This is how queueing systems for batch work hold Jobs back: they create or set every Job as suspended, decide when there is room, and flip the field to false.

While a Job is suspended, some fields of its Pod template may still change: node affinity, the node selector, tolerations, labels, annotations and scheduling gates. A queue uses that to point the Job at the nodes it chose. Up to 1.34 that is allowed only for a suspended Job that was never started. From 1.35 it is allowed for any suspended Job when the gate MutableSchedulingDirectivesForSuspendedJobs is on, which it is by default from 1.36.

Suspending a Job that is running is not free. Every running Pod that has not completed is sent SIGTERM and stopped within its grace period, and those Pods do not count toward completions. For a training run that means its last checkpoint is all that survives. Resuming the Job resets status.startTime to the current time, so the activeDeadlineSeconds clock starts again. The controller records Normal events named Suspended and Resumed, with the messages Job suspended and Job resumed.

A Job can also be handed to another controller entirely. spec.managedBy names it. The value must be a domain-prefixed path, a domain name, a slash and a path, such as kueue.x-k8s.io/multikueue, of at most 63 characters, and it is fixed once set. A bare word such as my-queue is refused by validation. When the value is anything other than kubernetes.io/job-controller, the built-in controller leaves the Job alone, and if nothing else is installed to manage it, the Job is never reconciled.

Chapter 9 of 14

All the GPUs or none

Gang scheduling binds a group of Pods together, or none of them

A distributed training run is one program spread over several GPUs. Suppose a later run needs four GPUs at once, one Pod each, and the four Pods talk to each other on every step. With ordinary scheduling, each Pod is placed on its own. If only three GPUs are free, three Pods start, hold their cards and wait for a fourth that may not come for hours. Three cards are then busy doing nothing, and they block other work that could have used them.

Kubernetes 1.36 added an alpha answer, behind the feature gate WorkloadWithJob, off by default. A Job may carry spec.scheduling with a schedulingPolicy of gang. Before it creates any Pods, the Job controller compiles that into two objects of the API group scheduling.k8s.io/v1beta1: a Workload, and a PodGroup made from the Workload's template. Each Pod is created with spec.schedulingGroup.podGroupName naming the PodGroup. The scheduler then binds no Pod of the group until all of them can be placed. gang.minCount says how many that is, and it defaults to the Job's parallelism.

The same block can ask for the Pods to share a topology level, such as one zone, and can set disruptionMode to all, so that the group is disrupted as one instead of losing a single member. All of spec.scheduling is fixed after creation except gang.minCount, which can change to scale the group. A Job that leaves spec.scheduling out gets the Basic policy and schedules exactly as before. The controller still creates a Workload and a PodGroup for every eligible Job, Basic ones included, owned by the Job and collected with it.

WorkloadWithJob only covers the Job controller's side. The Workload and PodGroup APIs, and the scheduler that honours them, sit behind other gates. GenericWorkload, alpha in 1.35 and 1.36 and beta but still off by default from 1.37, turns on the Workload and PodGroup APIs. In 1.35 and 1.36 the all-or-nothing algorithm is a separate kube-scheduler plugin behind the alpha gate GangScheduling. From 1.37 that gate is removed and gang scheduling is part of GenericWorkload. So on a 1.36 cluster a gang Job needs WorkloadWithJob for the Job controller, GenericWorkload across the control plane, and GangScheduling for the scheduler.

Chapter 10 of 14

A Job every night

A CronJob is a schedule and a template for Jobs

The team also wants a score for the running model every night. A CronJob does this. It holds a schedule and a jobTemplate, and its controller creates one Job from the template each time the schedule comes due. The schedule has five fields: minute, hour, day of the month, month and day of the week. 0 2 * * * means 02:00 every day. Steps such as */2 are accepted, a question mark means the same as an asterisk, and macros stand for common schedules: @hourly is 0 * * * *, @daily or @midnight is 0 0 * * *, @weekly is 0 0 * * 0, and @monthly and @yearly work the same way.

The field spec.timeZone says where that 02:00 is. When it is left out, the schedule is read in the local time zone of kube-controller-manager. Here timeZone is Europe/Berlin. The name must be a real zone from the IANA database. An unknown one is refused with unknown time zone followed by the name, and the value Local is refused with a message that asks for an explicit zone. kube-controller-manager carries a copy of Go's time zone database and uses it when the machine has none.

Each Job is named after the CronJob and the scheduled time in minutes since 1970, so the run for 27 November 2026 is qwen3-eval-29929020. The controller adds 11 characters, so a CronJob's name may be at most 52 characters long. The Job also gets the annotation batch.kubernetes.io/cronjob-scheduled-timestamp, written in RFC 3339 in the CronJob's own time zone, and the CronJob's status records lastScheduleTime and a list of the Jobs it has running.

Chapter 11 of 14

Four shards, two at a time

completions, parallelism and an index for each Pod

The evaluation set has 1,000 prompts. One Pod could send them all. Four Pods that each take a quarter finish sooner. The Job template says completions 4, parallelism 2 and completionMode Indexed. In Indexed mode each Pod gets a number from 0 to completions minus 1, here 0 to 3. Indexed mode requires completions to be set, and parallelism may go up to 100,000 in that mode. The container reads its number from the environment variable JOB_COMPLETION_INDEX. Index 0 takes prompts 0 to 249, index 1 takes 250 to 499, and so on. The index is also in the Pod's hostname, in an annotation and in a label.

The Job is complete when every index has one Pod that succeeded. The controller starts indexes 0 and 1, and starts 2 and 3 when those finish. These Pods ask for no GPU. They send their prompts to the Service qwen3 on port 8000, and the two model servers do the work. The Pods' hostnames make it easy to tell the shards apart in the model servers' logs.

An Indexed Job can also grow or shrink while it runs, as long as parallelism and completions are changed together and kept equal. When it shrinks, the Pods with the highest indexes are removed. This evaluation does not qualify, because it keeps parallelism 2 below completions 4 on purpose. A run that wants to spread over more model servers on a busy night would set both to the same number. completions is otherwise fixed once the Job exists, together with the selector, completionMode, the pod failure policy and most of the template.

Chapter 12 of 14

Retries per shard, and finishing early

backoffLimitPerIndex keeps one bad shard from failing the others. successPolicy ends a Job on its own terms

With one backoffLimit for the whole Job, a shard that fails again and again uses up the retries of all four. The field backoffLimitPerIndex gives each index its own budget, stable since 1.33. When one index runs out, it is marked failed and listed in status.failedIndexes, and the other indexes carry on. Setting it changes the default of backoffLimit to 2147483647, so the per-index budget is the one that matters. At the end, a Job with any failed index ends with the condition Failed, the reason FailedIndexes and the message Job has failed indexes, even when nearly every other index succeeded. Each Pod carries its index's count in the annotation batch.kubernetes.io/job-index-failure-count.

Two more fields work with it. maxFailedIndexes stops the whole Job once more indexes than that have failed, with the reason MaxFailedIndexesExceeded. And the pod failure policy action FailIndex fails one index at once, without using up its retries, for an error that a retry will not fix.

The opposite case is a Job that should succeed before every index has. successPolicy, stable since 1.33 and for Indexed Jobs only, holds up to 20 rules. A rule lists succeededIndexes, such as 0 or 0,2-3, or a succeededCount, or both. Training frameworks that use a leader and workers, such as PyTorch or MPI, often count the run as done when the leader is. A rule with succeededIndexes 0 says exactly that. Once a rule is met, the Job gets SuccessCriteriaMet with the reason SuccessPolicy, the remaining Pods are terminated, and Complete follows. If the Job has also met a failure rule, the failure wins.

The team adds backoffLimitPerIndex 1 and maxFailedIndexes 1 to the evaluation's template, so one flaky shard gets one retry and two failed shards stop the night's run.

Chapter 13 of 14

Late runs, overlapping runs and old Jobs

Four more fields on the CronJob

A schedule alone leaves three things open, and the CronJob has a field for each. concurrencyPolicy says what to do when the last Job is still running at the next scheduled time. Allow, the default, starts another Job. Forbid skips the new run. Replace deletes the running Job and starts the new one. startingDeadlineSeconds says how late a Job may start. If the controller could not create it within that many seconds, the run is skipped and counts as missed, and Kubernetes treats it as a failed Job. After an outage the controller counts the runs it missed. If there are more than 100, it records a TooManyMissedTimes warning and starts only the most recent one. Last, successfulJobsHistoryLimit and failedJobsHistoryLimit set how many finished Jobs are kept. The defaults are 3 and 1, and older Jobs are deleted.

The schedule is a promise of about once per time. The CronJob page says that in some situations two Jobs may be created for one time, or none, so the work should be safe to run twice. It also gives the one firm guarantee: with startingDeadlineSeconds unset or large and concurrencyPolicy Allow, each Job runs at least once. A CronJob can be paused with its own spec.suspend. That stops new Jobs and leaves running ones alone. Runs that fall due while it is suspended count as missed, and when it is resumed without a starting deadline, a Job for the most recent missed time starts at once.

Editing a CronJob changes only the Jobs created after the edit. A Job already running keeps the template it was created from.

Chapter 14 of 14

What an operator watches

Events, conditions and two families of metrics say whether the schedule is healthy

A nightly evaluation that silently stops running is worse than one that fails loudly. Three sources tell them apart. The first is events. On a Job, the controller records SuccessfulCreate with the message Created pod and the Pod's name, Completed with Job completed, and on failure a Warning whose reason and message copy the Failed condition, such as BackoffLimitExceeded. On a CronJob, the useful ones are Warning MissSchedule, Missed scheduled time to start a job, Warning TooManyMissedTimes, which suggests setting or lowering startingDeadlineSeconds or checking for clock skew, Warning FailedCreate, Error creating job, and Normal JobAlreadyActive when Forbid skipped a run. UnknownTimeZone and InvalidSchedule appear when the spec itself is wrong.

The second source is status. A Job's conditions carry the final reason: CompletionsReached or SuccessPolicy on success, and BackoffLimitExceeded, DeadlineExceeded, PodFailurePolicy, FailedIndexes or MaxFailedIndexesExceeded on failure. The Jobs page sets out a trade-off. Acting on FailureTarget starts a replacement Job sooner, but Pods of the old and new Job may run together. Waiting for Failed costs time and saves resources, which matters when those resources are GPUs. A CronJob's status holds lastScheduleTime and lastSuccessfulTime.

The third source is metrics. kube-controller-manager exports job_controller_jobs_finished_total with labels for the completion mode, the result and the reason, and job_controller_pod_failures_handled_by_failure_policy_total with the action. kube-state-metrics exports kube_job_status_failed, kube_job_failed, kube_cronjob_status_last_successful_time and kube_cronjob_next_schedule_time among others. A common alert fires when the time since kube_cronjob_status_last_successful_time grows past one schedule period, here a little more than a day.

What to keep

Count, retry, stop, clean up

A Job is a counter around Pods that are expected to exit. A generated selector and a finalizer on every Pod keep that count exact. completions and parallelism say how many must succeed and how many may run together, and Indexed mode gives each one a number with its own retry budget if you want one. restartPolicy decides whether the kubelet or the Job controller answers a crash. backoffLimit and activeDeadlineSeconds decide when the Job gives up, a pod failure policy keeps preemptions out of that count, and the replacement policy decides whether a new Pod waits for the old one to let go of its card. suspend lets a queue hold a Job back, and gang scheduling, still alpha, keeps a multi-GPU run from starting half way. A CronJob adds a schedule, a time zone, a rule for overlap and a limit on history, and its events and metrics show when a night was missed. On a GPU every one of these settings has a price in card hours, which is why the training run saved checkpoints to a volume.
