Running a GPU cloud

Metering is not billing

Four records that look alike, and the one number a GPU cloud lives on

A GPU cloud produces a great deal of data about its GPUs, and very little of it says what to charge. Four kinds of record are easy to mix up. Telemetry says how busy a GPU is. An allocation record says which tenant held it, from when to when. Billable usage is the part of that time the contract counts. A rated charge is billable usage with a price applied. This lesson follows one day at Northlight Compute through all four, with tenant A holding a server it does not use and an internal test running flat out on another.

On the way it opens up each record. It shows what NVIDIA's utilization counter really measures, how a sample travels from the GPU into Prometheus, why a gap in telemetry is not a zero, what Kubernetes can and cannot say about who held a GPU, and what a billing system asks of a meter. Then it works through a hypothetical month to show why the business runs on billable hours, and where mismatches between records cost real money.

Around that core it covers the rest of the commercial layer. It lists what sets the price of a GPU-hour and how tiered prices are computed, with a real product priced by data processed. It shows how a private GPU cloud uses the same records for showback and chargeback with no invoice at all, and what else metering is for. It treats capacity as an operations job, with a queue that grows while GPUs are free. It works through a service level agreement from promise to credit, and it ends with what an operations platform can and cannot promise.

Chapter 1 of 20

One day, three servers

The same twelve GPUs, seen by an engineer and by a bill

Here is the day this lesson uses. Tenant A has reserved gpu-srv-1 for the whole day under a commitment, and its training job has not started, so the four GPUs sit idle. Tenant B rents gpu-srv-2 on demand from 08:00 to 18:00 and works it hard. gpu-srv-3 belongs to no tenant. The provider's own engineers run a burn-in test on it from 09:00 to 15:00, a test that loads new hardware to find faults early. An engineer looking at dashboards would call gpu-srv-3 the busiest server and gpu-srv-1 a waste. The bill says the opposite. gpu-srv-1 earns the most and gpu-srv-3 earns nothing.

The unit used throughout is the GPU-hour: one GPU held or used for one hour. Northlight's three servers carry four GPUs each, so the site has 12 GPUs, and a 24 hour day holds 12 × 24 = 288 GPU-hours. Every number about this day is a slice of those 288.

Four systems watch the day, and each writes its own kind of record. The GPUs and their exporter write telemetry. The order service and metering write allocation records. Metering applies the product rules and writes billable usage. Billing applies prices and writes rated charges. They run on different clocks too. Telemetry is sampled every 30 seconds. Allocation changes only when an order changes state. Billable usage is computed once a day here, and rating once a billing period. The rest of the lesson shows which record each view comes from, and what goes wrong when one is used in place of another.

Chapter 2 of 20

Telemetry says how busy a GPU is

A gauge that is read every 30 seconds by default and knows nothing about customers

GPU telemetry usually comes from NVIDIA's DCGM exporter. DCGM is NVIDIA's Data Center GPU Manager, a tool that reads counters from the driver. The exporter publishes those counters as text on an HTTP port, 9400 by default, at the path /metrics. Prometheus, a monitoring database, fetches that page on a timer and stores each value with a timestamp. Fetching on a timer is called scraping. One of the counters is DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL_RATIO, the GPU's utilization as a ratio from 0 to 1. Exporter releases before 4.9.0 called it DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL and reported percent. Each sample carries labels for the GPU's index and its UUID. On Kubernetes the NVIDIA GPU Operator installs this monitoring together with the driver.

The page the exporter serves is plain text in the Prometheus exposition format. Each metric has a # HELP line with a description, a # TYPE line that says gauge or counter, and then one line per GPU with its labels in braces and the value after them. A gauge is a value that can go up and down, like utilization or temperature. A counter only grows, like energy used since the driver loaded.

At 10:00 the samples say 0.95 on gpu-srv-3 and 0 on gpu-srv-1, which is 95% and 0%. Both are correct, and neither names a tenant, a contract or a price. The exporter knows a GPU by its index and UUID and the host it runs on. Who rented the host is written somewhere else entirely.

Chapter 3 of 20

What the utilization number really measures

It counts the time a kernel was running, and nothing about how much of the chip it used

NVIDIA's nvidia-smi documentation defines GPU utilization as the percent of time, over the past sample period, during which one or more kernels was executing on the GPU. A kernel is one function launched on the GPU. The sample period is between 1 second and 1/6 of a second, depending on the product. The definition counts time, so it says nothing about how much of the chip each kernel used. A GPU that runs one small kernel without a pause reads 100%, even if most of its cores sit idle. The burn-in's 0.95 says that a kernel was running 95% of the time. It does not say that 95% of the GPU's arithmetic was in use.

Memory utilization is a different number again. It is the percent of time during which device memory was being read or written. It is not the share of memory that is allocated. The amount of memory in use is a separate field, DCGM_FI_DEV_FB_USED_BYTES in current exporter releases. NVIDIA also documents a false reading. During driver initialization with ECC enabled, the GPU and memory utilization can read high, because the driver scrubs memory. ECC is error correcting code memory.

The names and units changed recently. Exporter release 4.9.0, published on 6 November 2026, renamed 23 default and profiling fields to their DCGM 4.7 names. Eleven of them also changed unit: clocks to hertz, energy to joules, utilization from percent to a 0 to 1 ratio, memory from MiB to bytes and NVLink throughput to bytes per second. A dashboard or a rule written for the old names returns nothing after the upgrade, or reads 0.95 as if it were 0.95%. The same release sums per-process utilization for Kubernetes Pods, so a Pod sharing a GPU with others can show a ratio above 1.0.

Chapter 4 of 20

From the GPU's counter to a stored point

Three timers, and only one of them reads the hardware

A sample passes through three timers on its way into Prometheus. The first belongs to DCGM. The exporter starts DCGM's host engine, nv-hostengine, inside its own process, and asks it to watch each field at a cadence. That cadence is set by --collect-interval, in milliseconds, and its default is 30000, so 30 seconds. The host engine reads the hardware at that cadence and keeps the latest value in a cache.

The second timer belongs to Prometheus. Its scrape_interval decides how often it fetches /metrics. NVIDIA's documentation is precise about what a scrape does: it reads the latest cached sample, and it does not make DCGM query the hardware. Scraping every 10 seconds against a 30 second cadence returns the same value three times. Scraping less often loses samples. Prometheus's global default scrape_interval is 1 minute, so a job written by hand with no interval of its own keeps only every second sample of a 30 second exporter. On Kubernetes the exporter's Helm chart avoids that: it enables a ServiceMonitor by default with interval 30s, so the job the Prometheus Operator builds from it already matches the cadence.

The third timer is the timeout. scrape_timeout limits one fetch and defaults to 10 seconds. The exporter has its own --web-write-timeout for writing a response. NVIDIA's example job uses scrape_interval: 30s and scrape_timeout: 25s, and says to keep the timeout below both the interval and the exporter's write timeout. An exporter can also be asked too often. With --max-concurrent-scrapes set, requests beyond the limit get HTTP 503 while overlapping ones are combined.

The arithmetic for one metric is simple. At a 30 second cadence one GPU yields 24 × 3,600 ÷ 30 = 2,880 values a day, and the 12 GPUs yield 34,560. With the 1 minute global default Prometheus stores 1,440 per GPU and 17,280 in total.

Chapter 5 of 20

Gaps, staleness and the wrong average

When the exporter stops, the graph goes blank and nothing reads zero

At 12:00 the exporter Pod on gpu-srv-2 restarts and is gone for 2 minutes. Prometheus keeps trying. Each scrape writes a health series of its own, up, with the job and instance as labels. It is 1 when the target was reachable and 0 when the scrape failed. Prometheus also records scrape_duration_seconds and scrape_samples_scraped for every attempt. So the outage is visible, but only in up.

The utilization series behaves differently. When a target stops returning a series that it returned before, Prometheus marks that series stale, and a stale series returns no value. Until it is marked stale, a query takes the newest sample that is less than the lookback period old. The lookback is 5 minutes by default and is set with --query.lookback-delta. Either way the graph shows a gap or a repeated old value. It never shows 0%. Anyone who turns gaps into zeros has invented an idle period that did not happen.

The right function matters too. Utilization is a gauge, and the documented way to average a gauge over time is avg_over_time. rate, irate and increase are for counters only. For a counter such as energy in joules, rate and increase correct automatically for resets, for example when the driver reloads and the counter starts again from zero.

Finally, Prometheus is a monitoring store. Its local storage keeps samples for 15 days by default, and its documentation says that local storage is not clustered or replicated, so it is not durable against a lost disk or node. That is fine for dashboards. It rules telemetry out as the ledger a customer's invoice is built from.

Chapter 6 of 20

Labels can name a Pod, never a tenant

The exporter can ask the kubelet which Pod holds each GPU

When GPUs are handed out by Kubernetes, the exporter can add the workload to each sample. With --kubernetes set, it calls the kubelet's pod-resources API, a local gRPC service on the socket /var/lib/kubelet/pod-resources/kubelet.sock. The List call returns every Pod on the node with the devices assigned to it. The exporter waits up to 10 seconds for the answer. It then matches devices to GPUs by UUID, the default, or by device name, and adds the labels pod, namespace and container to the samples of that GPU. Optional settings add pod_uid and the Pod's own labels with the prefix pod_label_.

The exporter treats as GPUs the resource nvidia.com/gpu, MIG resources whose names start with nvidia.com/mig-, and any names given with --nvidia-resource-names. MIG, Multi-Instance GPU, cuts one GPU into isolated slices. In MIG mode the metrics appear per slice with labels such as GPU_I_PROFILE="1g.5gb" and GPU_I_ID="13".

These labels cost something. NVIDIA warns that each unique label set creates another Prometheus time series, so a cluster that restarts Pods often grows many short series. With the GPU Operator, setting dcgmExporter.enablePodLabels adds the Pod's own Kubernetes labels as label dimensions, and creates a ClusterRole and ClusterRoleBinding called nvidia-dcgm-exporter-read-pods with get, list and watch on Pods. It does not create the pod, namespace and container labels, which come from the pod-resources mapping. dcgmExporter.podLabelAllowlistRegex limits which Pod labels are emitted. From exporter 4.9.0 an empty allowlist is refused when Pod labels are on. In 4.8.4 an empty one included every label, and .* now asks for all of them.

Suppose Northlight runs tenant B's work as Pods in a namespace called tenant-b (illustrative). The 13:00 sample now says namespace="tenant-b". That is closer to a customer, and it is still only a name. The mapping from namespace to tenant, to contract and to price lives in the order service. And on gpu-srv-1, where tenant A's job has not started, there is no Pod at all, so the sample carries no workload label while tenant A still holds the server.

Chapter 7 of 20

Allocation says who held it

An interval with an owner, opened and closed by the order's state

The allocation record comes from a different place: the order. When an order becomes active, metering opens a record that names the tenant, the server, the number of GPUs and the start time. When the customer ends the order, the record is closed with an end time. Nothing in it comes from a sensor. It is written by the control plane, at state changes, so it is exact to the second and has no gaps. Tenant A's record covers the whole day. Tenant B's opens at 08:00 and closes at 18:00, the moment the order moves to terminating. The hour gpu-srv-2 then spends being wiped belongs to nobody. gpu-srv-3 has no record at all, because no order ever pointed at it.

The record is deliberately small. A good one carries an identifier, the tenant, the order, the product, the resource and its quantity, a start and an end, and the reason each end was written. It is written to durable, replicated storage, and it is never edited in place. A correction is a new record that refers to the old one, so the history of what was charged and why stays readable.

The two kinds of record disagree on purpose. At 10:00 telemetry said gpu-srv-1 was at 0% and gpu-srv-3 at 95%. The allocation records say gpu-srv-1 was held all day and gpu-srv-3 not at all. Both are right. They answer different questions.

Chapter 8 of 20

Held time inside Kubernetes

A GPU request is whole and exclusive, and its interval can be read from the API

When customers get GPUs as Pods, Kubernetes itself has the facts an allocation record needs. GPUs are extended resources offered by a device plugin, and the documentation says extended resources are integers and cannot be overcommitted. A Pod asks for whole GPUs. A GPU limit may be given alone, because Kubernetes then uses it as the request. If both are given they must be equal. A request without a limit is not allowed. So a Pod that asks for nvidia.com/gpu: 4 holds four whole GPUs that no other Pod can be scheduled onto, however busy it is.

Per-tenant caps use a ResourceQuota. For extended resources only quota items with the prefix requests. are allowed, for example requests.nvidia.com/gpu: 10 in tenant B's namespace, tenant B's quota of 10 GPUs.

The interval can be read from metrics that carry no utilization at all. kube-state-metrics publishes kube_pod_start_time and kube_pod_completion_time as Unix timestamps, and kube_pod_container_resource_requests for what each container asked for. Its documentation recommends the scheduler's own kube_pod_resource_request instead, as more precise, served at /metrics/resources.

Two details shape the record. Kubernetes events are not a ledger: the API server deletes them after --event-ttl, which defaults to 1 hour. And an interval does not end at an instant. A deleted Pod gets a grace period to stop, 30 seconds by default. Whether those seconds count is a product rule, and it has to be written down once so metering and billing apply it the same way.

Chapter 9 of 20

Billable usage is what the contract counts

Held time, filtered by the rules the customer agreed to

An allocation record says what happened. Billable usage says how much of it may be charged, and that depends on the product. Tenant A bought a dedicated reservation. The product is exclusive access to four GPUs for the period, so all 96 held hours are billable, whatever the utilization gauge showed. Tenant B bought on demand, billed for the time the order was active, so its 40 hours are billable. The burn-in on gpu-srv-3 used 24 GPU-hours at 95% and has no customer, so it adds nothing. A contract can also remove hours, for example by crediting back time lost to a provider outage. Today nothing was removed. Billable usage for the day is 136 GPU-hours.

The rules belong to the product catalogue, and each one is a short statement about time. Reserved: every held hour counts. On demand: hours between active and terminating count. Common refinements are a minimum charge, rounding up to a minute or an hour, and service credits when the provider breaks its promise. Each refinement has to be applied the same way in metering and in billing.

The two ratios of the day show why utilization is no proxy for revenue. Time-weighted utilization across all 288 GPU-hours was about 19%. The billable share was about 47%. Run the burn-in for 24 hours and utilization more than doubles while billable hours stay exactly where they were.

Chapter 10 of 20

Rating turns hours into money

The same hour can have two prices

Billing takes billable usage and applies prices. This step is called rating. The price for an hour depends on the customer's terms as much as on the product. The catalogue's list price here is 5,000 units for a GPU-hour. Tenant B pays that. Tenant A committed to a long reservation and gets 20% off, so it pays 4,000. Rating gives 384,000 units for tenant A and 200,000 for tenant B, 584,000 in total.

Divide that by the 136 billable hours and the result is about 4,294 units an hour. That figure is the effective price: what an hour really brought in after discounts. At list price the same hours would have brought 136 × 5,000 = 680,000 units, so the discount given today was 96 × 1,000 = 96,000 units. The effective price sits below the list price on every real price sheet, and revenue forecasts built on the list price come out too high.

Billing receives hours, tenants and products from metering. It does not receive utilization, because no rule in the catalogue uses it. The bill is also where a disagreement between systems becomes visible to the customer. If an order failed before it ever became active and a charge appears anyway, the customer sees it here first.

Chapter 11 of 20

What sets the price of an hour

Eleven factors, two ways to read a tier table, and a product priced by the gigabyte

Rating needs a price for each product, and the catalogue sets it from a list of factors. The usual ones for GPU capacity are the GPU model, the GPU count, the usage duration, dedicated or shared resources, bare metal or virtualized, the CPU and memory that come with the GPUs, storage, networking, data transfer, reservations or commitments, and the managed service level. An hour of an H100 with 80 GB of memory and an hour of an older card are different products with different prices. A server of 4 GPUs is not always priced at 4 times one GPU. A 24 hour minimum or a 12 month commitment changes the hourly figure. That is why an hourly GPU price alone may not represent the total cost a customer pays.

Two of the factors change the price because they change what the provider can do with the rest of the hardware. Dedicated means the tenant gets whole servers that nobody else uses, as tenant A has gpu-srv-1. The provider cannot sell an idle GPU in that server to anyone else, so the price covers the whole server whatever the load. Shared means the tenant gets a slice, such as one GPU of a server whose other three are sold to others, and the provider keeps the chance to fill the rest. Bare metal means the tenant gets the physical server with no hypervisor, the software layer that runs virtual machines. In this lesson's month example every bare-metal handover is followed by an hour of cleaning and re-imaging before the next tenant gets the server. Virtualized means the tenant gets a virtual machine with GPUs passed into it, and the host can be shared between tenants, though the GPUs still have to be cleaned between them. Each choice moves the provider's cost and its saleable hours, and the price follows.

Each factor that is charged on its own becomes its own meter with its own unit. GPU-hours add up over the period, so a billing meter sums them. Data transfer is counted in gigabytes and also sums. Storage held is a level that goes up and down, so it is usually reported as a value at a point in time. The invoice then has one line per meter, and the rating rule of each line can be different.

Prices often fall as quantity grows, through tiers. A tier table has the same rows in two modes, and they give different totals. Stripe's documentation names them. In volume-based pricing the whole quantity is billed at the rate of the tier it ends in. In graduated pricing each tier prices only the units inside it, and the parts are added. Stripe's own example has fonts at 7 NOK each for the first 5, 6.5 NOK for the 6th to the 10th and 6 NOK above that. A customer with 6 fonts pays 39 NOK under volume pricing, 6 × 6.5, and 41.5 NOK under graduated pricing, 35 + 6.5. Stripe's page shows the example in the reader's local currency, here NOK. It notes that under volume pricing the total might decrease when usage goes up. In the API the choice is the field tiers_mode, with the values volume and graduated.

Apply that to GPU-hours with a hypothetical Northlight table: 5,000 units for each of the first 100 GPU-hours in a month, 4,500 for hours 101 to 500, and 4,000 above 500. A tenant that uses 600 GPU-hours pays 2,700,000 units under graduated pricing, which is 100 × 5,000 + 400 × 4,500 + 100 × 4,000, an effective 4,500 an hour. Under volume pricing it pays 600 × 4,000 = 2,400,000. Volume pricing also has a cliff. 500 hours cost 500 × 4,500 = 2,250,000, and 501 hours cost 501 × 4,000 = 2,004,000, which is 246,000 less for one more hour. Metering must send the exact quantity for either mode to work, and the catalogue must say which mode it means.

A real product shows the same mechanics with a different unit. In F5's own pricing deck, F5 Insight for ADSP, F5's observability product for its Application Delivery and Security Platform, is priced by data processed per month, sold in blocks of 500 GB a month at an annual list price. The first block lists at $45,000 a year with no tier discount. Blocks two to four list at $36,000, which is 20% off. Block five onward lists at $27,000, 40% off. The deck's totals follow graduated pricing: 500 GB a month is $45,000, 1,000 GB is $81,000, 1,500 GB is $117,000, 2,000 GB is $153,000, and 2,500 GB or more starts at $180,000. Volume pricing would have made 1,500 GB cost 3 × 36,000 = $108,000. The deck also gives a sizing guide in BIG-IP instances, 50 for 500 GB rising by 50 per block to 250 or more for 2,500 GB, about 10 GB a month per instance. A customer gets a better price per gigabyte by buying more blocks up front: the first block works out at $7.50 per GB-month, and 2,500 GB averages $6.00.

The deck lists five SKUs, the catalogue codes a quote is built from. F5-ADSP-INS-500 is the 500 GB block as a term subscription. F5-ADSP-INS-500-A is the same block as an entitlement under FCP-A, and F5-BIL-ADSP-INS-500-A is its billing line, priced at $0. F5-ADSP-INS-SPT-PRM is support, included with the purchase. F5-ADSP-INS-TRIAL is a free 30-day trial. FCP is the F5 Flex Consumption Program: one agreement with a 3-year commitment and an annual spend that is adjusted each year on actual usage. For deployable software, which includes F5 Insight for ADSP, the commitment adjusts on the average growth in months 9 through 11 of each term, usage is tracked by telemetry, BIG-IQ or self-reporting depending on the product, and a monthly activity report gives the customer visibility. That is metering feeding a commitment in place of a per-unit invoice, the same split this lesson draws between measuring use and charging for it.

Chapter 12 of 20

What a billing system asks of a meter

Usage arrives as events with an identifier, a customer, a value and a time

Many providers hand rating to a billing product. Stripe documents its usage interface in detail, and it shows what any billing system needs from metering. Usage arrives as meter events. Each carries the event name configured on a meter, the customer ID, a numerical value and an optional timestamp. Each also has an identifier. If the sender gives none, Stripe generates one. A sender that gives its own, for example the allocation record's ID and the day, can safely retry soon after a timeout, because Stripe enforces uniqueness of identifiers within a rolling period of at least 24 hours. The documentation says this protection is aimed at accidental retries within very short intervals. A replay after that period, for example a day's events re-sent from a backup two days later, can be counted twice, so metering has to keep its own record of what was accepted. The identifier can be at most 100 characters long.

Time has limits. A timestamp must be within the past 35 calendar days and no more than 5 minutes in the future, the 5 minutes allowing for clock drift. Errors such as timestamp_too_far_in_past come back for events outside the window. Processing is asynchronous, so a summary may not yet show an event that was just accepted. Usage must arrive before the invoice for its period is finalized. An event accepted later can appear in summaries without changing that invoice or moving to the next one.

Each meter aggregates in one way: Sum adds every value in the period, Count counts events and Last keeps the most recent value. GPU-hours fit Sum. Corrections have a window too. Events can be cancelled only within 24 hours, and cancelling usage already on a finalized invoice does not change that invoice.

There are rate limits: 1,000 meter event calls per second per account, and only one concurrent call per customer per meter, so retries for one tenant go one at a time. Stripe advises pre-aggregating. Metering here sends one event per tenant per day, so 2 events today, in place of one per 30 second sample.

Chapter 13 of 20

Cost views: allocation, usage and idle

OpenCost splits the same hours the way this lesson does

Billing looks outward at customers. Inside the provider, a cost view assigns the cost of the hardware to whoever used it. The OpenCost specification, an open standard for Kubernetes cost, draws the same line this lesson draws. Resource allocation costs grow with the time a resource is provisioned, irrespective of usage, like an hourly rate for a CPU. Resource usage costs grow per unit consumed, like a cost per byte sent out. For resources with allocation costs, GPUs among them, the specification charges a workload for max(request, usage). For a GPU that is the greater of the GPUs requested and the GPUs used.

Applied to today, tenant A requested 4 GPUs and used none, so max(4, 0) charges 4 GPUs for 24 hours, 96 GPU-hours. Tenant B is charged 40. The burn-in is a workload too, if it ran as a Pod that requested 4 GPUs, so in a cost view it takes 24 GPU-hours, charged to the provider's own engineering team. On the invoice it was zero.

OpenCost calls what is left cluster idle cost: the part of allocation costs that is not allocated to any workload. Workload costs plus idle costs equal asset costs. Today that is 96 + 40 + 24 = 160 GPU-hours of workloads, and 288 − 160 = 128 GPU-hours idle. The cost view and the invoice agree on the two tenants and differ on the burn-in, and that difference is correct. One answers what the hardware cost and who caused it. The other answers what a customer owes.

Chapter 14 of 20

When nobody sends an invoice

Private GPU clouds keep the meter and replace the bill with showback or chargeback

Everything so far assumed customers who pay. Many GPU clouds have none. A bank or a research lab that builds a GPU cluster for its own teams runs what NIST SP 800-145, the standard definition of cloud computing, calls a private cloud: infrastructure provisioned for exclusive use by a single organization comprising multiple consumers, such as business units. Those consumers send no invoices to each other. The cluster still needs a meter. NIST lists measured service as one of the five essential characteristics of any cloud: the system controls and optimizes resource use through a metering capability, and resource usage can be monitored, controlled and reported, giving transparency to both the provider and the consumer. Its footnote says this is typically done on a pay-per-use or charge-per-use basis. Billing does not define a cloud. Measured consumption does.

Commercial GPU clouds connect the meter to prices and billing. Internal ones connect it to showback or chargeback. The FinOps Foundation, which publishes the common framework for managing cloud cost, draws the line between the two. Showback shows a group, at any granularity, the cost of what it is responsible for. Chargeback goes further and posts those costs formally into official accounting budgets, so that each owner of a profit and loss account bears the cost of what it consumed. The difference is the formality of sending expenses to the books. Neither is more mature than the other. Showback is always required in a FinOps practice. Chargeback depends on the organization's accounting policy, and when costs fall on a single cost centre the work of building official chargeback may not be worth it.

Run Northlight's day again as a private cloud. Tenant A becomes the research team, tenant B the product team, and the burn-in belongs to the platform team that runs the cluster. The allocation records do not change. Research held 96 GPU-hours, product 40, platform 24, and 128 were idle. An internal rate turns them into money. A common choice is a rate that recovers cost. In the month model later in this lesson, fixed cost spread over all 23,040 theoretical hours is about 1,302 units an hour, plus 300 variable, about 1,602, so call it 1,600 units a GPU-hour. The showback report for the day says research 153,600 units, product 64,000, platform 38,400 and idle 204,800, a total of 288 × 1,600 = 460,800. Under chargeback the same figures become journal entries against each team's cost centre. The idle 204,800 is a policy decision. It can stay with the platform team, or be spread over the 160 held hours at 1,280 units each, which would add 122,880 units to research's share.

The rule that made tenant A's idle reservation billable works the same way here. Research held four GPUs all day and used none, and the showback report says so in numbers. That is the point of metering inside a company. It creates accountability per tenant, and it is far more than pricing data. Inside, metering feeds team usage reports, showback, capacity planning and quota changes: a team that holds 96 hours a day and uses none is the first place to cut a quota. Facing customers, metering feeds billing, settlement of money between parties, usage reports the customer reads, and SLA or contract reporting. The records are the same. The audience and the consequence differ.

Billing is not required for every multi-customer GPU factory either. A service open to the general public is what NIST calls a public cloud. A service restricted to partners, consortium members or a defined group of customers can run a different commercial model. NIST calls infrastructure for the exclusive use of a specific community with shared concerns, such as mission, security requirements, policy or compliance, a community cloud. Picture three research institutes that fund Northlight's 12 GPUs together and are each guaranteed four. Each pays a fixed annual contribution written in the consortium agreement. The meter still runs, and its monthly report shows each member's GPU-hours against its guaranteed 2,880 a month, which is 4 × 720. If one member used another's idle share, the agreement says whether that is settled in money, in hours returned later, or not at all. A contract-based model works the same way: a fixed fee for a fixed share, with usage reported and never rated. In every one of these models metering stays. What changes is the commercial layer on top of it.

Chapter 15 of 20

From every hour to the hours that can be sold

A hypothetical month for a fleet of 32 GPUs

Now scale up to a month. The example is hypothetical and uses a fleet of 32 GPUs, which is larger than the 12 on this board. A 720 hour month gives 32 × 720 = 23,040 GPU-hours. That is theoretical capacity. Some of it can never be sold. Suppose each GPU is down for maintenance for one day, and each of the 8 servers is handed over 12 times with an hour of cleaning and re-imaging each time. That removes 768 and 384 GPU-hours. What is left, 21,888 GPU-hours, is saleable capacity.

Customers then hold part of it. In this example 11,520 GPU-hours are billable, which is 50% of the theoretical hours. The other 10,368 saleable hours found no buyer.

The chain has a name for each link because each link is moved by different work. Theoretical hours change only when hardware is bought or retired. Saleable hours are moved by operations: maintenance windows, failed hardware, and the time it takes to wipe and re-image a server between tenants. Billable hours are moved by products and sales: whether what is offered matches what customers want to hold. Reporting a single utilization figure hides all three.

Chapter 16 of 20

Capacity is an operations job

How much can be provisioned now, why a queue grows with GPUs free, and when to buy

The chain from theoretical to saleable hours is the result of capacity management, and capacity management is an operations function with its own daily view. The useful question is how much capacity can actually be provisioned to customers now, for the workloads they ask for. The number of GPUs the provider owns answers a different and much easier question. To answer it an operator keeps a view of current allocations, pending demand, reserved capacity, availability by GPU type, fragmentation, growth trends and planned workloads. A commercial service adds tenant quotas, maintenance, failures and new customer demand. From that view come three decisions: when to add capacity, where shortages are, and where resources sit overprovisioned.

Read the view for this lesson's day at 10:00. Allocations: tenant A holds gpu-srv-1, tenant B holds gpu-srv-2. Reserved capacity: tenant A's four GPUs are committed for the whole day, used or not. Internal use: the burn-in holds gpu-srv-3 until 15:00. Availability by GPU type: all twelve GPUs are H100s, and none is free to sell. A new order for a 4-GPU server arrives and waits. Here the queue grows from a real lack of capacity. Nothing can be done before 15:00 except to stop the burn-in early, which trades the provider's own testing for revenue. The burn-in is one case of internal use, which cuts saleable capacity together with maintenance, failures, provisioning time, cleaning, reserved buffers, fragmentation, and mismatches between the GPU types in stock and the types customers ask for.

Now a different day, where the queue grows for another reason. Tenant A holds all of gpu-srv-1 and, under a second order, two GPUs of gpu-srv-3. Tenant B holds two GPUs of gpu-srv-2. Four GPUs are free, two on gpu-srv-2 and two on gpu-srv-3, and the same order for a whole 4-GPU server waits. The free count says 4 and the largest free block on any server says 2. That gap is fragmentation: free capacity split into pieces too small for what is asked. The same thing happens inside Kubernetes, because one Pod always runs on one node, so a Pod that requests four GPUs needs four free on a single node. Telling the two cases apart takes two numbers from the view. If the total free is below the request, the queue grows from lack of capacity. If the total free covers the request and the largest free block does not, it grows from fragmentation, and buying hardware is the wrong fix. The right fix is placement: had the two small orders been packed onto one server, a whole server would be free. Many schedulers call this bin packing.

Growth trends and planned workloads answer when to buy. Take the hypothetical month of 32 GPUs from the chapter before. Saleable capacity is 21,888 GPU-hours. Suppose the operator keeps a buffer of 5% of theoretical hours, 1,152 GPU-hours, free for failures and urgent orders, so the ceiling for held hours is 20,736. Billable hours are 11,520 this month. If they grow by 1,500 GPU-hours a month, the ceiling is reached in (20,736 − 11,520) ÷ 1,500, about 6.1 months. If new servers take longer than that to buy, deliver, rack and burn in, the order has to go in now. A planned workload, such as a customer's training run booked for next quarter, moves the date forward by its own hours. Without the trend, a provider buys when the queue is already long, and the months of waiting are hours nobody can bill.

Price, cost and break-even

The whole month in six lines of arithmetic

A quick estimate multiplies every hour by the price: 23,040 × 4,000 is 92,160,000 units. That assumes every hour is sold. The effective price means here what it meant for the single day: what an hour really brings in after discounts. The month assumes an average discount of 20% across all customers, so its effective price is 5,000 × 0.8 = 4,000. On the day shown earlier only tenant A had a discount, 96,000 of 680,000 units or about 14%, and the effective price was about 4,294. The month's hypothetical customer mix is more discounted than that one day. With 11,520 billable hours at an effective price of 4,000, revenue is 46,080,000 units, half of the quick estimate. At the list price of 5,000 the same hours would have brought 57,600,000, so discounts cost 11,520,000 units this month.

Costs come in two kinds. Fixed cost is paid whether anything is sold or not: here 30,000,000 units a month. In a real GPU cloud it covers the hardware's financing, the facility, power commitments, network and staff. Variable cost follows usage: here 300 units for each billable hour, 3,456,000 in total. The result is 46,080,000 minus 3,456,000 minus 30,000,000, which is +12,624,000 units.

Hardware cost alone is not enough to price against. A full cost account for a GPU cloud covers power, cooling, the facility, network, storage, software, engineering, support, financing and customer acquisition. Cooling is a cost of its own because nearly every watt an H100 server draws becomes heat the facility has to remove. Storage is the volumes and datasets around the GPUs. Software is the licences for the operating systems, drivers, platforms and tools the service runs on. Engineering builds and fixes the platform. Support answers customers. Customer acquisition is what it costs to win each customer, spread over the months that customer stays. Split the hypothetical 30,000,000 that way: financing of the hardware 15,000,000, facility, power commitments and cooling 6,000,000, network and storage 3,000,000, software 1,500,000, engineering and support staff 3,500,000, customer acquisition 1,000,000. A provider that counted only the hardware financing would think it breaks even at 15,000,000 ÷ 3,700, about 4,054 billable hours or 17.6% of the theoretical hours, half the real figure. It would set prices and discounts that lose money every month while its own model said they were safe.

Each billable hour contributes 4,000 minus 300, so 3,700 units toward the fixed cost. Covering 30,000,000 takes about 8,108 billable hours. That is 35.2% of the theoretical hours, or 37.0% of the saleable ones. Below that share the month loses money. At 35% exactly, 8,064 hours, it loses 163,200 units. At 65%, 14,976 hours, it makes 25,411,200. The result moves by 3,700 units for every billable hour gained or lost, which is why the billable share is the number to watch.

Chapter 18 of 20

When the records disagree

Every mismatch is either a gift to a customer or a refund owed

Because four systems write four records, they can drift apart, and each kind of drift has a signature. An order that is active with no open allocation record means usage is being given away. The usual cause is a lost message between the order service and metering. An allocation record still open after its order completed or failed means a charge the customer will dispute. The usual cause is a missed state change, for example a provisioning failure that never moved the order out of active.

The records can also be right and the timing wrong. An allocation record that opens when the order is accepted, before the server is ready, charges for setup time the customer could not use. One that closes when the wipe finishes, in place of when the customer let go, charges for the provider's own cleaning. In this lesson the record opens at active and closes at terminating, and every other choice has to be written in the product definition.

Telemetry has its own failure modes, and none of them should touch the bill. An exporter outage leaves a gap. An upgrade across 4.9.0 renames fields and changes units. Collector rebuilds restart cumulative counters. These matter to anyone who bills on utilization, which is one more reason not to.

The defence is a reconciliation that runs on a schedule and compares the three sources: order states, allocation records and charges. Every active order has exactly one open record. No completed or failed order has one. Every server is either free, held, or being cleaned. Anything else goes to a person before the invoice is finalized, because after that a correction is a refund.

Chapter 19 of 20

An SLA is an operating framework

A promise with a definition, a measurement, a report and a remedy

Customers do not buy hardware performance alone. They also buy availability, support, incident response, recovery and service-level commitments. A service level agreement, or SLA, writes those commitments into the contract. That makes observability, incident management, recovery and SLA reporting part of the cloud business, as much as metering and billing are. Inside a company, teams can work under a shared internal policy, and a missed target leads to a conversation. External customers create stronger expectations, because the promise is in a contract, the customer can check it against its own records, and breaking it costs money.

A GPU cloud's commitments usually cover five things: resource availability, provisioning time, support response, failure recovery and maintenance communication. Each one needs four parts to work. A definition says exactly what counts, for example that a server is unavailable when it has no network connectivity, and that a slow GPU is not unavailable. A measurement says which record decides. A report tells the customer each period whether the promise was kept. A remedy says what the customer gets when it was not, usually a service credit, a reduction of a future bill. Northlight's catalogue in this lesson promises, as illustrative values: 99.5% availability per server per month, an order active within 30 minutes of acceptance, a first response to a severity 1 support case within 1 hour, a failed server replaced within 4 hours, and 5 days' notice before planned maintenance. A dashboard can show any of these numbers. Only the framework of definitions, records, reports and remedies makes them an agreement. That is why an SLA is an operating framework for delivering the agreed level of service, and much more than a monitoring metric.

Amazon EC2's public SLA shows the structure in full. It makes two commitments. The Region-Level SLA promises a Monthly Uptime Percentage of at least 99.99% for instances deployed across two or more availability zones in a region. The Instance-Level SLA promises at least 99.5% for each single instance, during any monthly billing cycle. Unavailable means the instance has no external connectivity. The Instance-Level Uptime Percentage is 100% minus the percentage of minutes in the month the instance was unavailable. For the instance level, uptime below 99.5% but at least 99.0% earns a credit of 10% of that instance's monthly bill, below 99.0% but at least 95.0% earns 30%, and below 95.0% earns 100%. The region level uses the same bands starting at 99.99%. Credits exclude one-time payments such as upfront payments for Reserved Instances, apply only against future payments, do not entitle the customer to a refund, and are issued only when the credit is more than one dollar. The customer must claim, by opening a case in the AWS Support Center, and the claim must arrive by the end of the second billing cycle after the incident. Exclusions cover causes outside AWS's reasonable control, the customer's own actions or equipment, and suspension under the agreement.

Apply that structure to tenant A's reservation of gpu-srv-1 for a 30-day month. The month has 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes. At 99.5% the server may be unavailable for 0.5% of them, 216 minutes. The 99.0% line is 432 minutes and the 95.0% line is 2,160. A switch port fails and gpu-srv-1 loses its network for 300 minutes. Uptime is 100% − 300 ÷ 43,200 = 99.31%, which falls in the 10% band. Tenant A's monthly bill for the server is 4 GPUs × 720 hours × 4,000 units = 11,520,000, so the credit is 1,152,000 units. Had the outage lasted 500 minutes, uptime would be 98.84% and the credit 30%, 3,456,000 units.

The hard part is the measurement. The definition decides which record counts, and the wrong record gives the wrong credit. A gap in DCGM telemetry is no evidence of downtime, because the exporter can stop while the server runs. The incident record is the source: monitoring detects the problem, incident management opens a record with a start time, the affected server and a cause, recovery closes it, and the minutes between are the unavailable minutes. The SLA report is computed from those records each month and sent to the customer. The credit goes to billing as a negative line before the invoice is finalized, the same deadline every correction in this lesson has. Whether announced maintenance counts as downtime is a choice the contract has to make, and maintenance communication is a promise of its own because a customer who knew in time could move a training run.

Chapter 20 of 20

Why the business runs on billable hours

Utilization is an engineering number. Billable share is the commercial one

Every line of the month's arithmetic used billable hours. Utilization appeared in none of them. Utilization tells an engineer whether a GPU is healthy and whether a workload is using what it rented, and that is valuable. It cannot say whether an hour earned money, because an idle reserved GPU earns and a busy test GPU does not.

So an operator who wants a better month works on the chain of hours. Shorter cleaning and fewer failed orders raise saleable hours. Halving the turnaround in the example returns 192 GPU-hours a month to the saleable pool. Products that match what customers ask for raise billable hours. Fewer and smaller discounts raise the effective price. Each of these moves a number in the month's arithmetic directly.

Utilization still has a commercial use, one step removed. A customer who reserves GPUs and leaves them idle for weeks is a customer likely to shrink the next commitment. A provider who shows that customer their own utilization helps them use what they pay for, and keeps the reservation. That is advice to a customer. It is never an input to the bill.

And the records have to agree. An allocation record that opens late, or stays open after a customer left, is money lost or a charge that must be refunded.

Two caveats keep this honest. The first is about software. An operations platform, the layer that ties catalogue, orders, provisioning, scheduling, metering, billing and lifecycle together, can standardize the products, automate the workflows, track service state, create consistent usage records and manage each resource from order to recycling. That improves operational consistency and lets the service scale without an engineer behind every order. It does not guarantee utilization, revenue or margin. A platform can make the 384 GPU-hours of cleaning shorter and the reconciliation reliable. It cannot make a customer want the hours that are left.

The second is about the product. Whether dedicated leasing earns more than GPUaaS, GPU capacity sold on demand by the hour, has no universal answer. Take gpu-srv-1 for a 30-day month. Leased to tenant A at 4,000 units an hour it brings 4 × 720 × 4,000 = 11,520,000 units, fixed for the month, with one handover and no hours lost to cleaning. Sold on demand at the list price of 5,000 it matches that only when 11,520,000 ÷ 5,000 = 2,304 of its 2,880 GPU-hours are billable, which is 80%. After the 300 units of variable cost per billable hour, the lease leaves 2,880 × 3,700 = 10,656,000 units, and on demand needs 10,656,000 ÷ 4,700, about 2,267 billable hours or 78.7%, to match it. Every on-demand customer also means another handover and another hour of cleaning. Above that share on demand wins, below it the lease wins, and the share depends on demand the provider does not control. A lease also ends, and an unrenewed lease is a server that has to find on-demand customers overnight.

What to keep

Four records, one chain

Telemetry, allocation, billable usage and rated charges answer four different questions, and only the last three lead to an invoice. Telemetry comes from the GPU and says how busy it was, as a share of time, sampled every 30 seconds, with gaps when an exporter stops. Allocation comes from the order and says who held it. Billable usage applies the contract, and rating applies the price. That is why a reserved GPU at 0% is billed and an internal test at 95% is free. The money follows one chain: theoretical hours, saleable hours, billable hours, effective price, revenue, cost. In the hypothetical month, 23,040 hours became 11,520 billable hours and a result of +12,624,000 units, with break-even near 35.2%. Around that chain sits the rest of the commercial layer: a catalogue whose price factors and tier modes set the rate, showback and chargeback where nobody is invoiced, capacity managed as a daily operations job, and SLAs whose credits come from incident records. The meter is the one part every model keeps.
