GPU and hardware

A rail-optimised GPU fabric

How GPUs are wired by rail, kept lossless on RoCE, split into tenants with EVPN, and proven before and after the hardware lands

A cluster of GPU servers needs two networks. One carries GPU-to-GPU traffic between servers during training and large inference jobs. The other carries everything else: storage, provisioning, management, the internet and the users. This lesson teaches how the first is built on rails, how its RDMA traffic (remote direct memory access, in which a network card writes straight into another machine's memory without either CPU copying the data) is kept free of loss, how the second is made aware of tenants, and how isolation between tenants is proven layer by layer. Every concept is shown as traffic on two HGX H100 servers, gpu-srv-1 for tenant A and gpu-srv-2 for tenant B, wired to four rail leafs, two east-west spines and an EVPN-VXLAN fabric of eight north-south switches.

The worked examples and the evidence come from a lab by Vishwas Tiwari of Aviz Networks. He built a scaled-down H100 AI factory on NVIDIA's HGX AI Factory reference architecture: 14 switches, 2 GPU servers, 16 GPU network ports on 8 rails, two tenants and 91 test cases, all in simulation. The lesson checks his design against NVIDIA's reference architecture and reads the switch documentation behind every setting. In the lab the two servers belong to different tenants, so traffic between them was blocked. Where a chapter sends GPU traffic from server 1 to server 2 to teach how a rail works, it treats the two as servers of one job, as they would be inside one tenant.

The closing chapters cover validation: Soft-RoCE, the 91 cases over Day 0, Day 1 and Day 2, what a digital twin can and cannot prove, and then proving readiness on the real hardware with NCCL tests. It borders several lessons. The network is part of the computer explains RDMA, ECN and pause frames on two servers. Keeping GPUs busy and healthy and What turns GPU servers into a GPU cloud pick up where this one stops.

Chapter 1 of 18

Why GPUs are wired by rails

The 2-8-9-400 server, its nine cards and the two fabrics they reach

NVIDIA's Enterprise Reference Architecture for HGX H100, H200 and B200 names its server by four numbers: 2-8-9-400. Two CPUs, eight GPUs, nine network cards, and 400 Gb/s of network bandwidth per GPU. Eight of the cards are BlueField-3 SuperNICs, single port at 400 GbE, one per GPU; the reference names the model B3140H. They connect to the compute fabric, also called east-west, which carries GPU-to-GPU RDMA between servers. The ninth is a BlueField-3 DPU, the B3220 in the reference, with two 200 GbE ports, cabled to two separate switches for redundancy. It connects to the converged fabric, also called north-south, which carries storage, provisioning, in-band management and user traffic. Inside the server the GPUs reach each other over NVLink and NVSwitch, 900 GB/s GPU-to-GPU on HGX H100 and H200 according to the reference document. The lab's servers match these figures, though the article names only BlueField-3 SuperNICs and a BlueField-3 DPU, not the model numbers.

The east-west fabric is rail-optimised. A rail is the set of network ports that sit at the same position in every server. GPU 1 of every server connects to rail 1, GPU 2 to rail 2, and so on to rail 8. On this board GPU 3 of gpu-srv-1 and GPU 3 of gpu-srv-2 both plug into rail leaf 3, so a message between them crosses exactly one switch: s1-gpu3 to ew-leaf-3 to s2-gpu3. The cables from one server fan out to eight different rails, so the rack stops being the unit of cabling and the rail takes its place.

A rail leaf is the leaf switch the rails plug into, and in NVIDIA's design each one carries two rails, rail k and rail k + 4. Rail leaf 3 takes rails 3 and 7, so GPU 3 and GPU 7 of every server meet on it. What decides whether traffic climbs to a spine is therefore not whether it changes rail but whether its two ends sit on different leafs. GPU 3 to GPU 7 of another server changes rail and still turns around in leaf 3. GPU 3 to GPU 4 changes leaf and has to climb. Chapter 3 shows the pairing in full.

The reason for the design is the shape of the traffic. Training splits work across GPUs, and after each step they must combine their results. Collective operations do that: in an all-reduce every GPU contributes its data and every GPU receives the combined sum. The library arranges the GPUs into rings, where each passes a piece to the next in a loop, or trees, where pieces flow up to a root and back down. Those collectives exchange data mostly between GPUs at the same position in different servers. Rail wiring puts those pairs one switch apart. Keeping the two fabrics separate has a second purpose that the lab states plainly: a storage burst never shares a queue with GPU-to-GPU RDMA.

Chapter 2 of 18

How NCCL keeps traffic on its rail

PXN moves data over NVLink to the right GPU before it leaves the server

Rail wiring only pays off because the collective library cooperates. NCCL, the NVIDIA Collective Communications Library, builds the rings and trees that carry an all-reduce or an all-to-all. Suppose GPU 4 of gpu-srv-1 has a chunk for GPU 3 of gpu-srv-2. GPU 4's own SuperNIC sits on rail 4 and reaches rail leaf 4. The receiver sits on rail 3 at rail leaf 3. Sent directly, the chunk would climb from leaf 4 to a spine and down to leaf 3.

NCCL avoids that with PXN. It first moves the chunk over NVLink to GPU 3 inside the sending server, then sends it out of GPU 3's own card on rail 3, so it arrives on the receiver's rail without touching a spine. The NCCL documentation describes the setting NCCL_PXN_DISABLE as disabling inter-node communication through a non-local NIC using NVLink and an intermediate GPU. Its default is 0, so PXN is on. NCCL_P2P_PXN_LEVEL defaults to 2, always use PXN.

A second setting decides whether a ring may change rails between servers. NCCL_CROSS_NIC defaults to 2: prefer the same NIC for each ring or tree, and use different NICs only when that performs better. A value of 0 forbids crossing rails entirely. So by default most inter-server traffic stays on matching rails.

The network sees the result. When both ends of a flow sit on the same leaf, traffic turns around inside that leaf and never climbs to a spine. In NVIDIA's 32-server shape each leaf holds two whole rails, as chapter 1 described, so the spines carry only traffic whose ends sit on different leafs, such as GPU 3 to GPU 4, and take over on failure. GPU 3 to GPU 7 changes rail but stays inside rail leaf 3. That is also why a cabling mistake hurts quietly. A GPU plugged into the wrong rail forces collectives through the spine or through extra NVLink hops, and nothing reports an error.

Chapter 3 of 18

Sizing in scalable units

Four servers at a time, two rails a leaf, and the switch counts that follow

The reference architecture grows in scalable units, SUs, of four servers. One SU brings 32 east-west ports at 400 Gb/s, 12.8 Tb/s in total, and 8 north-south ports at 200 Gb/s, 1.6 Tb/s. The east-west fabric is a non-blocking fat tree on SN5600 or SN5610 switches, which have 64 ports of 800 Gb/s that split into 128 ports of 400 Gb/s. Table 7 of the reference gives the counts. 32 servers, 256 GPUs, 8 SUs: 4 leafs and 2 spines. 64 servers, 512 GPUs, 16 SUs: 8 leafs in two planes of four, and 4 spines. 128 servers, 1,024 GPUs, 32 SUs: 16 leafs and 8 spines. The article's figures agree with the reference on every one of these.

Each leaf carries two rails, paired 1+5, 2+6, 3+7 and 4+8, the pairing chapter 1 introduced. On this board rail leaf 3 takes GPU 3 and GPU 7 of every server. So a pair such as GPU 3 and GPU 7 also meets inside one leaf, even though they are on different rails.

The arithmetic closes. At 32 servers there are 32 × 8 = 256 GPU ports, 64 per leaf. Non-blocking means each leaf has as many uplinks as server ports, so 64 uplinks per leaf, 256 in total, which two 128-port spines take exactly. Table 7 lists 256 node-to-leaf and 256 switch-to-switch cables.

The lab builds this 32-server shape with only two servers in it: 4 rail leafs, each serving rails k and k+4, and 2 spines, every leaf with its full set of spine uplinks, in the author's words. The article does not give the number of uplink links in the lab. With the eight north-south switches the lab has 14 switches: 6 east-west and 8 north-south.

Chapter 4 of 18

Pure Layer 3, a /31 per NIC

Every GPU port is a routed link, with no VLAN and no spanning tree

The east-west fabric in the lab is pure Layer 3. Every GPU port is a routed point-to-point link with its own /31. A /31 holds two addresses, and RFC 3021 lets a point-to-point link use both, one for the leaf and one for the card, because such a link needs no network or broadcast address. The 16 GPU ports of the lab use 16 such prefixes. There are no VLANs and no spanning tree on the GPU side, so no port is ever blocked to break a loop and no broadcast domain spans more than one cable.

"No VLAN" describes the GPU-facing ports, not the whole path. In the lab the rail ports also belong to tenants, and that changes how traffic crosses between leafs. On each rail leaf every GPU port sits inside its tenant's VRF, a virtual routing and forwarding instance: a separate routing table, so tenant A's routes and tenant B's never mix. To carry a VRF's traffic from one leaf to another, the lab uses VXLAN. VXLAN wraps the original packet in an outer IP and UDP header (UDP port 4789, RFC 7348) addressed from one leaf's tunnel endpoint, its VTEP, to the other's, and adds an 8-byte VXLAN header holding a 24-bit VNI, the VXLAN network identifier that says which network the packet belongs to. The VNI that carries a VRF's routed traffic is its L3 VNI: 4001 for tenant A on the east-west fabric and 4002 for tenant B. The author's check found 16 of 16 GPU rail ports in the right tenant VRF. Chapter 10 explains EVPN, the BGP routes that tell each leaf which prefixes sit behind which VTEP.

So routing between the leafs has two layers. The BGP underlay carries only the leafs' loopback and VTEP addresses, and ECMP, equal-cost multipath, lets a leaf reach every other VTEP through both spines. A tenant's /31 on another leaf is not in the underlay at all. It arrives as an EVPN type-5 route inside the tenant's VRF (RFC 9136), whose next hop is the remote leaf's VTEP, and packets to it are sent wrapped in VXLAN. Same-leaf traffic needs none of that, because the destination /31 is directly connected in the same VRF on the same leaf.

The wrapping has three consequences for RDMA between leafs. Each frame grows by 50 bytes: outer Ethernet 14, outer IPv4 20, UDP 8 and VXLAN 8, so a 4,174-byte RoCE frame becomes 4,224 bytes on the leaf-to-spine links. The spines see only the outer header, so the leaf must copy DSCP 26 into the outer IP header, or the spines queue RoCE in the wrong class. And when a spine marks congestion, it marks the outer header, so the receiving leaf must copy that CE mark onto the inner header when it removes the wrapping, as RFC 6040 specifies, or the receiving card never sees it and DCQCN never reacts. The article does not say how the lab set these. They are things to check on any VXLAN rail fabric.

The benefit of routed ports is fault containment. A mistake on one cable affects one /31, and the rest of the fabric keeps forwarding. A miswired port does not necessarily announce itself: it can still come up and route, as the cabling example in chapter 2 shows. The detector is the topology check of chapter 15, which compares the cabling LLDP reports with the intended design.

Chapter 5 of 18

How RoCE traffic is marked and queued

DSCP 26 to priority 3 for data, DSCP 48 to a strict queue for CNPs

RDMA over Converged Ethernet, RoCE, carries the InfiniBand transport inside ordinary Ethernet. Version 2, RoCEv2, puts it in IP and UDP to port 4791, so it can be routed. After the UDP header come the InfiniBand headers: the BTH, base transport header, with the operation, the destination queue pair (the connection) and a packet sequence number; for a write, the RETH, which says where in remote memory the data goes; and at the end the ICRC, a checksum over the fields that do not change in transit. RoCE slows badly when packets are lost. On the reliable-connection transport the receiver tells the sender which sequence number it expected, and many cards then resend everything from that packet onward, a scheme called go-back-N, so one loss costs a whole run of packets. So the east-west fabric is configured to avoid loss. The lab states the aim carefully: warn senders early, slow them down, and pause a priority only as a last resort. Lossless is an engineering goal, and no fabric guarantees it under every failure.

On Cumulus Linux one command sets the profile: nv set qos roce, lossless by default. Its documented defaults are the lab's design. The switch trusts the DSCP field of each packet, the six-bit differentiated services code point in the IP header that marks what class a packet belongs to. DSCP 26 maps to switch priority 3, which maps to traffic class 3, the lossless RDMA data lane. DSCP 48 maps to priority 6 and traffic class 6, which is scheduled with strict priority. That is where congestion notification packets, CNPs, travel. A receiving card sends a CNP back to the sender when data reaches it with a congestion mark that a switch set on the way, chapter 6 shows how, so feedback about congestion must never be stuck behind the congested data. The lab verified strict priority for DSCP 48 on every east-west switch. The remaining classes share bandwidth with traffic class 3 under ETS, enhanced transmission selection, the IEEE scheduler that gives each class a share of the link. Here the shares are set by DWRR, deficit weighted round robin, which serves the queues in turn by bytes in proportion to their weights, 50 percent each. The switch advertises the mapping to the cards over LLDP as an application TLV: UDP protocol 4791, priority 3. The profile also turns on PFC for priority 3 with a cable length of 100 metres; chapter 7 explains both.

The switch only classifies by the mark it receives. The host has to set it. NCCL's NCCL_IB_TC sets the traffic class its RDMA traffic carries, and its default, AUTO, means traffic class 0, which is DSCP 0. Left alone, NCCL data would land in traffic class 0 and never use the lossless lane. NCCL_IB_TC=106 gives DSCP 26 with the ECN-capable bit set. ECN, explicit congestion notification (RFC 3168), uses the two lowest bits of that byte: 10 means the sender can handle a congestion mark, and a switch that is congested changes them to 11 instead of dropping the packet. The traffic class byte holds DSCP in its top six bits, so 26 × 4 + 2 = 106. Between rail leafs the same mark has to reach the outer VXLAN header too, as chapter 4 explained. The card's own RoCE settings can apply the mark instead. Either way it is a host-side step, and a simulated fabric cannot check it on ConnectX or BlueField cards.

Chapter 6 of 18

ECN and DCQCN answer congestion first

Two senders, one receiver, worked against the configured thresholds

Congestion in an AI fabric usually comes from many GPUs sending to one at the same moment, which collectives do by design. The lab's first scenario is the simplest case: two GPUs send to one receiver at once. The receiver's link carries 400 Gb/s, and 800 Gb/s arrives at the leaf for it. On this board the two senders are GPU 3 and GPU 7 of gpu-srv-1, which both reach rail leaf 3, and the receiver is GPU 3 of gpu-srv-2. The excess, 400 Gb/s or 50 GB/s, piles up in the egress queue toward the receiver.

ECN marking is enabled on traffic classes 0 and 3. In the 5.16 documentation's sample for the lossless-single-ipool mode, the mode this lesson's fabric is set to, the minimum threshold shows as 146.48 KB and the maximum as 1.43 MB, with marking probability 100. The page counts KB and MB in units of 1,024, so these are 150,000 bytes and about 1.5 million bytes. On Spectrum-4 the intended minimum is 150,000 bytes and the value programmed in hardware is 159,744. The default lossless mode is documented differently, as a port ECN absolute threshold of 1,501,500 bytes for traffic class 3, close to the single-pool maximum.

With the single-pool thresholds the timing is short. At 50 GB/s of excess, the queue reaches the 150,000-byte minimum in about 3 microseconds and the maximum in about 30. Under the default mode's single 1,501,500-byte threshold, marking would start only at about 30 microseconds, ten times later, which leaves less buffer before PFC has to step in. Past the minimum the switch begins marking ECN-capable packets in traffic class 3, changing the ECN field from 10 to 11, congestion experienced. Between minimum and maximum the chance of a mark rises, and past the maximum every eligible packet is marked. The receiving card sees marked packets and returns a CNP on DSCP 48 to each sender. Each sending card lowers its rate under DCQCN, the rate-control loop in which the switch marks, the receiver notifies and the sender, the reaction point, cuts its rate (Zhu et al., SIGCOMM 2015). In the author's words, the senders cut their rate and the queue drains, with no pause and no congestion drops. If the two shared the port evenly they would settle near 200 Gb/s each, a figure this lesson adds as an illustration.

This is a model of the configured thresholds, as the author says, and not a measurement. Whether marks arrive in time, how fast the cards react and where the queue really peaks depend on the switch ASIC and the card firmware.

Chapter 7 of 18

When the burst outruns ECN: pause, xoff, xon and headroom

PFC stops one priority on one link, and the buffer above xoff catches what is already in flight

In the lab's second scenario the burst is too large for ECN to tame in time. A rate cut takes a round trip to arrive, and meanwhile the egress queue toward the receiver keeps growing. A switch with a shared buffer counts each waiting packet twice: against the egress queue it will leave from, which is what ECN watches, and against the ingress port and priority it arrived on, which is what PFC watches. So as the egress queue backs up, the ingress buffer for priority 3 on each leaf port facing a sender fills with the same packets. When it crosses the xoff threshold, the switch sends a PFC PAUSE frame upstream for priority 3 only. With two senders filling at the same rate, both ingress ports cross xoff and both senders are paused. PFC, priority flow control, is enabled on priority 3 alone, for receive and transmit. The sender stops that one priority on that one link. Everything else on the link keeps flowing.

A PFC frame is a 64-byte MAC control frame with EtherType 0x8808 and opcode 0x0101. It carries a class-enable vector, which says which of the eight priorities to pause, and eight pause timers counted in quanta of 512 bit times. At 400 Gb/s one quantum is 1.28 nanoseconds, so the largest timer, 65,535 quanta, pauses for about 84 microseconds unless a frame with a zero timer releases it sooner.

Data already on its way still arrives: bytes on the cable, the frame the sender is in the middle of, and whatever it transmits before it processes the pause. That data lands in the headroom, buffer space reserved above xoff. Headroom therefore depends on link speed, MTU, cable length and how fast the devices react. The RoCE profile sets cable length to 100 metres. The Cumulus documentation says the buffer calculation includes the cable length and that a wrong value wastes buffer, and it recommends letting the switch size these buffers itself. It gives no formula, so the arithmetic in the beats is an illustration. When the buffer drains below xon, the switch lets traffic resume. Nothing is lost, but every pause stops that priority completely for its duration, and a GPU waiting on that data waits too.

Chapter 8 of 18

The watchdog and adaptive routing

Breaking a stuck pause, and spreading load across the spines by what is busy now

Pauses have a failure mode. A queue that stays paused can push back on the port feeding it, which then fills and pauses its own upstream neighbour. This is a pause storm, and in the worst case a circular wait forms in which no paused queue can ever drain, a deadlock. The PFC watchdog guards against it. It watches each lossless queue. On Cumulus Linux it polls every 100 milliseconds by default, configurable from 100 to 5000, and acts after 3 detections by default, configurable from 1 to 1000. When a queue stays paused it stops honouring pause frames for that priority and drops new packets for that queue until the pauses stop, then restores normal behaviour. Its per-interface status reads OK or DEADLOCK, with a deadlock count. Dropping is the deliberate lesser harm: some RDMA packets are resent, and the fabric keeps moving.

Adaptive routing deals with a different problem. Plain ECMP picks a spine by hashing the fields of each flow, so a flow always takes the same uplink. RDMA traffic from collectives tends to be a few large, long flows, and two of them can hash onto the same uplink while the other sits idle. Adaptive routing spreads traffic across the spine uplinks by their load in real time instead of a fixed hash. It is a Spectrum hardware feature. The lab enabled it, and its effect can only be measured on real Spectrum switches. Spreading the packets of one flow over several paths has a cost: they can arrive out of order, and an ordinary RoCE card treats a gap in the sequence numbers as a loss and resends. NVIDIA's Spectrum-X design pairs per-packet adaptive routing with SuperNICs that place out-of-order packets straight into memory, so reordering does no harm there. The two halves only work together.

On this board both mechanisms live in the east-west switches. The watchdog runs on every rail leaf port. Adaptive routing matters on the climb from a rail leaf to the spines, the path that traffic between leafs, such as GPU 3 to GPU 4, takes wrapped in VXLAN.

Chapter 9 of 18

The north-south converged fabric

Provisioning, storage, management, internet and users, through one DPU with two ports

NVIDIA's reference calls the north-south side the converged fabric. It carries node provisioning, storage, in-band management, internet access and end-user connections. Each server joins it through its BlueField-3 DPU, the ninth card, with two 200 GbE ports cabled to two different switches. If one switch or one cable fails, the server keeps its way out through the other.

The reference describes the design as ideal for multi-user, single-tenant workloads, meaning many users of one enterprise. It separates the networks on this fabric with VLANs. At 32 servers it uses a collapsed design of 2 leafs and no spines. The lab departs from that on purpose, and the next chapter shows why.

The lab's north-south fabric has 2 spines and 6 leafs, each leaf with a role. The 2 compute leafs take the DPU ports, port 1 of every server on compute leaf 1 and port 2 on compute leaf 2. A server with two ports on two switches, both in a tenant VLAN, needs something that makes the pair act as one: EVPN multihoming (an Ethernet segment shared by the two leafs), MLAG between the leafs, or routed links to the host with no shared VLAN. Neither the reference architecture nor the article says which the lab used, so that design choice is open here. The 2 storage leafs connect the storage array. The 2 border leafs connect the internet and the customer's own network. Every leaf connects to both spines. Storage traffic, a checkpoint written by a training job or a dataset read at its start, runs on this fabric and never shares a queue with GPU-to-GPU RDMA on the rails.

The two fabrics are separate routing domains. The author's check found the east-west fabric isolated from the north-south one, and none of the north-south routes in the east-west fabric either. A GPU rail has no way to the internet, and a user request cannot reach a rail port. On this board the north-south fabric has no road to the rail leafs at all.

Chapter 10 of 18

Making the front end tenant-aware with EVPN-VXLAN

Transit spines, a VRF and L3 VNI per tenant, a shared storage VRF and type-5 routes

VLANs separate networks, but a VLAN alone gives no separate routing table. When two tenants share a fabric, each needs its own routes, its own gateways and a boundary that holds at every switch. The lab makes the north-south fabric tenant-aware with EVPN-VXLAN. VXLAN carries traffic between leafs inside UDP packets on port 4789, each tagged with a 24-bit VNI that says which network it belongs to. EVPN is the BGP address family that tells every leaf which addresses sit behind which other leaf, and in which VNI.

The article calls the two north-south spines EVPN route servers. In stricter BGP terms they are eBGP EVPN transit peers: they are not VTEPs, hold no tenant VRFs at all, and pass EVPN routes on between the leafs, while import is decided on each leaf by route target. A route server proper is the internet exchange construct of RFC 7947, and nothing in the article suggests that. Each tenant gets a VRF, its own routing table, on every leaf it touches, and an L3 VNI, the VNI that carries that VRF's routed traffic between leafs. Tenant A uses 6001 and tenant B uses 6002 on this fabric. Each tenant has a VLAN and a gateway on the leafs. Storage sits in a shared storage VRF. Each tenant subnet is advertised as an EVPN type-5 route, the IP prefix route of RFC 9136, carrying the tenant's L3 VNI.

Because tenants live only on the leafs, adding one never touches a spine. The author reports that adding a tenant changed no spine configuration on either fabric. The control plane checked clean: each north-south spine has 20 of 20 underlay sessions and 6 of 6 EVPN peers, one per leaf. The storage EVPN-VXLAN overlay is up across the storage and border leafs. From each tenant VLAN all 5 of that tenant's gateways answer, and a 9000-byte ping with don't-fragment set goes through with 0 percent loss, which proves that 9000 bytes passes on the paths tested. Whether those paths crossed VXLAN depends on where the gateways sit, which the article does not say. With a distributed anycast gateway the local leaf answers and the ping never leaves it, so only a ping to an address on a remote leaf proves room for the 50 bytes VXLAN adds. The author also reports north-south issues found and being fixed before the hardware ships.

Chapter 11 of 18

Proving isolation layer by layer

Ports, routing tables, data plane, onboarding, and the spines

A tenant boundary is only as good as its weakest layer, so the lab tests each layer separately. On the east-west fabric tenant A's VRF uses L3 VNI 4001 and tenant B's uses 4002. On north-south they are 6001 and 6002. The two lab servers belong to different tenants, gpu-srv-1 to tenant A and gpu-srv-2 to tenant B, which makes every cross-server test a cross-tenant test.

The first checks read state before any packet is sent. Port placement: 16 of 16 GPU rail ports sit in the right tenant VRF. Routing tables: 0 foreign prefixes in any tenant table, and 0 route-leak statements across all 14 switch configurations. Route leaking is how two VRFs are joined on purpose, the EVPN equivalent of peering two cloud VPCs. The article draws, without results, how such a leak would form a tunnel between tenants. Finding no leak statement rules out a deliberate leak only. Routes can also leak with no leak statement at all, for example when a VRF imports the wrong route target. What shows that no tenant can see another's routes is the other result: 0 foreign prefixes in any tenant table.

The next checks send traffic. From the rail VRFs, 0 of 64 cross-tenant addresses answer. RDMA connections from one tenant to the other, attempted on all 8 rails with Soft-RoCE, the software RDMA described in the next chapter, were all blocked. On north-south, from a tenant VLAN, the other tenant's gateways and host, the underlay loopbacks and the storage gateway all show 100 percent loss. Storage is shared through its own VRF. The article reports the 100 percent loss to the storage gateway without saying why it was expected. This lesson's reading is that a tenant VLAN reaching the storage gateway directly would have counted as a leak.

Two tests look at change. While tenant B was being pushed, tenant A pinged five times a second on three paths and lost 0 of 1,617 on each, so onboarding a neighbour did not disturb a running tenant. And adding a tenant changed no spine configuration on either fabric, so the spines never need touching when customers come and go. The lesson A server nobody has to trust shows the same boundary built on the DPU of each server.

Chapter 12 of 18

Validating in a simulation: RDMA with no RDMA card

Soft-RoCE on every rail interface, and what it could and could not show

Everything so far can be configured, and most of it tested, long before the GPU servers arrive, which can take months. Chapter 14 sets out what cannot: PFC, ECN, DCQCN, buffers, the watchdog and adaptive routing under real load. Mistakes found early cost an edit. Found during bring-up they cost GPUs that are installed and paid for and run nothing. The tool for testing early is a digital twin: a working software copy of the network, with the same switch operating system, the same topology and the same configuration as the real one, run on virtual machines so that tests behave as they would on the floor wherever the hardware is not involved. Tiwari's lab ran the whole fabric as such a twin, and its first problem was RDMA itself. A virtual lab has no ConnectX or BlueField cards, so the lab used Soft-RoCE, the Linux software implementation of RoCE, called rxe. It is created on top of an ordinary network interface. The rdma-core documentation gives the command as rdma link add rxe_eth0 type rxe netdev eth0, and rdma link lists the result. Any RoCE application can then run over it. The lab created 8 Soft-RoCE devices per server, one on each GPU rail interface.

The first test stayed on one host: an RDMA ping from the server to itself, which proved the software RDMA stack worked. The lab then attempted RDMA ping and bandwidth tests across the fabric on all 8 rails, with packet captures on the hosts and on the leafs.

The result was exactly what the tenant design demands, and also a limit. The two servers belong to different tenants, so every attempt across the fabric was cross-tenant, and every one was blocked. Only connection set-up packets were ever sent. That proves the boundary holds for RDMA. It also means no RDMA data crossed the fabric, so DSCP 26 on RDMA data was never seen on the wire, and the lossless path was never exercised end to end. Even when that test runs, rxe will mark DSCP 26 only if a traffic class is set, as NCCL_IB_TC or the application's own setting would do. The rdma-core documentation for rxe says nothing about a default marking, so the captures have to be checked: a run that left the traffic class unset would most likely show DSCP 0 and test the wrong lane. The author names the next test: same-tenant RDMA through the fabric, which needs a second server per tenant.

Soft-RoCE runs in the host's CPU, so even that test would show function and markings, never speed. Bandwidth from rxe says nothing about a 400 Gb/s SuperNIC.

Chapter 13 of 18

The twin itself

What a simulated switch runs, and what DSX Air builds around it

The author's lab ran in GNS3, an open source network emulator that runs virtual routers and switches and wires them together, with NVIDIA Cumulus VX switches, the virtual-machine edition of the Cumulus Linux switch operating system, all 14 of them, deployed from a ready-to-test virtual appliance from Aviz, ONES, which built the whole fabric with both tenants. A second Aviz blog, Can AI Factories Be Built Faster Without Hardware? Here's What NVIDIA & Aviz Reveal, presents Aviz ONES together with NVIDIA DSX Air, NVIDIA's cloud-hosted simulation platform. NVIDIA's Cumulus VX documentation page says it no longer provides Cumulus VX as a standalone image for Cumulus Linux and tells users to simulate a switch in NVIDIA Air instead. NVIDIA describes its DSX Air twin as an executable, API-accessible model of the factory's topology and software that can sit in a CI/CD pipeline. Aviz and NVIDIA both sell these tools, so their benefit claims are given as theirs.

A simulated switch runs the same Cumulus Linux operating system and features as an NVIDIA Ethernet switch, with the data plane carried by the Linux kernel: VLANs, VXLAN bridging and routing. That is why routing, EVPN, configuration and automation can be checked in the twin exactly as they will be configured in production, while forwarding itself is done by the Linux kernel and not by the switch ASIC. Air builds a topology from a JSON file, which NVIDIA recommends over DOT, the plain-text graph format of the Graphviz tools. Each node names its operating system, such as cumulus-vx-5.16.1 or generic/ubuntu2404, and its CPUs, memory and storage, and links join interfaces like swp1 on a leaf to eth1 on a server. Defaults are 2 GB of memory and 10 GB of disk. The switch model only pre-fills the port count and does not change the simulation.

Air adds an out-of-band management network unless told not to: an oob-mgmt-switch and an oob-mgmt-server at 192.168.200.1 that runs DHCP, DNS and NAT, serves zero-touch provisioning scripts to Cumulus Linux and NVOS switches, and is the SSH entry point. One OOB leaf serves up to 226 nodes, and the OOB network scales to about 23,000. A simulation moves through Inactive, Requesting, Provisioning, Booting and Active, and stopping it saves a checkpoint, up to 3 per simulation. The topology changes only while the simulation is stopped.

Chapter 14 of 18

What a twin can and cannot prove

The line between logical design and physics

The lab's own conclusion draws the line, and the switch documentation explains it. Validated in simulation, months early, is the logical design and its functional behaviour: topology, the cabling plan and addressing, the BGP underlay, ECMP and the EVPN-VXLAN overlay, tenant isolation in the tested scenarios with packet-level evidence, the RoCE and QoS configuration on every hop, failover and recovery, and the automation workflow run end to end from Day 0 to Day 2.

Still needing real Spectrum switches and ConnectX or BlueField cards: PFC pause frames under real load, ECN marking, CNPs and DCQCN rate control, buffer tuning for each ASIC generation, and line-rate NCCL all-reduce performance. Those are exactly the mechanisms of the congestion chapters. The twin can prove that every hop is configured to map DSCP 26 to priority 3. It cannot prove that the hardware classifies by that mark, since a virtual switch does not support packet marking or QoS buffer management, and in the lab DSCP 26 on RDMA data was never seen on the wire. Nor can it prove that a queue on rail leaf 3 really drains in time.

The Cumulus Linux page on virtual environments lists what a virtual switch does not support because it depends on the hardware: access control lists, in-service upgrade, PTP, port security, SPAN and ERSPAN, interface rates and packets per second, packet marking and remarking, QoS buffer management and shaping, the PFC watchdog, What Just Happened, NAT, adaptive routing, storm control, Open Telemetry and ASIC monitoring. Temperatures and sensors are artificial. So a configuration that sets adaptive routing or the watchdog is checked for syntax and intent in the twin, never for effect.

NVIDIA's own post makes the same separation from the other side. Node-based simulation checks configuration and software behaviour, while performance, power, memory and capacity models answer different questions, and their outputs should be kept apart. Aviz says the same in fewer words: everything except raw performance. No twin can show optics, a dirty connector or a GPU that throttles.

Chapter 15 of 18

Ninety-one cases over Day 0, Day 1 and Day 2

Day 0 builds, Day 1 adds tenants, Day 2 breaks things

The lab's test plan has 91 cases in three phases. Day 0 is the build: cabling and LLDP neighbours, the BGP underlay and ECMP, the RoCE and QoS profile on every hop, and the storage EVPN-VXLAN overlay. Day 1 is tenants: onboarding, VRF isolation and customer scenarios. Day 2 is operation: link, leaf and spine failure and recovery, and performance baselines. The phases match NVIDIA's description of a twin used across Day 0 planning, Day 1 deployment and Day 2 operations. Each earlier chapter of this lesson maps onto a phase. Rails, sizing, /31 addressing and the RoCE profile are Day 0. VRFs, VNIs, type-5 routes and the isolation tests are Day 1. Adaptive routing, the watchdog and failover are Day 2.

Many of these checks exist as ready-made tests in NVIDIA NetQ, the fabric validation tool for Cumulus Linux. Its validation reference lists, among others, Topology blueprint and LLDP service, BGP session establishment, address families and interface MTU, EVPN BGP session and VNI consistency, link MTU consistency, interface speed and auto-negotiation, the Cumulus Linux image version, and seven RoCE tests: mode and flow control, classification and ETS, congestion control, miscellaneous, QoS trust mode, version, and ACS P2P mode. They run with netq check, and netq show unit-tests prints their numbers so single tests can be included or skipped. The topology test compares the cabling NetQ learns from LLDP with a blueprint file of the intended design.

That is the bridge from twin to hardware. The topology file that built the simulation describes the intended cabling. On the real floor, LLDP reports the cabling that exists. When the two differ, a cable is in the wrong port, and the test says which, before any GPU job finds it the slow way. The miscabled GPU from the NCCL chapter, which forced rail traffic through a spine without raising an error, is exactly what this test catches.

Chapter 16 of 18

Proving readiness on real hardware with NCCL tests

Bring-up, burn-in, preproduction, production, each with its own bar

Once the servers are racked, the question changes from whether the design is right to whether this hardware performs. NVIDIA's post on cluster readiness puts it bluntly: a cluster can pass standard diagnostics and still fail a real distributed job, so the best test is to run one. It says a 512-GPU training job can underperform or fail because of one slow GPU, one link that degrades under load, or a setting that quietly sends traffic over a slower path, that operators may not notice until hours into a run or a customer ticket, and that teams can then spend days bisecting the cluster while the capacity sits idle. A cluster becomes ready in stages, bring-up, burn-in, preproduction and production, and a node that passes a smoke test is not yet ready to join a 512-GPU job. On Slurm, the batch scheduler common on training clusters, the test is one srun command, srun being Slurm's command for launching a job across many nodes at once. Kubernetes needs GPU and RDMA resource requests, NCCL settings matched to the fabric, a large shared-memory volume and a way to start all Pods together.

NVIDIA Cluster Readiness Engine, NVCRE, is an open source Kubernetes controller for this. You create a Certification that names the nodes and the test categories. It creates one Workflow per category, and each Workflow creates a Job. Results flow back up, and each failure is tied to a node and a reason such as ThresholdViolation, HardwareFailureDetected or WorkloadFailed. The built-in catalogue holds five NCCL variants (all-reduce, all-gather, all-to-all, loopback, and loopback across NVSwitch), the level-4 diagnostic of DCGM, NVIDIA's Data Center GPU Manager, which stress-tests each GPU and its memory, and pretraining runs of Nemotron 5 at 8B and 56B parameters in NeMo, NVIDIA's framework for training large models.

Scale decides what is measured. testScale intra-node tests each node alone, intra-rack groups nodes by the nvidia.com/gpu.clique label, a Kubernetes node label that NVIDIA's GPU software sets to the same value on every node sharing one NVLink domain, full-scale puts every node in one group, and diagnose splits failing groups in half and reruns them until it reaches minGroupSize, with maxConcurrent limiting how many jobs load the fabric at once. An NCCL test inside one server measures NVLink. The same test across servers measures the rails, and only then do ECN, DCQCN and PFC run under real load.

Chapter 17 of 18

Reading the numbers

Bus bandwidth, the pass bar, and the node that failed it

nccl-tests reports two bandwidths. Algorithm bandwidth, algbw, is the message size divided by the time. For collectives that number falls as more ranks join, so the tests also report bus bandwidth, busbw, which scales algbw by a factor for each collective so it can be compared with the hardware's peak at any rank count. For all-reduce the factor is 2(n − 1)/n.

A good result is a busbw close to the bottleneck the test crosses. The nccl-tests performance notes say bus bandwidth should reflect the speed of the hardware bottleneck. Inside one NVLink domain that is NVLink. Across servers it is the rails, and that is the case this fabric is built for. On a 2-8-9-400 H100 server each GPU has one 400 Gb/s SuperNIC, 50 GB/s per direction. If NCCL spreads its rings or channels across all eight cards, as rail-optimised placement intends, each card carries one ring's hop between servers while the rest of the ring moves over NVLink, and the bound for a cross-server busbw is the node's total rail bandwidth, 8 × 50 = 400 GB/s, well above one card's 50 GB/s. That figure is this lesson's illustrative estimate. Neither NCCL nor nccl-tests states it, and protocol overheads keep real results below it, so a healthy cluster's result would be expected to land somewhat under 400 GB/s. A bar of 90 percent, about 360 GB/s, is equally an illustration.

NVIDIA's own worked example is from a different kind of system, and it shows how the two bandwidths relate. A 16-node GB300 cluster, 4 GPUs per node, 64 ranks: 16 GB messages at 473.44 GB/s algbw. The factor is 2 × 63 ÷ 64 = 1.96875, and 473.44 × 1.96875 = 932.09 GB/s busbw, the figure in the report, with 9 samples and MNNVL, multi-node NVLink, enabled. Because those GPUs reach each other over NVLink across nodes, that report measures NVLink and not rails.

NVCRE ships no default thresholds. You write them as CEL expressions, such as busBandwidthGBps value >= 900, which NVIDIA gives as an illustrative bar for GB200 NVL72-class systems, or goodputRatio >= 0.9 and avgTFLOPsPerGPU >= 800 for training. A run that completes but misses its bar gets a ValidationFailed condition and counts as a failure.

Cabling and firmware have their own checks: the NetQ topology and version tests on the switches, and AICR, NVIDIA's open source AI Cluster Runtime, on the nodes, which locks drivers, operators and kernels into version-locked recipes and detects drift from them, as the lesson Dynamo on a validated cluster explains. Burn-in is the long run: DCGM level 4 takes under 2.25 hours on an 8-GPU Hopper node, level 3 under 35 minutes.

Chapter 18 of 18

Changes go through the twin first

A governed loop from proposal to production, and back

The twin keeps its value after the build. NVIDIA describes a closed loop for every later change. A proposed change to infrastructure or software enters CI/CD or change management. An agent configures or selects the matching twin, with topology, tenant policy, workload profile and constraints. It runs the validation tools the workflow allows, such as configuration checks, security and compliance checks and health analysis. It compares the results with policy and approved documentation. It then writes an evidence report and either recommends promotion, opens a remediation task or routes the case to a human. NVIDIA stresses that this is governed automation: agents experiment freely in the twin, and human gates and policy decide when anything touches production. Its examples of agent roles are a design agent, a validation agent that checks tenant isolation and resource limits, an operations agent that prepares tickets, and an improvement agent that grows the test coverage.

For this fabric, adding a tenant C is that kind of change. In the twin it is pushed, the Day 1 tests run again, the ping paths of tenants A and B must lose nothing, and the spines must stay untouched. Air's checkpoints let a failed attempt be thrown away. Only then does the change go to the real switches.

Production then needs both kinds of watching. NVSentinel, NVIDIA's open source fault monitor for GPU nodes, listens passively to DCGM metrics, Xid errors (the error codes the GPU driver logs), logs and maintenance events, and can cordon and drain a faulty node. NVCRE generates load to find failures that produce no telemetry, and its failed certifications can become NVSentinel health events. NVIDIA advises tracking the gap between simulated and observed behaviour, which is the measure of how far the twin can be trusted.

What to keep

Logic in the twin, physics on the floor

A rail-optimised fabric puts GPU n of every server on rail n, so that GPU 3 reaches GPU 3 of another server through one leaf, and NCCL's PXN and its same-NIC preference keep most traffic there. NVIDIA's reference sizes it in four-server units with two rails per leaf: 4 leafs and 2 spines for 32 servers, 8 and 4 for 64, 16 and 8 for 128. Every GPU port is a routed /31. RoCE stays as close to lossless as engineering allows by marking data DSCP 26 into priority 3, sending CNPs on DSCP 48 ahead of everything, letting ECN and DCQCN slow senders first, and keeping PFC with its xoff, xon, headroom and watchdog as the last resort. Beside it, the north-south converged fabric carries storage, management and users through each server's DPU, and EVPN-VXLAN makes it tenant-aware with a VRF and L3 VNI per tenant, type-5 routes and spines that never change. Vishwas Tiwari's lab built all of this in simulation with two servers, proved isolation layer by layer across 91 cases, and said plainly what a twin cannot prove: pauses, ECN and DCQCN under load, buffers per ASIC and line-rate NCCL. Those belong to the hardware, measured as busbw against the bottleneck it crosses and a bar you set. The cluster can then serve what it was bought for, such as Qwen3-32B in its 34.3 GB FP8 build under vLLM, on GPUs that were proven before the first request.
