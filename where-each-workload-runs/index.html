Running a GPU cloud

Private, public, on-prem or hybrid: where each workload runs

Four words that answer different questions, and a placement decision made one workload at a time

Teams planning AI infrastructure often start with a choice between two camps: build it in house or rent it from a cloud. The words used for that choice mix up three separate questions. Private and public say who the infrastructure is for. On premises says where it stands. Hybrid says that more than one environment is in use and they are joined. A private cloud can stand in someone else's building. A public cloud can hand one customer a whole GPU server nobody else touches. A rack of GPU servers in the company's own hall can be on premises and still not be a cloud.

This lesson takes the four words apart with NIST's definitions, then compares private and public AI cloud on six dimensions: exclusivity, control, scalability, managed services, operational responsibility and cost structure. It shows how the shared responsibility line moves between provider and customer without disappearing, works out what a useful GPU hour costs at different utilization, and measures data gravity in hours of transfer. It then looks at on-premises AI: what it covers, the five reasons enterprises choose it, what it does not deliver by itself, and the operating duties that come with it. The last chapters place Northlight's five AI workloads one by one, follow a hybrid request through an AI gateway that sends some prompts to a hosted model API and others to the company's own vLLM Pods, and show that management and control planes have a placement of their own.

Chapter 1 of 16

Four deployment models, and a word for a place

NIST defines who a cloud is for, and leaves the building open

NIST Special Publication 800-145, The NIST Definition of Cloud Computing, was published in September 2011 and is still the definition most others point back to. It names four deployment models. A private cloud is "provisioned for exclusive use by a single organization comprising multiple consumers (e.g., business units)". It "may be owned, managed, and operated by the organization, a third party, or some combination of them, and it may exist on or off premises." A community cloud is the same idea for several organizations that share concerns such as a mission, security requirements or compliance. A public cloud is "provisioned for open use by the general public" and "exists on the premises of the cloud provider." A hybrid cloud is "a composition of two or more distinct cloud infrastructures (private, community, or public) that remain unique entities", bound together by technology that lets data and applications move between them.

Each definition says who the infrastructure is for. Only the public one fixes a location, and that location is the provider's. On premises is a different kind of word. It names a place: a site the organization itself controls. The words therefore combine in every direction. A private cloud can run in the organization's own data center, in a colocation hall, or at a managed provider that keeps dedicated racks for one customer. Manually run GPU servers in the company's own hall are on premises and are not a private cloud, because nothing about them is delivered as a cloud.

In this lesson the scene stands for Northlight's own private AI cloud. Northlight is one company. The customer at the bottom of the scene is one of its business units, and every part of the control plane above serves Northlight alone. The lesson Who controls the cloud quotes the private cloud definition while it weighs sovereignty. This lesson uses the same words for a narrower job: deciding where each AI workload should run.

Chapter 2 of 16

A GPU server is not a private cloud

Five characteristics turn hardware into a cloud, and four layers make it an AI cloud

NIST lists five essential characteristics that a cloud has whatever its deployment model. On-demand self-service: a consumer "can unilaterally provision computing capabilities, such as server time and network storage, as needed automatically without requiring human interaction with each service provider." Broad network access: capabilities are reached over the network through standard mechanisms. Resource pooling: the provider's resources "are pooled to serve multiple consumers using a multi-tenant model", assigned and reassigned according to demand. Rapid elasticity: capabilities can be provisioned and released, sometimes automatically, to scale with demand. Measured service: the cloud meters use so that it "can be monitored, controlled, and reported".

Each one maps to a part of the scene. The portal and its API are self-service. The fabric gives network access. The three servers with four H100 each form the pool. The scheduler and the provisioner give and take back capacity. Metering records who held which GPU and for how long. A server bought, racked and handed to one team by a ticket and an email has none of these. It is on-premises AI hardware.

A private AI cloud adds an AI stack to that operating model. A useful shorthand is: Private AI Cloud = Private Cloud Operating Model + AI-Optimized Infrastructure + AI Platform + Governance. Drawn as layers, the bottom is a dedicated foundation with exclusive use, self-service, resource pooling, measured usage and policy control. Above it sits AI infrastructure: GPUs or other accelerators, compute, the high-speed network that distributed training needs, and storage that feeds datasets, models, checkpoints, vectors and logs. Above that sits the AI platform and governance: data, models, orchestration such as GPU scheduling and quotas, security, observability and governance. On top run the enterprise workloads: training, fine-tuning, RAG, inference and agents. Without the lower layers, Qwen3-32B served by vLLM on a GPU is a model server. It becomes part of a cloud when anyone in the company can get one through the same governed, metered path.

Chapter 3 of 16

One request through a private AI cloud, in six steps

Request, provision, connect data, run, govern, release

A private AI cloud aims to keep the cloud experience while one organization keeps the control. Its workflow has six steps. A user requests resources: GPUs, VMs, containers, storage or a ready AI environment. The platform provisions the environment after it checks policy and capacity. Data and AI workloads are connected: datasets, models, vector stores and applications. The workload runs, as training, fine-tuning or inference. The organization monitors and governs it: resource use, access, models, data, security events and policies. Finally the resources are released or reallocated, back into the private pool for the next request.

Follow one request. The research unit wants to evaluate Qwen3-32B against an internal test set before the model goes to production. It asks for one H100 for eight hours. Identity confirms the user belongs to the unit, and the scheduler checks the unit's quota: four GPUs, of which none are held. It picks a free GPU on gpu-srv-3 and the provisioner prepares it. The environment then needs the model. The FP8 build, Qwen3-32B-FP8, is 34.3 GB of files in storage. Over the fabric they reach the server, and vLLM takes about three minutes to load them and start serving. With 80 GB of HBM, the weights leave roughly 45.7 GB for the KV cache and the runtime. The test set stays in storage inside the boundary. While the evaluation runs, metering records the hold and the audit trail records who started it. After eight hours the unit releases the GPU, the provisioner wipes what the job left behind, and the GPU returns to the pool as free.

The last step is where an owned cluster most often falls short. Without release, a GPU stays with whoever asked first, and the pool shrinks with every finished project. The lesson Why a job waits while GPUs are free shows what quotas and queues do when the pool is short.

Chapter 4 of 16

A public cloud can hand you a whole server

A public provider can still give one customer dedicated hardware

A common shortcut says private means on premises and public means shared GPUs. The first half fell in the opening chapter. The second half fails too. NIST's resource pooling describes a provider's resources serving many consumers through a multi-tenant model. That is true of the provider as a whole. Individual products still give one customer dedicated physical hardware: dedicated GPU instances, bare metal GPU servers, dedicated hosts and reserved clusters. Whether a single GPU device is shared does not decide the deployment model.

Two real products show the range. On AWS, Capacity Blocks for ML let a customer "reserve GPU-based accelerated computing instances on a future date". Durations are set in 1-day increments up to 14 days, and in 7-day increments up to 182 days. One block can hold up to 64 instances, and an organization can hold up to 256 instances across blocks on a given date. A block can start up to eight weeks ahead, cannot be cancelled, and ends at 11:30 UTC on its last day, with termination of its instances starting at 11:00 UTC. Instances in a block are placed close together inside EC2 UltraClusters for low-latency networking. A p5.48xlarge in such a block has 8 H100 GPUs, 192 vCPUs and 2 TiB of memory.

On Oracle Cloud Infrastructure the shape BM.GPU.H100.8 is a bare metal server: 8 H100 with 80 GB each, 112 OCPUs, 2,048 GB of memory, 16 local NVMe drives of 3.84 TB and 8 × 2 × 200 Gbps of RDMA networking. Bare metal means no hypervisor of the provider between the customer and the hardware. Nobody else's code runs on that machine while it is rented.

The environment around those servers is still the provider's. Its control plane, its identity service, its network beyond the server and its staff serve every customer. That is where public differs from private. The lesson A tenant is a boundary shows that a dedicated server still needs the other kinds of separation.

Chapter 5 of 16

Six dimensions, side by side

Neither model wins on all six, so the workload decides

Private and public AI cloud can be compared on six dimensions.

Exclusivity. A private AI cloud serves one organization. A public AI cloud is offered by a provider to the broader market, even when a product gives one customer dedicated hardware.

Control. Private gives the organization more direct say over hardware configuration, network architecture, security policy, identity and access management (IAM), data movement, model access, the software stack, the upgrade policy and the maintenance windows. In public cloud the provider standardizes and runs more of those, and the customer accepts its choices. A private cloud can hold a CUDA driver version for a year because a model server depends on it. A public provider picks its own schedule.

Scalability. Public cloud draws on the provider's larger pool, so GPUs, regions, storage and services can be added without buying hardware first. GPU capacity there is still limited by model, region, reservation and the provider's availability. A private cloud runs inside owned or reserved capacity: a clear ceiling, and full control of planning under it. Northlight's twelve GPUs stay twelve until more are bought, delivered and racked.

Managed services. Public platforms offer databases, object storage, managed Kubernetes, data pipelines, model APIs, machine learning platforms, monitoring and security services. In a private cloud the organization deploys or selects more of these itself. Part of what a public cloud sells is this breadth, beyond the GPUs.

Operational responsibility. In a private cloud the organization or its managed provider carries hardware lifecycle, capacity planning, cluster operations, upgrades, patching, monitoring and incident response. In public cloud the provider carries the physical infrastructure and much of the platform. The next chapter shows what stays with the customer.

Cost structure. Private means hardware or dedicated capacity plus operations. Public is usually on-demand, reserved or consumption based. Which is cheaper depends on how long and how fully the capacity is used, which the chapter after next works out.

Chapter 6 of 16

The responsibility line moves, and the customer keeps a share

Public cloud takes the physical layer, never your data, identities or configuration

AWS states its shared responsibility model in two phrases. AWS is responsible for "Security of the Cloud": the hardware, software, networking and facilities that run its services. The customer is responsible for "Security in the Cloud", and how much that covers depends on the service. EC2 is infrastructure as a service, so the customer manages the guest operating system with its updates and security patches, any software installed on the instance, and the security group, the firewall AWS provides per instance. For abstracted services such as S3, AWS runs the infrastructure, the operating system and the platform, and the customer still manages its data, including encryption options, classifies its assets and sets permissions with IAM. Some controls are shared in separate contexts. AWS patches its infrastructure and the customer patches its guest OS and applications. AWS configures its devices and the customer configures its own systems.

Microsoft lists what a customer keeps "regardless of the type of deployment": data, including classification, protection, encryption decisions and governance compliance. Endpoints that reach the cloud. User accounts. Access management, including role-based access control and multifactor authentication. Its AI shared responsibility model splits an AI application into three layers. The AI platform runs the model and holds its training data and weights. The AI application turns a prompt into a call to the model. AI usage is how people consume it. With a SaaS product the provider runs more layers. With PaaS or IaaS the customer runs more. Microsoft recommends starting with SaaS and reserving custom model building for organizations with deep expertise in data science and in AI security, privacy and ethics.

For AI the customer's share also includes model governance: which models are allowed, which prompts and outputs are logged, and what agents may do. In a private cloud the organization adds the rest: hardware lifecycle, capacity planning, cluster operations, upgrades, patching, monitoring and incident response. Security depends on architecture, configuration, IAM, monitoring and discipline on both sides. A private cloud with a flat network and shared admin passwords is less safe than a well-run public account. A public account with a public storage bucket is less safe than either.

Chapter 7 of 16

What a useful GPU hour costs

Divide the hourly cost by the share of hours that do work

Comparing an hourly rental price with the purchase price of a server answers nothing. The comparison that works puts both on the same unit: the cost of one GPU hour that did useful work. For owned capacity the cost runs every hour of the year whether the GPU works or not. For rented capacity the customer pays only for the hours it holds.

Take two illustrative numbers. An owned H100 costs Northlight $1.80 for every hour of its life once the server, its share of the network and storage, power, cooling, space, software and staff are spread across its years of service. The same GPU rented on demand costs $3.00 per hour. Neither figure is a market quote. The arithmetic is what matters.

The production inference service holds two GPUs every hour of the year: 2 × 8,760 = 17,520 GPU-hours. Owned, that is 17,520 × $1.80 = $31,536. Rented on demand for the same hours, 17,520 × $3.00 = $52,560. The fine-tuning job needs 8 GPUs for 3 days once a quarter: 8 × 72 × 4 = 2,304 GPU-hours a year. Rented, 2,304 × $3.00 = $6,912. Owning 8 GPUs for that job alone costs 8 × 8,760 × $1.80 = $126,144 a year, because the GPUs cost money in the other 97 percent of hours too. Their utilization is 2,304 ÷ 70,080 = 3.3 percent, so each useful hour costs $126,144 ÷ 2,304 = $54.75.

The break-even point is the share of the year at which owning and renting cost the same: $1.80 ÷ $3.00 = 60 percent, or 5,256 of 8,760 hours. Above it, owned capacity costs less per useful hour. Below it, renting does. Utilization inside the held hours matters too: an owned GPU that is busy 50 percent of the time costs $3.60 per useful hour.

A full comparison adds what the hourly rate hides. Public cloud adds data transfer, storage, managed services and premium support. Private adds the purchase, the data center, networking, software, operations staff, and replacement when the next GPU generation arrives. The fuller formula is workload duration, utilization, operations, data movement, software, support and lifecycle.

Chapter 8 of 16

Data gravity: carry the model to the data

A model is tens of gigabytes, a company's documents can be hundreds of terabytes

Data gravity is the pull a large dataset has on the work that uses it. Moving work to the data is cheap. Moving the data to the work is slow, can cost link charges on the way in and egress fees on the way back out, and makes another copy that has to be governed.

Northlight's legal and engineering documents fill an illustrative 200 TB store in its own data center. Copying them to a public region over a dedicated 10 Gb/s link takes 200 × 10¹² bytes × 8 bits ÷ 10 × 10⁹ bits per second = 160,000 seconds, about 44 hours at full line rate with nothing else on the link. Real copies run slower, and every day's changes have to follow. The model goes the other way in seconds. The FP8 build of Qwen3-32B is 34.3 GB, which crosses the same link in 34.3 × 10⁹ × 8 ÷ 10¹⁰ = 27.4 seconds. The BF16 build, 65.5 GB, takes 52.4 seconds.

That asymmetry is the first benefit a private AI cloud claims: AI runs next to large enterprise datasets instead of moving them out. The others follow from it. Data and model control: the organization decides where sensitive data and models are processed. Predictable capacity: dedicated GPUs make planning for critical workloads direct. Customization: infrastructure, networking, security and software fit the enterprise's needs. Governance: identity, policy, audit, model access and data controls connect to the enterprise's own systems.

The workloads that typically land in a private AI cloud share these traits: enterprise RAG, internal copilots, AI over sensitive documents, financial and healthcare AI, manufacturing AI, fine-tuning of proprietary models, production inference, agentic AI, computer vision, data-intensive AI and regulated workloads. Sensitive data alone does not decide the placement. Data classification, workload pattern, cost, latency, compliance and the team's operating ability are weighed together.

Chapter 9 of 16

On premises: from one server to a closed site

A place under the organization's control, and five reasons to choose it

On-prem AI means running AI workloads on infrastructure the organization controls in its own on-premises environment. Compute, networking, storage, data, models and the AI software stack all sit under its direct control. The term covers a wide range: a single GPU server, a multi-node GPU cluster, a Kubernetes AI platform, an AI factory, an on-premises private AI cloud, or a restricted or air-gapped site. The cluster northlight-eu in this chapter's scene is one point on that range: two GPU nodes with four H100 each, the Deployment qwen3 with its Pods qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-x7k2p on gpu-node-1 and qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-m9q4t on gpu-node-2, and an image registry inside the site.

Enterprises give five reasons. Data and model control: an AI system has more sensitive assets than its training data. Model weights, prompts, embeddings, vector data, inference logs and the actions agents take all count, and keeping them inside the organization's boundary can be a hard requirement. Data locality: large datasets already live in the company's own data centers. Latency and local processing: industrial vision, local search and RAG, real-time control, internal interactive AI and inference next to edge devices all need to answer quickly to systems on the same site, without depending on a wide-area path. Restricted or regulated environments: limited internet access, data residency rules, internal security policy, regulated processing and segmented networks. Hardware and stack control: the choice of GPU model, CPU and memory, network, storage, drivers, runtimes, Kubernetes, AI frameworks and model-serving stack. Beyond those five, the article's own figure adds economics: stable, long-running workloads that keep owned capacity busy.

On-prem and private AI cloud answer different questions. On-prem answers where. Private cloud answers for whom and how it is run. northlight-eu becomes part of a private AI cloud only when its GPUs are offered through self-service, pooling and metering, as the second chapter set out.

Chapter 10 of 16

What on premises does not buy by itself

Speed, savings and safety come from the whole system around the servers

Three beliefs travel with on-prem AI, and none holds automatically.

Faster. Local hardware shortens the network path and can be tuned for one workload. Actual performance depends on the whole system: the GPU, the CPU and memory, the network, the storage, the data pipeline, the model's architecture and the software's optimization. A cluster running last generation's GPUs with a slow storage path can lose to a public region that already offers the next generation. Public providers often get new accelerators and managed services into customers' hands sooner than an enterprise can buy, rack and qualify them.

Cheaper. On-prem costs include GPU and server purchase, the data center, power and cooling, networking, storage, software, operations staff, hardware lifecycle, and replacement and upgrades. The economics chapter showed that owned capacity wins only above a utilization threshold. A cluster bought for a peak and idle the rest of the time costs more per useful hour than renting.

More secure. Security depends on identity, network policy, patching, monitoring, governance and operations. A cluster in the company's hall with an unpatched kernel and a Pod that may reach anything is exposed from the inside, and attackers reach on-prem networks through phishing and stolen credentials as well. The scene's policy engine and Falco are there because location alone protects nothing.

On-prem also does not mean disconnected. Whether the site reaches the internet is a separate design decision. In northlight-eu the nodes pull images from registry.northlight.example inside the site, and that registry may still fetch from public sources. A site with no automated connection at all is an air-gapped site, a narrower case with its own supply chain, which the lesson A cloud with no way out covers step by step.

Chapter 11 of 16

More control, more duties: a driver upgrade on gpu-node-1

On-prem AI is an infrastructure, platform, operations and governance program

A production on-prem AI environment has four layers, and the organization runs all of them. Infrastructure: GPUs and CPUs, network, storage and the data center. Platform: Kubernetes or containers, AI frameworks, training and fine-tuning, model serving, data and RAG, agent platforms. Operations: provisioning, scheduling, access and quotas, observability, capacity management and lifecycle. Governance: identity, security policy, model and data access, audit and compliance. That is why on-prem AI is better planned as a program than bought as hardware.

The duties that come with it are concrete: hardware failure, drivers and firmware, Kubernetes, GPU scheduling, provisioning, capacity planning, observability, security patching, model serving, and backup and recovery. In a public cloud the provider does some of these. On premises every one lands on a Northlight engineer.

Take one of them. A new GPU driver fixes a bug, and gpu-node-1 has to get it. The node runs Pod qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-x7k2p. An engineer runs kubectl drain --ignore-daemonsets gpu-node-1. The node is marked unschedulable, with the taint node.kubernetes.io/unschedulable, and its Pods are evicted through the Eviction API. Kubernetes' documentation says a drain lets containers terminate gracefully and respects any PodDisruptionBudget. With a budget of minAvailable: 1 for the qwen3 Pods, the eviction goes ahead, because qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-m9q4t on gpu-node-2 still serves. The ReplicaSet behind the Deployment creates a replacement Pod with the same template hash and new random characters, scheduled to gpu-node-2, which has three free GPUs. vLLM takes about three minutes to load 34.3 GB of weights before the new Pod is ready, so for those minutes one Pod carries all the traffic. After the driver is installed and the node checked, kubectl uncordon gpu-node-1 puts it back into scheduling.

The drain is ten seconds of typing. Knowing the budget, the spare capacity and the three minutes of reduced service is the operating ability that on-prem requires. More control means more responsibility.

Chapter 12 of 16

When private fits, and when public fits

Workload first, then data, control and service model, then hardware

A private AI cloud tends to fit when the requirements include sensitive or regulated data, restricted networks, air-gapped or separated environments, control over how data and models move, custom hardware or software architecture, dedicated capacity, stable and highly utilized AI workloads, internal AI platforms shared across the company, and governance and audit requirements.

A public AI cloud tends to fit when teams need to start quickly, demand changes a lot, several GPU types need to be tried, managed AI and data services carry real value, global regions are needed, infrastructure operations should stay minimal, or the work is short-lived or experimental.

Five dimensions turn those lists into a decision for one workload. Data and governance: how much control is required over data, models and the boundaries they may cross. Capacity and scalability: how many GPUs, and how variable the demand. Managed services: how much provider-run AI and data functionality the workload needs. Operations: whether the organization can run the infrastructure and the platform itself. Economics: whether the work is temporary, bursty or steady, and what utilization to expect.

The order of questions matters. A planning sequence that holds up is: workload, data, control, service model, and only then compute, network, storage, platform, operations, governance and economics. The GPU choice belongs inside that architecture. Starting from a GPU model reverses it. Training, fine-tuning, real-time inference, RAG and agents need very different compute, network, storage and governance, and a gap in the one area a workload depends on can undo the work done well everywhere else.

Chapter 13 of 16

Five workloads, five placements

Hybrid means each workload gets its own answer

NIST's hybrid cloud keeps each environment a distinct entity and joins them with technology that lets data and applications move. Its own example is cloud bursting: sending load to another cloud when one runs out. For AI the useful form of hybrid is placement by workload. The question stops being private or public and becomes which workload runs under which control, cost and operating model.

Northlight has five AI workloads. Production inference serves Qwen3-32B-FP8 from two Pods on two GPUs every hour of the year, and its prompts quote internal documents. Steady use and sensitive prompts both point to private, and the economics chapter put it at $31,536 a year owned against $52,560 rented. Enterprise RAG searches the 200 TB document store, so data gravity keeps it private and next to the data.

Fine-tuning runs four times a year for three days. Full fine-tuning of Qwen3-32B holds 16 bytes per weight, 524 GB of state, sharded over 8 GPUs at 65.5 GB each plus activations, as the lesson From base model to your model works out. Northlight's private pool has twelve GPUs, four already held, and keeping eight more idle most of the year would cost $126,144. A 3-day Capacity Block of one p5.48xlarge gives eight H100 for $1,728 per run at the illustrative price, $6,912 a year for four runs. The training conversations are an estimated 8.1 million tokens, tens of megabytes, and have been cleared to leave the site. The resulting BF16 model, 65.5 GB, comes back in under a minute on a 10 Gb/s link. If the conversations had not been cleared to leave, the job would stay private and wait for capacity.

Evaluating a newer GPU generation before buying it goes public as well: renting a BM.GPU.B200.8 for a week answers the question without a purchase. The fifth workload, an assistant for every employee, uses a hosted frontier model through its API, while private data is kept back at the gateway. That one is hybrid inside a single request path, and the next chapter follows it.

Chapter 14 of 16

Hybrid inside one request path

One gateway sends each prompt to a hosted model or to the company's own GPUs

The owner's distributed inference deck draws AI delivery as three layers. Layer 1 is an API gateway that exposes and protects APIs and AI endpoints. Layer 2 is an AI gateway for model abstraction and selection, budgets and AI policies. Layer 3 is an inference gateway for model-aware routing, queue management and serving optimization. Three deployment patterns follow. SaaS first uses layers 1 and 2: governed access to commercial LLM APIs with little infrastructure. Self-hosted uses layers 2 and 3: models run in private infrastructure or a controlled cloud, with an inference gateway in front of the GPUs. Hybrid uses all three: commercial LLM APIs and privately hosted models behind one AI gateway that routes each workload by cost, latency, capability, privacy and availability. The deck's own caption is that the boundaries overlap, so the choice follows where control is needed.

Northlight's employee assistant is the hybrid case on the factory scene. The app calls one endpoint on the BNK data plane at 203.0.113.80. BNK hands the prompt to F5 AI Guardrails, which returns one of three verdicts: Pass sends it on unchanged, Fail drops it with HTTP 403 before any GPU or provider sees it, and Obfuscation masks personal data or secrets in place. F5 AI Gateway then applies the group's policy: an allowed list of providers and models, plus a block list that always wins. A general writing request goes to the hosted model API. A request that names a contract goes to the self-hosted model, where the endpoint picker chooses among Northlight's vLLM workers. The guardrail check runs the same way for a hosted API and for self-hosted vLLM.

Two things make this hybrid in the NIST sense. The hosted service and the private factory stay separate environments with separate operators. The gateway is the technology that binds them, so an application does not need to know where its answer came from. The lessons Three gateways, three jobs and Inside the LLM gateway look at the gateways themselves.

Chapter 15 of 16

The management plane has a placement too

Data plane here, control plane there, or both on site

Placement is decided for the tools that govern AI as well as for the models. A product that inspects prompts has a data plane, where the traffic passes, and a control and management plane, where policy is written and logs and health arrive. The two can stand in different places.

The owner's F5 decks show the forms this takes. The F5 AI Security Platform is deployed as SaaS, self-hosted or air-gapped. Its gateways sit in any region, each SaaS or self-hosted, while one management and control plane configures them all: configuration goes down, logs and health come up. Workforce AI Security, the forward proxy for employees' AI use that the lesson What employees send to AI follows, comes in three forms: SaaS, a hybrid with the data plane on premises and the control and management plane in SaaS, and a fully on-premises install with data, control and management planes on site for regulated customers. F5 Insight, the operations platform, comes as on-prem air-gapped for regulated and disconnected sites with no outbound connection, on-prem and SaaS connected for local control with SaaS intelligence, and SaaS hosted for central management across many sites. The BNK data plane itself runs on a host CPU, on an RTX PRO 6000 server, on a ConnectX-7 or ConnectX-8 SmartNIC, or on a BlueField-3 or BlueField-4 DPU. The deck marks the host form for flexible on-prem and hybrid deployments, and the DPU form for large multi-tenant AI factories.

The split matters because each plane carries different data. The data plane sees every prompt and response. The management plane sees policy, metadata, health and, unless it is configured otherwise, logs that can quote prompts. A company whose rule is that prompts never leave the site can still accept a SaaS management plane if the logs it receives are limited to metadata, or it can choose the on-prem form. Who controls the cloud asks the same question of a provider's management plane, and the answer comes from the same split.

Chapter 16 of 16

An internal cloud and a cloud business are different builds

Serving your own teams and selling GPUs to customers need different layers

A private AI cloud serves the organization that owns it. Its users are employees and its tenants are business units. Identity comes from the company directory. Usage records feed capacity plans and show-back reports that tell each unit what it consumed. Governance follows the company's own policies.

A customer-facing GPU cloud, the kind often called a neocloud, starts from the same hardware and adds a commercial layer. It needs products in a catalogue with prices, tenancy strong enough to separate customers who do not trust each other, self-service ordering for outsiders, metering that holds up as the basis of an invoice, billing, service level agreements, and a lifecycle that returns every server clean to the pool. Good infrastructure does not become a cloud business by itself, and the build an enterprise needs for its private AI cloud is not the build a provider needs to sell capacity.

That difference changes which tools fit. An enterprise building a private AI cloud wants control over infrastructure, data, models and policy, with a cloud operating experience for its own teams. An infrastructure owner building a GPU cloud wants productization, tenancy, provisioning, metering and billing for outside customers. A platform designed for one is not automatically the answer for the other, and the choice starts from which of the two the organization is building.

On this scene both readings are possible. Read as Northlight's private cloud, the customer is a business unit and billing is a cost report. Read as a provider, the customer is another company and billing is an invoice with money attached. The lessons Metering is not billing, From an order to a server and back and A tenant is a boundary cover the layers the second reading adds.

What to keep

Ask which workload, then ask where

Private and public say who the infrastructure is for. On premises says where it stands. Hybrid says several environments are joined and used together. A private cloud can be hosted elsewhere, a public cloud can hand one customer a whole bare metal server, and a rack of GPUs on site becomes a cloud only with self-service, pooling, elasticity, metering and governance on top. The six dimensions show why neither model wins everywhere: private gives control and a hard ceiling, public gives reach and managed services. Responsibility moves with the model and never leaves the customer's data, identities and configuration. Cost per useful GPU hour turns on utilization, with a break-even near 60 percent in the illustrative numbers, and data gravity makes moving a 34.3 GB model cheaper than moving 200 TB of documents. On-prem adds control and the duties that come with it, down to draining a node for a driver. So Northlight places each workload on its own: steady inference and RAG in its private cloud, a cleared fine-tuning burst and hardware trials in public capacity, and an employee assistant that uses both behind one gateway with one policy.
