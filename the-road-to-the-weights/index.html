BNK and gateways

The road to the weights

Object storage behind BIG-IP: where a model comes from, and what a single POST can destroy

Before a GPU can answer anything it needs its model, and the model is a file that lives somewhere else. In an AI factory that somewhere is object storage spoken to over the S3 API. The same storage takes in raw data, feeds training jobs, keeps the models they produce, hands models to the workers that serve them and holds the documents that retrieval looks things up in. All of it is HTTP. This lesson follows a worker fetching the 34.3 GB of weights of Qwen3-32B in its FP8 form through BIG-IP, shows why the encryption is done in front of the storage and not on it, and then looks at a request that reads like any other and deletes a thousand objects.

Chapter 1 of 12

A model is a file somewhere else

A worker starts empty

A new model server starts on gpu-2. Its GPU memory is empty. It serves Qwen3-32B in its FP8 form, where each of the 32.8 billion parameters takes about one byte. The weights it needs are 34.3 GB of files, a little more than one byte a weight, because some layers stay at higher precision. They come in seven files of about 4.9 GB each, and they are not in the container image. They sit as objects in a bucket called models in the factory's own object storage, where the training job that made them, or whoever downloaded them, put them. A tenant that keeps its weights in a store of its own, outside the factory, fetches them through the factory's egress instead, and the lesson on what leaves the factory follows that path. An object is a named run of bytes with a little metadata. A bucket is a flat container of objects, and a key such as qwen3-32b-fp8/config.json is only a name that happens to contain a slash.

The worker does not talk to a storage node directly. There are 40 of those in this factory, and they come and go for repairs and upgrades. It talks to one name, s3.northlight.internal, which resolves to one address held by BIG-IP. On BIG-IP that address is a virtual server: a listener on port 443 with a TLS profile, a set of rules and a pool of storage nodes behind it. BIG-IP decides which node serves each request.

To the worker the whole storage system is one HTTPS address. Everything later in this lesson depends on that. Every request for every bucket passes one place, so that place can end the encryption, spread the load, work out what each request really does and refuse the ones that should not happen.

Chapter 2 of 12

Three layers, and this road is the first

Data, inference, and governance of who may use them

F5 draws the AI factory as three layers stacked on each other, and calls them pillars. The bottom one is data delivery: the training corpus, the trained models and the knowledge documents, held in object storage and spoken to over S3. Above it is inference, where models become a service that answers requests: model servers on GPUs and the traffic management that picks a GPU for each request. On top is AI and token governance, which controls how those services are consumed. The three goals F5 puts under them are trusted data (secure access, data quality), optimised models (the right model, performance) and governed AI (policy, traceability, performance).

One sentence on F5's slide splits the work. Pillar 1 builds the prompt. Pillar 2 sends it to the GPU that can answer soonest. Pillar 3 decides who is authorised to call it. F5's worked example is a travel planner: a family of five asks for a ten-day trip to Japan. An orchestrator and five agents (travel, flight, hotel, activity, weather) work on it. Pillar 1 grounds every agent call in destination documents retrieved from storage. Pillar 2 is BNK with the endpoint picker, which places each model call on a GPU by load and by the prefix it already holds. Pillar 3 is the AI gateway, which decides who may call which model and with what, masks personal data in prompts, defends against injected instructions, mediates tool calls and counts tokens.

In this factory the three layers are three places on the scene. A request to api.northlight.example at 203.0.113.80 meets governance at the AI gateway first. The endpoint picker then chooses a worker. And that worker can only answer once it has fetched its 34.3 GB of FP8 weights from object storage through BIG-IP, which is what gpu-2 is about to do in the chapters that follow. This lesson is about that bottom layer. The lesson which GPU answers soonest covers pillar 2, and three gateways, three jobs covers pillar 3.

Each layer also has an owner. F5's takeaway for the data layer is to engage the storage engineering team, the people who run the object store and its 40 nodes. For inference it names the AI infrastructure and platform operations teams. The storage team's question is whether a device in the data path costs them throughput or safety, and F5's answer on its takeaway slide is that BIG-IP in the data path does no harm and makes it more resilient, faster and more secure. Those are F5's words. F5's own test chart, read in a later chapter, backs the first of them: with BIG-IP in the path the throughput stays within about 6 percent of the storage alone, in most runs a little below it. The chart does not show the storage getting faster. The chapters that follow show the other two claims, resilience and security, at work.

Chapter 3 of 12

Five kinds of traffic, one protocol

Model retrieval is only one of the flows on this address

The worker's download is one of five flows that cross the same address, and they do not want the same things. Ingestion is raw data arriving from outside: large uploads, at a rate that has to be limited so one source cannot fill the pipe. Training data retrieval is jobs that list a bucket and then download from it, with very high throughput. Model storage is a training run uploading what it produced. Model retrieval is the reverse, a serving worker downloading it. Knowledge processing is a job that lists and downloads documents so they can be cut into pieces and indexed for retrieval.

All five are S3 requests and all five want TLS ended in front of the storage. Ingestion and training data retrieval also want rate limiting and the most raw performance. A sixth flow sits beside them and is a different animal. When a model answers a question with retrieval, the lookup goes to a vector database over a REST or gRPC API. That is an application API, and it is protected like one, with TLS and a web application and API firewall.

So one address carries uploads of many gigabytes, listings of millions of keys, parallel downloads and small metadata calls. A device in front of it cannot treat a request as just a request. It needs to know which of these jobs is talking.

Chapter 4 of 12

Encryption ends in front of the storage

A storage node's CPU has better things to do

Every one of these connections is encrypted, and somebody has to do that arithmetic. If each storage node does its own, every handshake and every encrypted gigabyte comes out of the CPU that the node needs for its real work. That work is computing checksums, spreading objects across disks so that a lost disk loses nothing, and checking old data for decay. MinIO, for example, cuts every object into data and parity pieces with Reed-Solomon coding. On a set of 16 drives with the default parity of 4, an object becomes 12 data pieces and 4 parity pieces, and any 12 of the 16 rebuild it.

So the TLS session ends at BIG-IP and the storage nodes get their CPU back. There is also one place to hold certificates and decide which ciphers are allowed, where otherwise there would be 40.

That matters more now than it used to. Traffic recorded today can be decrypted later by a quantum computer, and one common estimate puts the chance of such a machine existing by 2035 at about half. So the key exchange is moving to hybrid methods such as X25519MLKEM768, which combine a classical exchange with a post-quantum one. The client's key share grows from 32 bytes to 1,216 and the server's from 32 to 1,120. Changing that in one place is a different job from changing it on every node.

Chapter 5 of 12

Who is asking: a signature in every request

S3 has no login. Each request proves itself

S3 has no session and no login step. Every request carries an Authorization header made with Signature Version 4. The client hashes the method, the path, the query string, a chosen list of headers and a hash of the body, and signs that with a key derived from its secret key. The header names the algorithm AWS4-HMAC-SHA256, the access key ID with the date, region and service, the list of signed headers and the signature. The storage node looks up the secret for that access key ID, repeats the calculation and compares.

Two consequences matter for a device in the path. The host header is always signed, and so is the query string. BIG-IP may read everything, since it has ended TLS, but if it rewrites the host or the path the storage computes a different signature and answers 403 SignatureDoesNotMatch. And the access key ID is in clear text in the header, so BIG-IP can tell who claims to be asking without holding any secret.

Long-lived access keys are one of four ways to get credentials. An identity can be federated from SAML or OIDC. A security token service can hand out keys that expire. And a presigned URL carries the signature in the query string, good for at most seven days, usable by anyone who holds it. Treat such a URL as a bearer token: narrow scope, short life. Buckets that answer without any signature should never be the default.

Chapter 6 of 12

Fetching 34.3 GB

Many pieces at once

The list came back: ten small files and the weights, split over seven large ones of 4.88 to 4.97 GB. Nobody downloads 34.3 GB as one stream. One stream is limited to one connection and one storage node, and if it breaks near the end it starts again. S3 lets a client ask for part of an object by naming a range of bytes in a Range header. The answer is 206 Partial Content, with a Content-Range header that says which bytes these are and how long the whole object is. A request may name only one range, so many ranges means many requests.

So the worker opens many connections and asks for the model in pieces of 64 MiB, which is 67,108,864 bytes each. The whole model is about 512 such pieces. It keeps 32 requests in flight at a time, so the download is 16 rounds of 32. BIG-IP spreads those requests over the storage nodes, which is the point of having one address in front of them. Each piece that fails is fetched again by itself.

How long it takes is set by how fast the storage can read, since the network between them is far wider. At 2.5 GB/s the 34.3 GB take about 14 seconds. Shared among 32 streams that is about 78 MB/s per stream, so one piece arrives in a little under a second. The 400 Gb/s network could carry 50 GB every second.

Chapter 7 of 12

Forty nodes behind one address

Spreading, reusing and surviving a dead node

With 32 requests in flight, BIG-IP has 32 choices to make. It does not hand them out blindly in turn. Storage requests differ hugely in size, so a pool for storage is normally balanced by how many connections each node has open right now, and the next request goes to the node with the fewest. Requests can also be placed by how much bandwidth a node is already carrying. A monitor probes every node on a timer. A node that stops answering is taken out of the pool, and a request that was on its way to it fails by itself. Because a GET of a byte range changes nothing on the storage, the worker can simply ask again, and this time another node answers.

BIG-IP also keeps its connections to the storage nodes open and reuses them for requests from different clients. A storage node then holds a small, steady number of connections instead of one for every client connection, and it spends no CPU on connection setup.

All of this would be a poor trade if the device in front slowed the storage down. F5's own tests put BIG-IP in front of S3 storage with small 8 KiB objects, large 100 MiB objects and a mix, at 0, 10 and 75 milliseconds of added network delay. The throughput with BIG-IP in the path stayed in line with the throughput without it.

The chart behind that claim has levels worth reading, here taken by eye from F5's bars, so each is good to a few percent. With 8 KiB objects the storage moved about 36 Gb/s without BIG-IP and 35 with it at 0 ms of added delay, about 24 with and without at 10 ms, and about 5 at 75 ms, the two bars level. With 100 MiB objects it moved about 715 Gb/s without and 670 with at 0 ms, about 710 and 685 at 10 ms, and about 565 and 580 at 75 ms. The mixed load moved about 705 and 680 at 0 ms, 700 and 670 at 10 ms, and 600 and 565 at 75 ms. So the bars with BIG-IP sit at most about 6 percent from the bars without, sometimes above them. F5 calls that no material degradation.

The chart makes a second point on its own, and it holds whether BIG-IP is there or not. Small objects collapse with delay and large ones hold up. Each request waits at least one round trip before its bytes arrive. F5's chart does not say whether its 75 ms is added once per round trip or in each direction, so the figures here take the smaller case, 75 ms per round trip, and are lower bounds. An 8 KiB object is 65,536 bits, so 36 Gb/s is about 549,000 objects a second and 5 Gb/s is about 76,000. By Little's law, the number of requests in flight equals the rate times the time each one takes. At 75 ms each, 76,000 a second means at least 5,700 requests in flight. Keeping 36 Gb/s at 75 ms would need about 41,000. A 100 MiB object pays the same round trip once for 12,800 times as many bytes, so the delay disappears into the transfer time. The worker's 64 MiB pieces sit on the large side of that line. A job that reads millions of small files, such as a training set of small images, is on the small side, and there the cure is more requests in flight or bigger objects. A faster device in front would not change it.

Chapter 8 of 12

A verb is not an action

GET can read an object, list a bucket, or read its security policy

So far every request was a GET, and they did two different things. One listed a bucket and the rest read parts of an object. That is the awkward fact about S3 behind a load balancer or a firewall. HTTP has a few methods, and each has a general meaning: GET reads a resource, PUT creates or replaces one, POST hands data over to be processed. S3 has well over a hundred actions, each with its own meaning for storage, security or administration, and they are folded into those few methods. Counted on MinIO, GET stands for 21 actions, PUT for 17 and POST for 15.

Which action a request is depends on the path, on the query string, sometimes on a header and sometimes on the body. A GET on an object reads it. A GET on a bucket with list-type=2 lists it. With the bare parameter policy it reads the bucket's access policy, which is security configuration. A PUT on an object uploads it, unless the header x-amz-copy-source is present, in which case nothing is uploaded and the storage copies an object it already has.

A rule that says this client may GET has said almost nothing. To control storage you have to work out the action, and that means rebuilding each one from its combination of method, query string, header and body.

On BIG-IP that work is an iRule, a short Tcl program attached to the virtual server that runs on events in the traffic. F5's session demonstrates one, made from a single request to an AI assistant: allow only uploads to bucket1. It has two parts. A RULE_INIT block runs once when the rule is loaded and sets three static values: the allowed bucket, "bucket1", the base domain for buckets named in the host, ".s3.example.internal", and a switch that logs refusals to /var/log/ltm. An HTTP_REQUEST block runs for every request once its headers have been read. It stores [HTTP::method] and looks further only when that is PUT or POST, the methods that change data. For those it reads the host in lower case with [string tolower [HTTP::host]] and the path with [HTTP::path], which is the path without the query string. Then it finds the bucket two ways. In the virtual-hosted style the bucket is the front of the host name: the rule cuts any port off with getfield, then takes what comes before the base domain. This check runs only when the base domain is not an empty string, so setting it to "" turns the virtual-hosted style off. If no bucket came from the host, the rule falls back to the path style and takes the first part of the path as the bucket. A request for any other bucket gets the refusal S3 clients understand, an AccessDenied error in XML.

That rule decides on method and bucket. It would still let a POST to bucket1 with the query key delete through, because it never reads the query. F5 points at exactly that case with two references on the slide: Dell's knowledge base article on deleting an ECS bucket that holds many objects, and the Amazon reference page for DeleteObjects. Closing the gap takes a few more lines: split [HTTP::query] on the ampersand, look for a key named delete, and answer with HTTP::respond 403 before the request reaches a node.

Chapter 9 of 12

One POST, a thousand objects gone

It looks like the start of an upload

Here is why it matters. A training job on gpu-3 writes checkpoints as it goes, and large uploads begin with a POST. The same job has a bug in its cleanup code, or its key has been stolen. Either way a request arrives that is also a POST with a small body. The query string says delete. The body is an XML list of object names, and S3 allows a thousand of them in one request. The request must carry a Content-MD5 header or another checksum of the body, and that is the only extra thing it needs.

Sent to the models bucket, that one small request can name a thousand model files. In this lesson's factory the models bucket holds fewer keys than that, 17 of them for this one model, so one request is enough to remove every model the factory serves. A bucket with more keys takes one such request per thousand. The storage does not stop at the first problem. It deletes key by key and answers 200 OK with one result per key, and a key that does not exist is reported as deleted too. On a bucket with versioning the objects become delete markers and can be brought back. On a bucket without it they are gone.

To a device that looks only at methods and addresses it is one more POST to the storage, like the one that opens an upload. BIG-IP has worked out the action. The factory's rule is built the way F5's demo is built, but where the demo names one bucket for everyone, the factory keeps a list per identity. This identity may start uploads in the checkpoints bucket. It may not delete anything in models. The request is refused before any storage node sees it.

Chapter 10 of 12

The upload that is allowed

A checkpoint goes in as parts

The honest work of that training job looks like this. A checkpoint is tens of gigabytes, so it is uploaded in parts. One POST opens the upload and returns an ID. Then the parts go up as PUTs, each carrying its number and the ID, many at the same time, and each one is answered with an ETag, a fingerprint of what arrived. A last POST lists the part numbers with their ETags, and the storage joins the parts in ascending order into one object.

S3 sets the limits. An upload has at most 10,000 parts. A part is between 5 MiB and 5 GiB, and only the last may be smaller. This checkpoint is 40 GiB and the job uses parts of 64 MiB, so it sends 640 parts. With that part size the largest object it could ever build is 10,000 times 64 MiB, which is 625 GiB.

Until the last POST the object does not exist, and the parts still take up space. An upload that is never completed or aborted keeps its parts forever, so a job that crashes should send a DELETE with the upload ID, and the bucket should carry a lifecycle rule that aborts stale uploads. BIG-IP sees each of these requests as its own action and allows them, because they are in the checkpoints bucket and on this identity's list. POST, the method that carried the delete, opens and closes this upload. The difference was never in the method.

Chapter 11 of 12

A profile for each job, a limit and a log

What an identity may do follows from what its job is

Once the action is known, permission can follow the job. A source that only delivers raw data gets the ingestion profile: it may upload objects to the buckets it is authorised for, and it may not list, download or delete anything. A serving worker gets the opposite: GetObject and ListObjectsV2 on models and nothing else. A training job reads its data, writes its checkpoints and deletes nothing. Every request is authenticated and authorised, and none of these profiles needs the storage administrators to trust that a client sends only the methods it promised.

The same point limits speed. A training job that lists a bucket of two million objects gets 1,000 keys per answer, so one full listing is 2,000 requests, and a job that does this in a loop can starve everyone else. A limit per identity and per action keeps one tenant's listing from becoming another tenant's slow model load. A client over its limit gets 503 with the code SlowDown, which S3 clients already know to back off from.

And because every request for every bucket passes here, one log line can say who asked, for which action, on which bucket and key, what was answered and how long it took. That is the audit trail, the source of the metrics, and the evidence for a service level: throughput, concurrency and latency per tenant.

F5's checklist for storage behind BIG-IP has five lines, and two of them reach past the request itself. The first is encryption at rest. TLS protects the bytes while they travel and ends at BIG-IP. The disks need their own protection, which is server-side encryption done by the storage as it writes. Amazon S3 applies SSE-S3, AES-256 with a key per object, to every new object since 5 January 2023. MinIO AIStor encrypts with keys from an external key manager, as SSE-S3 for a whole deployment, SSE-KMS with keys per bucket, or SSE-C with a key the client sends. A stolen drive from one of the 40 nodes then holds nothing readable, and sealing or deleting a key makes every object under it unreadable at once. That makes the key manager as precious as the data.

The second is protection from floods and abuse in front of the storage. A storage node that answers every connection can be exhausted by connections alone, before any signature is checked. BIG-IP counts first. A virtual server has a connection limit and a connection rate limit, with a mode that applies the rate per source or per destination, so one misbehaving client is cut off at the edge. The per-identity request limits above then keep a tenant with valid keys inside its share. The other three lines are already in this lesson: authenticate and authorise every request, health checks with failover and safe retry, and audit logs with metrics and service level targets.

Chapter 12 of 12

Which devices are ready for post-quantum

Watching the BIG-IPs that hold the road, as a fleet

Everything so far happened on one BIG-IP. A real factory has several: a pair in front of the storage, more at the border, and older ones that nobody has touched in years. The hybrid key exchange from the chapter on encryption in front of the storage only protects a road if the BIG-IP on it can do it, has it configured, and clients actually use it. Answering that across a fleet is an inventory job, and it is the first thing F5 Insight for ADSP v2.0 adds to its observability. F5 Insight is F5's operations platform for BIG-IP, NGINX and Distributed Cloud. It runs on premises with no outbound connection at all, on premises with a connection to F5's cloud services, or hosted by F5 as a service.

The PQC readiness dashboard works per virtual server. For each virtual server and its client SSL profile it shows one of four states: PQC supported, configured, in use, or configured but not in use. It also lists the cryptographic dependencies and gaps. On BIG-IP the configuration it looks at is concrete. From version 17.5.1 a cipher rule can name the hybrid group, for example with tmsh: create ltm cipher rule TMSH_PQC cipher DEFAULT dh-groups X25519MLKEM768:DEFAULT signature-algorithms DEFAULT. A cipher group allows that rule, and the client SSL profile uses the cipher group. It works only with TLS 1.3. F5 says a later BIG-IP release adds two more hybrid groups built on NIST curves, SecP256r1ML-KEM-768 and SecP384r1ML-KEM-1024. Crypto agility is the ability to switch to such new methods quickly as standards change, and the dashboard is where planning for it starts. F5 cites the US timeline as a reason to start now: a move to PQC across federal agencies by 2030, and national security systems fully quantum resistant by 2035. In the US documents behind those dates, 2035 is the federal goal for the move as a whole, and 2030 is when NIST plans to deprecate today's classical key exchanges and when NSA's CNSA 2.0 sets some of its milestones. They are targets and deprecation dates, and no single rule orders every agency to finish by 2030.

The same release sends what it sees elsewhere. F5 Insight exports time-series metrics to platforms such as Datadog and Splunk, and the operator chooses which metrics and data points leave. The SIEM or monitoring tool can then line up the storage path's request rates and latencies with the GPU workers' own metrics during an incident.

Three more features complete the picture. An Insights Scorecard gives each application a score from 0 to 100 and a grade from A to F, and shows the evidence behind every finding: configuration risks, protection gaps and areas to improve. Workload identification through F5 AI Data Fabric groups virtual servers, pools and backend services into application workloads and labels them by kind, such as AI/ML, healthcare, finance or identity, each with a confidence score that an operator can confirm, rename or reject. And access to Insight itself follows the directory: administrator, operator and viewer roles, sign-in through SAML, LDAP groups mapped to roles, custom roles, and an audit log of interactions with Insight's AI assistant.

What to keep

One address, one place for TLS, and the action known

Models, training data and documents all travel as S3 requests, and S3 is HTTP. Putting BIG-IP in front gives every client one address and spreads the load over the storage nodes. It takes the encryption off those nodes, so their CPU goes to keeping data safe, and it leaves one place to manage certificates and to move to post-quantum key exchange. And because a method says so little about what an S3 request does, the control that counts is on the action. Work it out from method, path, query, header and body, and a worker can fetch a model, a job can upload a checkpoint, and a single POST cannot empty the models bucket. This is the first of the factory's three layers: data delivery builds the prompt and feeds the weights, inference sends the request to the GPU that can answer soonest, and governance decides who may call it. The people who own this layer are the storage engineering team. F5's slide also calls the path faster, which its own chart does not show, so the case that holds up has three parts. BIG-IP in the data path does no harm, which the throughput chart shows within a few percent. It makes the path more resilient, with monitors, failover and safe retries. And it makes it more secure, with TLS and post-quantum key exchange in one place, the action checked on every request, and one log. For the storage product in use, F5 and its storage partners publish vendor-specific deployment guides and best practices, and a deployment starts from the guide for that product. F5 Insight then watches the BIG-IPs that hold the road as one fleet, down to which of them already speak post-quantum.
