Running a GPU cloud

A tenant is a boundary

What has to be kept apart when two customers rent GPUs from one cloud

Northlight Compute rents out GPUs. It has three servers, gpu-srv-1 to gpu-srv-3, with four H100 cards each, and two customers, tenant A and tenant B. People who hear the word multi-tenancy tend to picture one GPU cut into pieces for several users. That is one small design choice. The question that decides whether Northlight is a cloud is whether each customer is governed as a boundary of its own, with its own logins, machines, limits, network, data and bill. This lesson walks through the six things that have to be kept apart. It looks closely at every real way to hand out GPU compute: a whole server, a passed-through card, MIG slices, virtual GPUs, time-slicing and MPS. For each one it shows how it is switched on, what an operator sees, and what it protects and fails to protect. Then it turns to Kubernetes, where a namespace is often mistaken for a tenant, and to the boundaries that have no GPU in them, five of the six. It ends with a customer who shares no hardware at all and still needs the other five. Along the way it shows why bare metal is not automatically faster than a VM, how far a stolen key of one tenant reaches and how it is stopped, why a private pool for one company's teams is a GPU cloud too, what changes when a pool is sold to many customers, and a checklist an operator can hold any platform against.

Chapter 1 of 17

A user is a login, a tenant is an owner

Everything that gets created has one owner, and the owner is the tenant

A user is one login: a person, or a program with a key. A tenant is the customer that the user acts for. The tenant owns the servers, the networks, the volumes, the quota and the bill. Many users can belong to one tenant, and inside it they trust each other to some degree. Two tenants do not trust each other at all. That is the difference between a multi-user system and a multi-tenant one. At Northlight, tenant A is a company with a dozen engineers, and tenant B is a different company.

When an engineer of tenant A signs in, the identity service returns a token that names the user and the tenant. Every later decision in the control plane reads the tenant from that token. It never trusts a tenant name sent in the body of a request, because any caller can type any name there. The token is signed by identity, so a caller cannot change the tenant in it without the signature failing.

Roles live inside the tenant. Maya holds the role gpu-admin, and that role means something only inside tn-a. If tenant B also has a gpu-admin, the two roles share a name and nothing else. Kubernetes follows the same pattern one level down: a Role always sets permissions within one namespace, and RBAC permissions are purely additive, with no deny rules. Kubernetes itself has no object called a tenant. A platform built on it has to define what a tenant is and attach everything else to that definition.

A tenant does not have to be another company. The Kubernetes multi-tenancy guide describes two broad kinds. In multi-team tenancy a tenant is typically a team inside one organization. Teams there often trust each other to some degree, and the guide still calls RBAC, quotas and network policies essential to share a cluster safely and fairly. In multi-customer tenancy a tenant is a group of users who share a workload, as large as a whole company or as small as one team at it, and the guide adds that one organization often uses both meanings in different places. So a tenant can be a customer, an organization, a business unit, a project or a team. NIST's definition of a private cloud, infrastructure for the exclusive use of a single organization comprising multiple consumers, gives business units as its example of those consumers. Inside one enterprise, a research division and a product division can each be a tenant, with its own logins, GPU quota, network segment, datasets and usage report, while one company pays for all of it. What changes from a customer tenant is the degree of trust and the way usage is settled, and the boundaries themselves stay the same. At Northlight a tenant is a paying company, and that choice is written into identity before anything else exists.

Chapter 2 of 17

Six things have to be kept apart

The GPU is one of them, and the other five have no GPU in them

Each part of the board guards one side of a tenant. Identity decides who may act for which tenant. The servers carry compute, which here means GPUs with the CPUs, memory and disks around them. The scheduler holds each tenant's quota and decides who gets capacity. The network fabric keeps each tenant's machines in a segment of their own. Storage keeps volumes, datasets, model weights and secrets behind credentials that belong to one tenant. Metering writes down who held what. A tenant is a real boundary only when all six hold at once.

Right now tenant A holds all of gpu-srv-1 and two GPUs on gpu-srv-2, six in total. Tenant B holds one GPU on gpu-srv-2. One GPU there is free. The four on gpu-srv-3 are being wiped after a contract that just ended. That makes 4 + 2 = 6 for tenant A, 1 for tenant B, 1 free and 4 being wiped, which is 12.

Keeping apart also covers what a tenant can learn. A list call returns only the caller's own things, because the control plane filters every list by the tenant in the token. Northlight's API answers a request for another tenant's server by its ID with the same answer as an ID that never existed. Answering 403 Forbidden would tell tenant A that the ID is real and belongs to someone. That is a design choice of the provider, and not every API makes it. S3-style object storage, for one, commonly answers AccessDenied for a bucket that exists and NoSuchBucket for one that does not, so bucket names leak their existence there. The names, counts and IDs of another tenant are themselves data that has to stay inside its boundary, and where an API does leak existence, the names have to carry nothing secret.

Chapter 3 of 17

Compute, undivided: a whole server or a whole GPU

No GPU is shared, and two different things are still shared

The simplest way to give a tenant compute is bare metal: the whole server, with no hypervisor of the provider's on it. The tenant gets four GPUs, every CPU core, all the memory and the local disks. No other tenant is on the machine at the same time, so nothing on it can be contended or spied on while the tenant holds it.

What bare metal does not remove is the tenant who had the server before, and the one who comes after. The two are separated in time instead of in space. The disks hold the last tenant's operating system, keys and checkpoints until they are erased, so the server has to be wiped before the next customer logs in. That is why gpu-srv-3 shows four GPUs being wiped at the start of this lesson, and why they count as free only when the wipe ends. With 1 GPU free on gpu-srv-2 and 4 on gpu-srv-3, the pool then has 1 + 4 = 5 free.

One step smaller is GPU passthrough. A hypervisor runs on the server and assigns an entire physical GPU to one virtual machine. The driver inside that VM has the card to itself, and no other VM can submit work to it. The CPUs, the host memory, the disks and the network ports are still shared with the other VMs on that host. Northlight runs gpu-srv-2 that way, with three VMs: two for tenant A and one for tenant B. NVIDIA's documentation adds a rule for each card: it can be passed through or it can host virtual GPUs, and it cannot do both at the same time.

That rule decides how to read the next chapters. They show MIG, vGPU, time-slicing and MPS on gpu-srv-2 as what-ifs: the ways Northlight could cut the same four H100s instead. Today three of its cards are passed through, cards 0 to 2, and only card 3 is free. A card that a VM holds through passthrough cannot be cut by the host at the same time, so a single-card example, such as switching MIG on, uses card 3. Examples that count all four cards, such as 4 × 7 or 4 × 4, describe gpu-srv-2 after its three VMs have gone and the whole server has been rebuilt for sharing.

Chapter 4 of 17

Bare metal hands over root, so the boundary moves off the host

The tenant controls the operating system, and the host cannot vouch for itself

On gpu-srv-3 tenant B is root. It can load kernel modules, change routes and firewall rules, rewrite anything on the disks, and try to update the firmware of the network cards and drives. Any control the provider placed inside that operating system is now under tenant B's control. The owner's material puts the rule this way: the enforcement of tenancy cannot live where the tenant lives, and the host cannot vouch for itself. A firewall on the host, an agent on the host or a log written by the host all belong to the tenant for as long as the rental lasts.

So every boundary of a bare-metal server is enforced somewhere the tenant cannot reach. The network segment is set on the switch port, or on a DPU: a network card with its own processors and its own operating system, such as an NVIDIA BlueField, that the host cannot reconfigure. The owner's designs place the tenant's VRF, its own routing table, and its own VNI, the number that tags its traffic in a VXLAN overlay, on that outside point. The isolation has to fail closed: if the enforcement point breaks, traffic stops instead of flowing freely.

The management controller, the BMC, sits on a network of its own, 10.9.0.0/24 here, that only the provisioner reaches. A host that could talk to its own BMC could change its boot device or power, which turns a tenant into an administrator of the infrastructure. Firmware is an attack surface for the same reason. A change made by one tenant can survive a disk wipe and wait for the next one, so firmware has to be checked or rewritten between rentals, and no data of any kind may remain. That is a requirement the provider has to build in. Ironic's default cleaning concentrates on the disks, and its firmware steps, such as a BIOS factory reset, are off until the provider gives them a priority.

Chapter 5 of 17

Bare metal is not automatically faster

Removing the hypervisor removes one layer, and that layer is often not where the time goes

Bare metal is often sold as the fast option, on the reasoning that nothing stands between the workload and the hardware. What it removes is one layer of abstraction: the hypervisor between the tenant's operating system and the server. Whether that layer was costing anything depends on what it did for the GPU. With passthrough it does very little once the VM is running. On Linux the card is handed to the VM through VFIO, and the kernel documentation describes this device assignment as what virtual machines use when they are configured for the highest possible I/O performance, with significantly reduced latency, higher bandwidth and the bare-metal device driver used directly. NVIDIA's vGPU guide says the same of its passthrough mode: the GPU is accessed exclusively by the NVIDIA driver running in the VM, bypassing the Virtual GPU Manager. The driver in the VM is the ordinary NVIDIA driver, and the kernels it launches run on the same SMs and read the same HBM as they would on bare metal.

Most of a model server's time is spent on the card. Every decode step of Qwen3-32B in the official FP8 build reads about 34.3 GB of weights from GPU memory. An H100 SXM reads 3.35 TB/s, so one step takes at least 34.3 ÷ 3,350 ≈ 0.0102 s, about 10 ms, and the card can make at most about 98 such steps a second. That floor is set by HBM and is the same in a passthrough VM and on bare metal. What a hypervisor can touch is the work that crosses the host: interrupts, device memory access mapped through the IOMMU, the virtual network that carries requests in and tokens out, and the CPU time for tokenising and batching. How large that share is depends on the hypervisor, the CPU, the IOMMU settings and the workload. This lesson quotes no general percentage for that reason. A provider that wants a number measures its own model server both ways.

Bare metal pays off where topology matters. A training job across all four H100s of gpu-srv-3, or across several servers, depends on NVLink between the cards, the PCIe layout between each card and its network card, and the fabric. With the whole server that topology is easier to see and control, and nothing else contends for the CPUs, memory, disks and ports. Shared virtual GPUs are a different case, because their speed moves with their neighbours. Under the default best effort scheduler a vGPU may use the cycles other vGPUs leave idle, so it runs fast while the card is quiet and slower once the others get busy. The equal share scheduler divides the card equally among the running vGPUs, so a vGPU's share grows when others stop and shrinks when others start. The fixed share scheduler gives each vGPU a share that stays the same as others come and go. In equal-size mode that share is one over the maximum number of vGPUs of its type on a card. NVIDIA's example is the A10-8Q, of which three fit on one A10, so each gets one third, 33%, of the processing cycles. A shared vGPU's speed therefore depends on its allocation, on the scheduler, on its memory and on who else is on the card.

A GPU VM is sometimes the better product. It suits users who need less than a whole card, environments that live for hours, such as a test run or a training class, and teams that want an environment quickly. In this lesson's timeline the bare-metal build of gpu-srv-3 ran from the order to an active server in about ten minutes, most of it writing an operating system to wiped disks, while a VM can start from an image the host already holds. A company that already runs VMs keeps its security tooling, its golden images, its snapshots and its backup jobs, and those operations carry over to GPU VMs unchanged. Desktops are the clearest case. Virtual desktop infrastructure, VDI, gives each person a remote desktop backed by a share of a GPU. NVIDIA's vGPU software comes in editions it calls vWS, vPC and vApps, and its guide describes using them to deliver remote virtual desktops and applications. Choosing between bare metal and a VM is a choice about the workload and the operations around it, and raw speed is one input among several.

Chapter 6 of 17

Slices with walls: MIG and vGPU

One card becomes several, and each piece has resources that are its own

MIG, short for Multi-Instance GPU, cuts one card into GPU instances in hardware. NVIDIA supports it from the Ampere generation onward, and an H100 can hold up to seven. The card's memory is divided into eight slices and its compute cores, called streaming multiprocessors or SMs, into seven. NVIDIA defines a memory slice as roughly one eighth of the memory resources, in capacity and in bandwidth, and an SM slice as roughly one seventh of the SMs. Each instance gets slices of its own and a separate path through the crossbar, the L2 cache, the memory controllers and the DRAM address buses. A neighbour that thrashes its cache, or crashes with a GPU fault, stays inside its instance.

Sizes come from a fixed list of profiles. A name such as 3g.40gb means three compute slices and 40 GB of memory. On an H100 80 GB the smallest, 1g.10gb, fits seven times, and 7g.80gb is the whole card. A GPU instance can be cut again into compute instances. Each compute instance has SMs of its own, and they share the memory and engines of their GPU instance, so the memory wall stands at the GPU instance.

MIG still has limits. On the H100 there is no graphics support in MIG mode. NVIDIA's current guide makes one exception, the +gfx profiles of some RTX PRO Blackwell cards and of Thor, and no other card runs graphics APIs under MIG. Being Ampere or newer is also not enough on its own. MIG runs only on the cards in NVIDIA's supported list, and Ada cards such as the L40S and the L4 are newer than Ampere and are not on it. Bare-metal MIG runs on Linux only, CUDA IPC between GPU instances is not supported, and profiling of shared resources is not available. vGPU is how a hypervisor gives a virtual machine a share of a card. Each vGPU has a fixed amount of GPU memory. On a card that is not partitioned, the vGPUs take turns on the compute engine. A vGPU that occupies a whole MIG instance runs in parallel with the others, which NVIDIA describes as running several VMs on one card while keeping the isolation vGPU provides.

Chapter 7 of 17

Turning MIG on and cutting a card

MIG mode is a switch, the pieces are made by hand, and one known issue makes the order matter

MIG is off by default. An administrator turns it on for one card with nvidia-smi -i 0 -mig 1, or for every card by leaving out the GPU index. The tool answers with a line such as Enabled MIG Mode for GPU 00000000:36:00.0 and then All done. The generation matters here. On Ampere cards such as the A100, turning MIG on needs a GPU reset, and the setting survives reboots because a bit is stored in the card's InfoROM. From Hopper on, which includes the H100, no reset is needed, and the mode is no longer kept across a reboot. Unloading and reloading the driver turns it off again.

MIG mode alone runs nothing. NVIDIA's guide is explicit that without GPU instances and their compute instances no CUDA work can run on the card. The instances are created with nvidia-smi mig -cgi followed by profiles, given by ID, short name or full name, and -C creates the matching compute instances in the same command. The pieces do not survive a reboot either, so the layout has to be rebuilt at every boot, which NVIDIA leaves to its mig-parted tool and a system service.

Each profile can be placed only at certain positions on the card, which nvidia-smi mig -lgipp lists, so a request can be refused when the free slices sit in the wrong places. NVIDIA's guide also shows a case where the order of the request matters, and it gives that case a narrower cause. In its A100 40 GB example of a 3-2-1-1 layout, asking for profiles 19, 19, 14 and 9 fails with Insufficient Resources, while 9, 19, 14 and 19 succeeds. The guide puts this down to a known issue with the APIs: profile 9, 3g.20gb, must be given first, and the other combinations of profiles do not have this requirement. It is a known bug to work around. Most layouts can be asked for in any order. Two rules decide who may change the layout. Toggling MIG mode needs the CAP_SYS_ADMIN capability, and creating or destroying instances needs the mig-config capability, a device node that root holds by default. A container given that device node could repartition the card under every other tenant on it, so it is never handed to a tenant.

Chapter 8 of 17

MIG under Kubernetes: strategies, labels and the MIG Manager

A node label asks for a layout, and the GPUs are emptied before it is applied

On a Kubernetes cluster the NVIDIA GPU Operator manages MIG. Two settings work together, and NVIDIA warns that they are easy to confuse. The MIG strategy decides how the device plugin advertises the slices, and it does not turn MIG on. The MIG Manager turns MIG on and creates the slices when a configuration is applied.

The strategy is none, single or mixed, and none is the default. Under single, every MIG device is advertised as an ordinary nvidia.com/gpu, so a Pod asks for one GPU and gets one slice. That only works when every GPU on the node is in MIG mode with the same profile. Otherwise GPU Feature Discovery marks the node with a product label ending in -MIG-INVALID. Under mixed, each profile becomes a resource of its own, such as nvidia.com/mig-1g.10gb, and cards with MIG off stay nvidia.com/gpu.

A layout is asked for with a node label. Nodes start at nvidia.com/mig.config=all-disabled. The operator sets it to all-1g.10gb, for example. The MIG Manager then sets nvidia.com/mig.config.state to pending, terminates every GPU Pod on the node, applies the layout and sets the state to success, or to rebooting first when a reboot is configured. NVIDIA states that no user workloads may run on the GPUs being configured. Changing the slice sizes on a shared node is therefore an outage for every tenant on it. Under single strategy, a card cut into seven 1g.10gb pieces advertises seven GPUs. NVIDIA's example node has one H100 and so reports seven. A four-card node like gpu-srv-2, with all four cards cut that way, would offer 4 × 7 = 28. Since GPU Operator v26.3.0 the MIG Manager can build the layout list from the hardware at runtime, where the driver supports it. With older drivers, such as the 535 branch, the MIG Manager uses the fixed ConfigMap default-mig-parted-config.

Chapter 9 of 17

Sharing without walls: time-slicing

One card is advertised several times and its users take turns, with nothing between them

Time-slicing is the cheapest way to share. The NVIDIA device plugin is told to advertise each card several times, as replicas, and Kubernetes hands each replica to a different Pod. The processes then take turns on the whole card. NVIDIA's documentation states the limit plainly: there is no memory isolation and no fault isolation between replicas. Every workload can reach the card's memory. Each process still has a CUDA context of its own, so an ordinary crash in one program ends only that program. A fault at the level of the card, one that needs a GPU reset for example, reaches every replica, because nothing on the card contains it. All the Pods draw on the same 80 GB.

The arithmetic is simple. NVIDIA's own example is a node with 8 GPUs and 10 replicas, which advertises 8 × 10 = 80 nvidia.com/gpu. With four replicas, gpu-srv-2's four cards would show 4 × 4 = 16. With renameByDefault set to true, the resource is called nvidia.com/gpu.shared instead, so Pods have to opt in to a shared card by name.

Time is shared between processes, and Pods do not enter into it. The GPU gives an equal share of time to every GPU process across all the Pods, and a Pod can run as many processes as it likes. NVIDIA adds that asking for more than one shared GPU does not buy a proportional share of compute. That is why it recommends failRequestsGreaterThanOne set to true, which makes such a request fail at once. The setting is false by default to stay compatible with older behaviour.

Two more limits matter to a provider. Sharing applies to every GPU on a node, so one node cannot mix shared and unshared cards. And DCGM-Exporter cannot attribute GPU metrics to containers while time-slicing is on, which leaves the usage record without a per-Pod split.

Chapter 10 of 17

Sharing without walls: MPS

Several processes run on one card at the same moment, with caps and a shared fate

MPS, the Multi-Process Service, lets several processes run kernels on a card at the same moment through one shared server process. A control daemon, started with nvidia-cuda-mps-control -d, launches the server, and each CUDA program connects to it as a client. Since the Volta generation each client has a GPU address space of its own, so one client cannot read another's memory through a bad pointer.

MPS can cap what a client uses. CUDA_MPS_ACTIVE_THREAD_PERCENTAGE limits the share of SMs, and CUDA_MPS_PINNED_DEVICE_MEM_LIMIT limits memory, for example 0=1G,1=512MB for two cards. NVIDIA is precise about what a cap is. It limits how much a client may use and reserves nothing, and kernels from different clients can still run on the same SM. When the device plugin manages MPS, each replica gets an equal fraction of memory and compute, so four replicas of an 80 GB card get 80 ÷ 4 = 20 GB each. The plugin calls this support experimental, and it does not work on cards with MIG enabled.

The weak point is faults. A fatal GPU fault in one client is reported to every client on the GPUs they share, without saying which one caused it, and all of them have to exit. Until they do, the server is in FAULT and new clients get CUDA_ERROR_MPS_SERVER_NOT_READY. The server log records the PID of a client that caused a fatal memory fault. A client killed without finishing its GPU work can leave the server and the other clients in an undefined state. The server serves only clients with its own UID, unless it runs in multi-user mode, which drops isolation between users. Outside the device plugin, MPS itself also runs on top of MIG, where the limit of 48 clients shrinks with the size of the compute instance. Both forms of sharing fit processes that trust each other, which means users inside one tenant.

Chapter 11 of 17

A namespace is not a tenant

It scopes objects in the API. The Pods still meet on a node

Many GPU platforms run Kubernetes and give each customer a namespace. A namespace isolates a group of API objects. Names inside it cannot clash with names elsewhere, and three kinds of policy attach to it: RBAC Roles that say who may do what, a ResourceQuota that caps what can be requested, and NetworkPolicies that filter traffic. All three are needed, and all three are rules. None of them is a wall between workloads.

Much of a cluster sits outside every namespace. Nodes and PersistentVolumes have none. CustomResourceDefinitions, StorageClasses and webhooks are cluster-wide, and kubectl api-resources --namespaced=false lists all such kinds. A claim for storage is namespaced, and the volume behind it is not, so the multi-tenancy guide asks for a reclaim policy of Delete on a shared StorageClass to stop a volume moving from one tenant to the next. DNS crosses namespaces too. By default any Pod can look up a Service in any namespace by its name, qwen3.tenant-b.svc.cluster.local, which tells tenant A what tenant B runs.

Until a NetworkPolicy says otherwise, every Pod can reach every other Pod in the cluster. Pods from two namespaces can land on the same node, and containers on a node are processes on one shared kernel. After a container escape, the other tenant's containers and volumes on that node are in reach. The guide's reason for putting authorization first is that a tenant who can edit another tenant's objects can change or switch off every other policy. For tenants that do not trust each other, it adds node isolation, where a set of nodes runs one tenant's Pods only, and sandboxing, where each Pod runs in a virtual machine or on a userspace kernel.

Chapter 12 of 17

Hardening the namespace boundary

Each rule has a gap of its own, and each gap has a documented fix

A ResourceQuota is enforced only when the API server runs with the ResourceQuota admission plugin, and only in namespaces that contain one. For GPUs only the requests. form is allowed, because extended resources are whole numbers and cannot be overcommitted. The quota checks Pods as they are created. A Deployment over the limit is accepted, and its controller then fails to create the extra Pods, so the 403 appears in the workload's events. Quotas are absolute numbers that know nothing of cluster capacity or nodes, so contention is first come, first served. The admin also has to stop tenants from editing their own quota.

A NetworkPolicy does nothing without a network plugin that implements it, and nothing in the API says when it is ignored. Policies only add allowed traffic, and a connection needs both the sender's egress and the receiver's ingress to permit it. A namespace is chosen by the label kubernetes.io/metadata.name, which the control plane sets and nobody can change. Traffic from a Pod's own node is always allowed.

Pod Security Admission blocks the escape itself. The label pod-security.kubernetes.io/enforce: restricted on a namespace rejects Pods that ask for privileged mode or host namespaces. Dedicated nodes take two parts. A taint such as dedicated=tenant-b:NoSchedule with a matching toleration lets tenant B's Pods onto the nodes. A node label and required node affinity keep them off all others. The label should start with node-restriction.kubernetes.io/, a prefix the NodeRestriction plugin forbids kubelets to set, so a compromised node cannot claim to belong to another tenant. Sandboxing is chosen per Pod with spec.runtimeClassName. Per-tenant control planes go further, with their own API server and etcd, and the guide notes they do nothing for isolation on the nodes.

Chapter 13 of 17

Quota, network, data and the record

Four boundaries that are enforced far away from the GPU

Five of the six boundaries have no GPU in them. Identity came first, in the opening chapter. This chapter takes the other four, and scheduling and quota come first. Tenant A may hold six GPUs and holds six. It asks for a seventh. One H100 is free on gpu-srv-2, and the request is refused all the same. The limit is a property of the tenant, and free capacity does not change it. Without that check, the first tenant to ask could take every free GPU and leave tenant B unable to grow.

The network is next. Each tenant's machines sit in a segment of their own on the fabric, so a packet from tenant A's server to tenant B's is dropped at the switch. In the owner's designs a segment is a VRF with its own VNI on an EVPN-VXLAN fabric. The VRF gives the tenant a routing table of its own, so two tenants can even use the same private addresses. The VNI is the 24-bit number in each VXLAN header that says which tenant network a packet belongs to. Quotas do nothing for traffic volume. The Kubernetes guide says quotas cannot protect against every kind of sharing, network traffic among them, and suggests node isolation as the better answer. In a separate section on quality of service it describes the CNI bandwidth plugin, which rate-limits a Pod's traffic with Linux tc queues, and warns that the plugin is considered experimental.

Storage follows the same rule with credentials: a key issued to tenant A opens tenant A's volumes and buckets and nothing else. A GPU that is perfectly isolated protects nothing if a storage key is shared or a bucket policy is too wide. Last is the usage record. Metering writes down which tenant held which GPUs and for how long. Bills, capacity plans and disputes all start from that record, so it has to be kept per tenant and kept away from tenants' reach.

Chapter 14 of 17

Noisy neighbours are not only on the GPU

A model server needs CPU, memory, disk and network, and those can be shared too

On gpu-srv-2 each virtual machine has a whole H100 to itself. The three machines still share one host: its CPU cores, its memory, its local disks and its network ports. A model server leans on all four. Tokenising requests and assembling batches is CPU work. Loading the weights of Qwen3-32B, 34.3 GB of files in the official FP8 build Qwen3-32B-FP8, is a long read from storage. Every answer leaves through the network port.

So tenant B can slow tenant A down without touching tenant A's GPU, by saturating a disk or an uplink. This is the noisy neighbour problem. In this lesson's illustrative numbers, a load that takes about three minutes on a quiet host takes about nine while tenant B copies a dataset, three times as long, with tenant A's H100 idle the whole time. A GPU that waits for its weights earns its tenant nothing and is still billed, because the allocation record runs.

The fixes are reservations and limits on each shared thing: pinned CPU cores, reserved memory, caps on disk and network throughput. Kubernetes quotas do not cover network traffic. The multi-tenancy guide names the CNI bandwidth plugin, which shapes a Pod's traffic with Linux tc queues, and marks it experimental. MIG, vGPU and every other way of dividing a card do nothing here, because this contention is on the host and the network. When a customer needs performance that does not move, the stronger fix is to stop sharing the host, which is what tenant B bought with gpu-srv-3.

Chapter 15 of 17

A stolen key stays inside its tenant

No boundary stops a credential from being stolen, and the boundary decides how far a stolen one reaches

Every boundary in this lesson checks a credential and then trusts what the credential names. A key that is stolen therefore keeps working for whoever holds it, until it is revoked or it expires. Keys get copied from environment variables, from laptops, from build logs and from breached build hosts, and nothing in a cloud can tell that the caller holding a valid key is not the person it was issued to. The owner's material on F5 BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes, BNK, says this on a slide called Straight Talk. What BNK will not do is stop a credential from being stolen. What it does is limit the blast radius, speed up containment and give a stronger story to enterprise buyers. The blast radius is everything an attacker can reach and spend with one stolen credential. The same deck says buyers of shared GPU and inference infrastructure first want to know what happens to their data if another tenant is compromised. BNK's answer rests on three foundations the deck lists: multi-tenancy, which isolates each customer at the network layer even on shared GPUs, zero trust, where every connection is verified against policy, and authorization, where every request is tied to a tenant, metered, and can be revoked at once.

Walk one stolen key through Northlight. An attacker copies an API key of tenant B at 03:12. The key carries tenant tn-b and a role, so the attacker acts as tenant B and as nothing more. A list returns tenant B's servers only. Tenant A's servers answer 404, the same as IDs that were never issued, and tenant A's buckets answer 403. Inside tenant B the key reaches everything its role allows: the bucket tn-b-checkpoints, the login keys it may install, and new orders. That part of the damage is real, and no boundary prevents it. Orders stop at tenant B's quota. Tenant B may hold 10 GPUs and holds 5, so the key could add at most 10 − 5 = 5, and with one H100 free in the pool the most it can take this minute is 1. A server the attacker logs in to sits in VLAN 2102, with no route to tenant A's VLAN 2101 or to the BMC network 10.9.0.0/24. Tenant B's data is exposed. Tenant A's data, servers and network are not.

Narrower keys shrink the radius further. A key that may only read usage cannot order anything. A key bound to one project opens that project's buckets only. On the inference path the owner's BNK gateway material ties every request to a tenant and meters it, and sets budgets in tokens per minute and tokens per day, TPM and TPD, for each client and service account. A stolen inference key can then spend at most its budget, however fast the attacker loops, before its requests are refused. In the owner's lab the gateway answered 403 model_not_allowed for a request outside a key's model scope. It also stopped a key at a spending budget set in micro-USD: the gateway reserves the cost of the prompt plus max_tokens before it forwards a request, and answers 429 budget_exceeded when that reservation would pass the budget. The lab tested that spending budget, and the TPM and TPD budgets come from the workflow material.

Containment is revocation, and how fast it lands depends on how the token is checked. Tenant B's administrator first deletes the API key in Northlight's own API, so it can sign in no more. The API key is Northlight's own credential, and no OAuth standard covers it. The access token already issued from it is an OAuth token, and RFC 7009 defines how to revoke one: POST /revoke with token= and an optional token_type_hint of access_token or refresh_token, answered with HTTP 200. The RFC says the invalidation takes place immediately, and allows that in practice there can be a propagation delay while some servers know and others do not. Revoking a refresh token SHOULD also invalidate the access tokens issued from the same grant, where the server supports access token revocation. The catch is the self-contained token. Northlight's identity service signs tokens that last one hour, and a service that checks only the signature accepts a revoked token until it expires, up to 60 minutes later. RFC 7009 names two ways out: short-lived access tokens, or some backend interaction between the service and the authorization server, which the RFC calls currently non-standardized. RFC 7662, token introspection, later standardized such a check. A service sends it the token and gets back "active": false for a token that has been revoked or has expired.

Cutting off traffic is a separate way to contain an attacker, and it does not wait for any token check. The owner's lab measured it with a firewall block list on the BNK data plane, not with token revocation. With simulated GPUs, the block list was updated 0.1 s after the decision to block, while 5 of 5 other callers kept being served, and a drop in the data plane typically took effect within a minute. Records speed containment from the other side. An order for GPUs at 03:12 from a tenant that never orders at night shows in the order and audit records at once, whether it is refused or not. Any GPU such an order does win shows in the usage record long before it shows on a bill.

One company's pool or many customers' cloud

A GPU cloud can be private, single-tenant and unvirtualized, and selling it adds a business on top

Northlight sells to outside companies, and that can make a GPU cloud sound like a public service by definition. NIST SP 800-145 defines cloud computing by five essential characteristics: on-demand self-service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity and measured service. None of the five says who the consumer is or whether the machines are virtual. NIST's private cloud is infrastructure provisioned for exclusive use by a single organization comprising multiple consumers, with business units as the example, and it may be owned, managed and operated by the organization, a third party or both, on or off premises. An enterprise that puts its own GPU servers behind a portal, lets its teams order capacity themselves, provisions it automatically, meters it and takes it back when it is released runs a GPU cloud. This is often called private or internal GPU as a service, GPUaaS. Such a pool commonly replaces the bill with showback, a usage report for each team, or chargeback, an internal charge against each team's budget, and both rest on the same metering as a customer bill.

A GPU cloud need not be virtualized either. Bare metal describes how compute is delivered, and cloud describes how it is requested, provisioned, measured and released. gpu-srv-3 shows it: tenant B ordered it through the API, the provisioner built it with no person involved, metering opened a record, and when the contract ends the server is wiped and goes back to the pool. A GPU cloud need not be multi-tenant at all. A single-tenant cloud, dedicated to one customer or one organization, still needs self-service, provisioning, metering and a lifecycle. Multi-tenancy becomes a requirement when shared or multi-customer operation does. NIST's resource pooling serves multiple consumers using a multi-tenant model, and inside a private cloud those consumers are the organization's own units.

Turning an internal pool into a service for many customers changes the job more than the hardware. An internal pool for research and product teams already needs provisioning, scheduling, access control, observability and capacity planning. Outside customers add customer accounts and organizations, each with its own users, roles, credentials, projects, policies and quotas. They add audit, a record of who did what that can be shown to a customer or an auditor. They add support workflows: a customer opens a case, and a provider engineer needs access to that one tenant's objects for that one case, granted, limited in time and logged. They add service levels, which can cover availability, provisioning time, support response, failure recovery and notice of maintenance. At that point the work is no longer resource management alone. It has become the operation of a cloud business.

Capacity management changes most. An internal pool is planned against known demand, and its headline number is installed GPUs. A commercial service has to know how much it can provision to a customer now, after taking away what is held, reserved, in maintenance or broken. Northlight has 12 installed H100s. Tenant A holds 6 and tenant B holds 5, so 12 − 6 − 5 = 1 can be sold this minute. Quotas are ceilings and reserve nothing. Tenant A has 6 − 6 = 0 left and tenant B has 10 − 5 = 5, so the quotas already allow 5 GPUs of orders against 1 free. A capacity manager watches that gap, because tenant B ordering its full headroom would be refused for lack of hardware while its quota still allows it. Metering feeds both this plan and the bill. Billing is commonly described as three separate steps: metering measures use, rating applies the prices, and billing or settlement turns rated use into an invoice. A GPU-hour counter on its own is not a billing system.

A checklist for the boundary

Seven areas an operator checks before calling a platform multi-tenant

Everything in this lesson fits one list, which an operator or a buyer can use to check whether a platform keeps tenants apart. It starts with the definition. Write down what a tenant is on this platform: a customer, an organization, a business unit, a project or a team. Every later item attaches to that definition. A platform that uses two meanings, such as the customer for billing and the team for quota, needs a written rule for how they nest. Identity comes next: users, roles, service accounts and credentials, all scoped to one tenant, with the tenant read from a signed token and never from a request body. Then compute: which delivery models are offered, dedicated server, dedicated node, GPU VM, vGPU or MIG instance, and which of them may carry tenants that do not trust each other.

Networking covers three things. Traffic between tenants is dropped, by a VRF and VNI per tenant on the fabric and by a default-deny NetworkPolicy inside Kubernetes. Each tenant has private networks of its own. Each tenant has a way in from outside, and that path is part of the boundary too. On this board the customer reaches the fabric directly, and that path has to land in the customer's own segment, through its own VPN, public address or load balancer, and nowhere else. Network policy ties the three together and is enforced where the tenant cannot change it.

Storage covers volumes, datasets, model weights, checkpoints and secrets, and their backups. A backup is a copy of tenant data that outlives the volume it came from. In Kubernetes a VolumeSnapshot is a namespaced request, similar to a claim, and the VolumeSnapshotContent behind it is a cluster resource, like a PersistentVolume. The VolumeSnapshotClass must set a deletionPolicy. Delete removes the storage snapshot along with the VolumeSnapshotContent, and Retain keeps both. A tenant that leaves under Retain leaves snapshots of its data in the cluster, so backups need the same owner, the same keys and the same end of life as the data they copy. Policy covers quotas, priorities, reservations and the scheduling rules that apply them.

Operations close the list: provisioning, metering, observability, audit, lifecycle and recovery, each kept per tenant. Kubernetes auditing gives a chronological record of actions that answers what happened, when, who initiated it, on what, where it was observed, from where and to where. For a tenant boundary each record also needs the tenant, and the log sits where no tenant can edit it, the same rule as for the usage record. Lifecycle means the wipe and the firmware check between rentals, and the removal of a departed tenant's volumes, buckets, snapshots and keys, with its records kept or deleted on a written schedule. Recovery means bringing a tenant back after a failure without exposing it to anyone else: a failed server is rebuilt into the same segment, with the same tenant's keys, from that tenant's backups only.

What to keep

Dedicated hardware still needs the other five

Tenant B now holds all of gpu-srv-3. Nobody shares its GPUs, its CPUs, its memory or its disks, so the compute boundary is as strong as it can be. The other five are untouched by that. Tenant B's users still have to be told apart from tenant A's. A quota still decides how many servers tenant B may take. The server's ports still have to sit in tenant B's network segment, enforced on a switch or a DPU that tenant B's root account cannot reach. Its datasets and model weights are still protected by credentials and nothing else. Its usage still has to be recorded, and the server has to be wiped, disks and firmware, when it comes back. A cloud that never splits a GPU is multi-tenant all the same. The ways to share a card are choices inside one of six boundaries.
