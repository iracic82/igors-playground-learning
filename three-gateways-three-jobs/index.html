BNK and gateways

Three gateways, three jobs

API gateway, AI gateway, inference gateway: what each one decides and when you need it

The word gateway is used for three different things in front of a model, and people argue past each other because of it. One kind grew out of API management and treats a model as one more API to expose and protect. One kind was built for AI and decides who may use which model, what it may cost and what may be said. One kind lives next to the GPUs and decides which of them serves each request. They answer different questions, they are run by different teams, and a real deployment often has more than one. This lesson sends one agent's work through all three, opens up the AI gateway's own loop and its control of tools, and sorts out the many names the market uses. Along the way it places all three in the AI service plane, follows a builder through the five questions a governed path has to answer, splits the AI gateway into its control plane and its regional gateways, shows how users and groups arrive from the identity provider, works through a real group policy with its arithmetic, lets BNK hand the model choice to an external router, names the people who buy each part and the platform the AI gateway belongs to, and ends with the three ways the layers are combined.

Chapter 1 of 17

Why one click needs governing

Identities, tools, models and places all multiply

Take the travel planner again. One user request sets off an orchestrator and five specialist agents: flights, hotels, activities, weather and travel advice. Some of them call a model the company hosts on its own GPUs. Some call a commercial model over the internet. Some call tools through MCP, the Model Context Protocol, the common way an agent finds and calls tools: a weather service, a booking system, a document store. Now count what has to be controlled.

Every agent is an identity that is not a person. Security teams call these non-human identities, and one click here already makes six of them active against one human. Each needs authentication, the proof of who it is, and authorization, the list of what it may do. Every tool is a way to act on the world, and so a way for a tricked agent to do harm. Every model has its own price, its own key and its own API. Every location, a second office or a second cloud, adds another copy of all of it.

F5's session writes it as a product: identities times tools times models times locations. Four problems come out of it. Who is calling, a human or a machine. What it may execute, meaning which agents may use which tools. Who pays, because a token bill with no owner cannot be charged to a team. And security, because leaked data and injected instructions arrive inside the same requests. Nobody can put the rules into each agent, since every team writes agents its own way and changes them weekly. The rules have to live somewhere all the traffic passes.

Chapter 2 of 17

Two layers: GPU routing and AI governance

A control point without rewriting every app

F5's answer starts by separating two jobs that are easy to blur. One is GPU routing: getting each request for a self-hosted model to the right worker fast. In the factory that is pillar 2, BNK with the endpoint picker. The other is AI governance: deciding who may use which model and tool, at what cost and under which content rules. Governance applies just as much to a commercial model across the internet, where no GPU of yours is involved.

The governance layer is a central enforcement point in front of every model and tool. Applications and agents are configured once to call it, and from then on policy changes there without touching the agents. That is the point of the slide's subtitle: a central enforcement point without redesigning every AI app. F5 draws it with four functions: identity and authorization, mediation of MCP tool calls, model policy, and guardrails and quotas.

Gartner's definition, which F5 quotes, describes an AI gateway as an intermediary between applications and AI services or models that gives one place for security, governance and observability of AI workloads. F5 lists four challenges such a point answers: access to models and tools that is fragmented across providers, interfaces and ways of signing in, data leakage with injected prompts and rogue tools, token spend with no budgets or attribution, and blind spots in usage, quality and security across providers. The value it names for each, in the same order: no lock-in thanks to failover across providers, the same security for every use of AI, lower costs that can be charged to their owner, and one place for audit and compliance reports. On this scene both layers are drawn. The AI gateway hangs off the border, and the picker sits behind it next to the GPUs.

Chapter 3 of 17

Where the AI service plane sits

A layer between AI demand and GPU capacity

F5's BNK and NVIDIA deck draws the whole stack as a layer cake, and the gateways of this lesson all live in one layer of it. At the top is the demand: apps, agents, users and APIs. At the bottom are the things that supply answers. First the models, then the infrastructure that runs them, then the chips inside that infrastructure, and under everything the energy that feeds the chips. Between demand and supply sits a layer F5 calls the AI service plane. The deck defines it as the production layer between AI demand and GPU capacity, where routing, multi-tenancy, security and economics are enforced. It lists five jobs inside it: routing, security, observability, caching and optimization.

The next slide turns the cake on its side and calls it the AI service path. Users, apps, agents and APIs send AI demand into the service plane. Out of the other side comes governed AI traffic, going to models, inference endpoints and Kubernetes GPU clusters. Three kinds of system hang off to the side of the path: storage and RAG systems, which hold the documents an agent retrieves, security systems, and observability systems. The plane's own work is written as six verbs, which the deck lists without spelling out. In this lesson's words, Deliver: get the request to a model that can answer. Optimize: use the GPUs well, with caching and cache-aware picks. Secure: check who calls and what is said. Observe: record what happened. Govern: apply who may use what, at what cost. Scale: keep doing all of that as traffic grows.

The deck's argument for a separate layer is that ordinary application delivery was not built for this traffic. AI traffic streams tokens for seconds or minutes per request, many tenants share the same GPUs, and agents call in loops with no person waiting. In this lesson's terms the three gateways are three parts of that plane. The API gateway and the AI gateway carry the secure and govern verbs. The inference gateway carries deliver and optimize for the factory's own GPUs, where Qwen3-32B runs from its FP8 build of 34.3 GB on Pods qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-x7k2p and qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-m9q4t. Observe and scale run through all three. The deck's next slide is headed BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes is the AI Service Plane, and says capabilities that normally span several routing and orchestration layers now sit in one enterprise control point.

Chapter 4 of 17

The API gateway: a model is one more API

Reuse what already guards your APIs

The first approach F5 calls API-native. It adds awareness of AI to an API gateway that a company already runs. An API gateway publishes APIs and protects them. F5's slide lists its key capabilities: authentication and authorization with API security, token budgets with quotas and rate limits, and policy, audit and lifecycle management, which follows an API from its first version to its retirement. Pointed at a model, it treats the model as one more API with intent. What is new is mostly the counting. Where it used to count requests per minute, it now counts tokens, because one request can cost a hundred tokens or a hundred thousand.

This approach is best where API management is already established and most use of models goes to commercial providers. Its advantage is operational. The team, the tooling, the developer portal and the processes already exist, and the model becomes one more entry in a catalog. F5's slide names Kong as an example of this kind of product. It is run by the API platform team, and developers are its users.

Its limits follow from what it was built for. It sees a request as a method, a path, headers and a body. It does not read prompts as language, so it cannot judge whether a prompt leaks a customer's data. It does not choose between models by price. And it knows nothing about GPUs. In F5's layered design this layer is optional. When it is present it is the outermost layer, exposing and protecting the APIs and AI endpoints, and it passes what it accepts on to the AI gateway.

Chapter 5 of 17

What a builder has to work out

The approved path must be easier than the ungoverned one

F5's AI Security Platform session tells the AI gateway's story through two people. Alex is a builder, a developer who wants one stable AI experience. Sam runs the AI platform and its security, and needs identity, policy, security, cost control and a record of what happened. Alex's work is ordinary: review code, build an AI feature, connect an agent, test a better model, and meet the release date. Calling a model directly is easy for one person. The trouble starts when everybody does it.

Before every one of those tasks Alex has five things to work out, and the session lists them under five words. Identity: which credential or key to use. Approved services: which provider and which model the company allows. Data: whether code or secrets may leave the building in a prompt. Cost: who owns the usage and the budget. Security: which guardrails and hooks the request must pass. Each answer lives with a different team, and each can change. A developer who has to ask five teams before every test takes a shortcut, such as a personal key to a commercial API pasted into a config file. The session puts the design rule plainly: Alex needs the approved path to be easier than the ungoverned path. Its answer is three parts. One enterprise inference API, so Alex codes against one contract. Policy applied centrally, so the five answers are already attached to Alex's group. Cost attributed automatically, so nobody keeps a spreadsheet of keys.

Sam's side has its own story. A team often starts with a small proxy in front of the providers: one endpoint, central provider keys, basic routing and some usage visibility. Then Claude Code, applications, agents and internal tools all connect to it, and the weekend project becomes a 24-hour service. Sam now runs a PostgreSQL database, Redis and highly available workers, secrets and key rotation, upgrades and security fixes, retries when a provider fails, alerting and incident response, and budgets and chargeback for many teams. The session's line is that any successful AI gateway becomes critical infrastructure. That is the job the AI gateway in the next chapters is built for.

Chapter 6 of 17

The AI gateway: one endpoint, many models

Built for models, agents and tools

The second approach is AI-native: a gateway built for model and agent traffic from the start. Its first job is to give every caller one stable API, whatever model stands behind it. F5 AI Gateway presents one entry point under one name, which can sit in the company's own domain with certificates the company manages. Behind that name several gateway nodes share the load, and the same design runs as a service or entirely on the company's own premises. Callers address it on purpose. It is an endpoint they are configured to use, and it does not sit in the network path catching other traffic.

A caller asks for a model by an alias that the platform team defines, here travel-default. The gateway maps the alias to a real model at a provider, attaches the provider's key, and translates the request into that provider's format where it differs. The caller never holds a provider key. If the provider fails or slows down, the gateway falls back to another model, and it can choose by policy, by cost or by latency. Budgets per team and per user, chargeback of the tokens spent, guardrails, caching of answers and governance of MCP tools are on the same slide.

Callers prove who they are in one of three ways. A virtual key is a key a user creates in the gateway's portal, inside one of their groups, and sends with every request. It suits scripts and apps that cannot sign in. An access token comes from the company's identity provider. The gateway checks its issuer, audience, signature and expiry and exchanges it for a short-lived platform token. That suits an app acting for a user who has already signed in. A device login suits coding agents such as Claude Code. The tool shows a code, the user approves it once in a browser through the identity provider, and from then on the tool refreshes its own token, with no key stored on the machine. Claude Code and Codex appear on F5's slides as examples of this client pattern, a coding tool that calls an inference API. F5's field deck says so in as many words: they are examples, and they are not certified integrations.

F5's Distributed Inference session names Portkey as an example product of the AI-native approach, beside Kong for API-native and Envoy for inference-native. The same session lists the providers F5 AI Gateway governs: AWS, Azure, GCP, OpenAI, Anthropic and Gemini. Three of those are clouds that host many vendors' models, and three are the APIs of the companies that make the models, Gemini being Google's. A group may be allowed some of them and not others, and a budget can be set per provider as well as per user and per group. A token for the gateway can be a JWT, a JSON Web Token: a signed set of claims from the identity provider, whose signature a receiver checks with the identity provider's published keys. JWT access tokens are on F5's list for the release after the first, and the session says no JWT claim schema is settled yet.

There are two ways to run it. In the SaaS method both the management and control plane and the gateways run as a service. The gateways sit in the available SaaS regions, North America and EMEA first and APCJ after them, and there is nothing to install or run yourself. In the on-premises method both the control plane and the gateways run in your own environment, you choose the site where each gateway runs, and everything stays inside your environment. Callers see the same thing in both: one name, in your own domain if you prefer, with certificates you manage.

Under the gateway sits F5's own data plane. The session says TMM, the traffic engine that BNK also runs at the border of this factory, is the data-plane foundation of F5 AI Gateway, and it is careful about what that means. The product is a larger system: a control plane, identity, governance, the integration with security, objects for providers and models, telemetry, and the gateway data plane. It is not a BIG-IP configuration model, it is not everything running inside TMM, and it is not BNK. The session states two facts side by side. F5 moved away from the earlier open-source implementations of its AI gateway. And the new gateway is built on TMM with 100% F5-owned source code. The same build runs on a laptop, a VM, a private cloud or as SaaS, with no Kubernetes needed.

Chapter 7 of 17

One control plane configures many gateways

Configuration flows down, logs and health flow up

One entry point does not mean one machine. F5 AI Gateway splits into two planes. The management and control plane is where an administrator works, through a console or an API. The data plane is the gateways themselves, the nodes that actually receive requests and send them on. The session draws three of them, Gateway: US East, Gateway: US Central and Gateway: US West, and each can be SaaS or self-hosted. The rule between the planes is short. Configure once, apply to every gateway. Configuration and policies flow down from the control plane to the gateways. Logs, health and audit records flow up from the gateways to the control plane, asynchronously.

Asynchronously means the records do not hold up the traffic. A gateway ships its logs, health and audit records up in the background, after the requests they describe. The session does not spell out how a gateway checks each request. The way it draws the flow, with policy sent down to each gateway ahead of time, reads as each gateway holding the policy it was sent and applying it where the request arrives, so that a request from an office in Chicago can be handled in US Central without the console being in the same region. Two limits follow from what the session does say. The dashboards trail the traffic by however long the records take to arrive. And shared enforcement across nodes, for example one budget counted across several gateways at once, is listed as a direction for general availability, so in the early release a limit should be read as held per gateway until F5 says otherwise.

Each gateway carries its own slice of the configuration. Four things are associated with it: the users and groups that have access to it, the providers it may send to, the MCP servers and tools it fronts, and the guardrail policies it applies. So a gateway in US East can be open to the Platform and Finance groups and reach an MCP server that only exists in that region, while US Central serves Support. The administrator sees all of it in one place, with dashboards and alerts built from the records that flow up. For this factory that means the platform team can add a gateway near a new office and give it the same groups and guardrails with one association, instead of building and securing a new proxy.

Chapter 8 of 17

Who is signed in: SSO with JIT or with SCIM

How users and groups reach the gateway

A group policy only works if the gateway knows who belongs to which group. F5 AI Gateway takes its users and groups from the company's identity provider, the system where staff accounts live. The session names Microsoft Entra ID, Okta, Ping Identity and Google Workspace, and any provider that speaks OIDC or SAML, the two common single sign-on protocols. Single sign-on, SSO, means the user signs in once at the identity provider and other systems trust the result. There are two ways to keep the gateway's copy of users and groups in step, and they differ in when the copy is made and what happens when someone leaves.

SSO with JIT, just in time, creates the user at first sign-in. Nothing exists in the gateway before then. When Dana from Finance signs in for the first time, the identity provider sends a signed token, the gateway creates Dana's account on the spot, and it reads Dana's groups from that sign-in token. With OIDC the groups usually arrive as a claim in the ID token, and with SAML as an attribute in the assertion. It is simple to set up. The open question is the other end of the life cycle. Under JIT the gateway's copy of Dana is made at sign-in, and the session describes no step that tells the gateway when Dana leaves. It promises that clean exit only for the SCIM method, so a buyer who relies on JIT should ask how leavers and their existing virtual keys are handled.

SSO plus SCIM fixes that. SCIM, the System for Cross-domain Identity Management, is an HTTP and JSON protocol defined in RFC 7643 and RFC 7644. The identity provider pushes users and groups to the gateway before anyone signs in, using standard endpoints: /Users and /Groups, with POST to create, PATCH or PUT to change and DELETE to remove. A User resource carries an active attribute, true for a user who may sign in. When Dana leaves, the identity provider sets active to false or deletes the user, and the gateway disables the account. F5's session adds what the gateway does next: the leaver's keys and sessions are revoked. That is why F5's field deck asks early in a deal whether there is SSO, ideally with SCIM, so that usage can be attributed to real people.

Chapter 9 of 17

Inside the AI gateway: one loop for every request

Call, identify, authorize, control, inspect, route, upstream, return, observe

Every request through F5 AI Gateway follows the same loop. Call: the client sends its request to the one entry point. Identify: the gateway reads the key or token and finds the caller's group. Authorize: may this group use this gateway and this model. Control: is the caller within its budget and rate limits. Inspect: guardrails check the prompt. Route: the gateway picks the model and provider. Upstream: it sends the request on. Return: the answer comes back through the gateway, where it can be checked too. Observe: the gateway records the request, its tokens and its spend for audit.

The object that holds the rules is the group. F5's Distributed Inference session states the aim as least privilege for every user, app and agent, through RBAC and ABAC. Least privilege means each caller gets only the models, tools and spend its work needs. RBAC, role-based access control, grants rights by role, here by membership of a group. ABAC, attribute-based access control, decides from attributes of the caller and the request, such as which gateway it arrived at, which provider it asks for or how much of its budget is left. A group policy names which gateways members may use, which providers and models they may use, with a block list that always wins over the allow list, which MCP servers and which of their tools they may call, what each member may spend in a period, and rate limits in requests, tokens or both, each with its own period. It also sets how long a virtual key lives and how long the old and new keys overlap during rotation. A policy can be saved as a template and linked to many groups, so one edit updates all of them. Budgets stack in layers: per member, per group, per model and per provider.

Guardrails sit at defined checkpoints, on the prompt going out and on the answer coming back. F5 AI Guardrails reads the text for injected instructions and personal data and returns a verdict. The session lists four actions for the gateway to take on it: allow, redact, block or audit. It marks them, with ordered checks and policy selection, as the direction for general availability, and says the early release has no rich guardrail policy management. Because the checks live in the gateway, the security team can tighten them without any change to the application's code.

Chapter 10 of 17

Three groups, three policies

The session's own example, with the arithmetic

F5's session shows the group policy with example data for three groups, and the numbers make the design easy to read. Every setting is made once per group and applies to every member. Platform has 48 members. They may use all three gateways, US East, US Central and US West, all models, and all MCP servers and tools. Each member may spend $200 a month and send 1,000 requests a day, and a virtual key lives 90 days. Finance has 12 members. They use the US East gateway only, three chosen models, and reporting tools only. Each member may spend $50 a month and send 300 requests a day, and keys live 30 days. Support has 120 members. They use US Central only, two chosen models with one more model explicitly blocked, and chosen ticketing tools. Each member may spend $20 a month and send 100 requests a day, and keys live 7 days.

The budget and the rate limit are per member, so the group's own ceiling is the product. Platform: 48 times $200 is $9,600 a month, and 48 times 1,000 is 48,000 requests a day. Finance: 12 times $50 is $600 a month, and 12 times 300 is 3,600 requests a day. Support: 120 times $20 is $2,400 a month, and 120 times 100 is 12,000 requests a day. Across the 180 people the company has capped its exposure at $12,600 a month before any group or provider budget is added on top. The session gives the key lifetimes without a reason. One way to read them is by exposure: Support's keys are in the most hands and its tools reach customer tickets, and its keys are the shortest lived at a week, while Platform's engineers keep a key for a quarter. Rotation has an overlap so that a new key works before the old one stops. The block list matters most for Support: even if a later edit widens the allow list, the blocked model stays blocked, because the block list always wins. Since the three policies share most settings, a template can carry the common part, and one edit to the template updates every group linked to it.

The same records answer a question tokens alone cannot, for the GPUs the company runs itself. Thaki Cloud's insights series (thakicloud.com/en/insights) points out that even an internal AI factory with no billing may benefit from usage visibility: GPU hours by team, capacity consumption by project, reserved against actual usage, showback or chargeback, and capacity planning. Showback reports to each team what its use cost without moving money. Chargeback moves the cost into the team's own budget. Take this factory's qwen3 Deployment, 2 Pods with one GPU each, running all of a 30-day month: 2 times 720 hours is 1,440 GPU hours reserved. Suppose the GPUs were serving requests for 864 of those hours, 60%. If Support sent half of the tokens qwen3 served, Platform 35% and Finance 15%, the showback splits the busy time into 432, 302.4 and 129.6 GPU hours, which add back to 864. The 576 idle hours are capacity bought and unused, and that number, reserved against actual, is what tells the platform team whether another GPU is needed or one could go. The scene also draws a third worker, gpu-3 at 10.244.3.23, in the same InferencePool. This example meters only the qwen3 Deployment's two Pods. Metering gpu-3 as well would add another 720 reserved GPU hours a month, 2,160 in all.

Chapter 11 of 17

Tools need a gateway too

MCP servers under the same group policy

Models are half of what an agent touches. The other half is tools, and an agent with a tool can change things: book a hotel, file a ticket, push code. MCP is the common way agents reach tools. An MCP server lists its tools, each with a name, a description and a JSON schema for its input. The agent calls one with a JSON-RPC 2.0 request whose method is tools/call, carrying the tool's name and its arguments.

Without a gateway each agent connects to each tool server directly, with whatever credential its developer put in it, and nobody sees the calls. The MCP gateway in F5 AI Gateway puts tools under the same group policy as models. The gateway discovers the tools on each MCP server it fronts, and an administrator approves or disables each one. Access is then granted per group down to the single tool. The travel team's agents may call get_forecast on the weather server, and no agent of theirs may call delete_booking on the booking system. Every tool call is written to the audit log with the agent that made it. The slide names Claude Code, Codex and custom agents as callers, and Jira, GitHub and internal tools as servers.

Identity is the deeper question. When an agent books a hotel for a user, the booking system should know both that the agent did it and on whose behalf. F5 names on-behalf-of identity as where the MCP gateway is going. The decision this lesson shows is the one every call needs first: may this agent, in this group, call this tool on this server.

Chapter 12 of 17

The inference gateway: which GPU

For models you run yourself

The third approach is inference-native, and it exists only where there are GPUs to choose between. It is built on the Kubernetes Gateway API and its Inference Extension, which add two pieces to an ordinary gateway. An InferencePool names a set of model server Pods, chosen by label, and the port they serve on. An endpoint picker is a separate service that the gateway asks for every request, and it chooses one Pod of the pool. The gateway asks over Envoy's external processing protocol, ext_proc, and the picker answers with a header, x-gateway-destination-endpoint, carrying the address of the chosen Pod. Here the gateway is BNK and the picker is the F5 endpoint picker on port 9002. That is BNK from 2.4 on. The owner's audit of the 2.3 documentation found no InferencePool, endpoint picker or ext_proc, and the owner's BNK 2.4.2 lab runs an InferencePool as an HTTPRoute backend, with the Inference Extension's own CRDs installed separately.

F5's slide lists the key capabilities. Routing aware of the KV cache, so a request goes to a worker that already holds the start of its prompt, and of queue depth, so it does not wait behind a long line. Load balancing that understands inference and counts tokens and time where a web balancer counts connections. Prioritization and load shedding: some requests matter more, and when a pool is full the least important can be refused with a 429 to protect the rest. Traffic splitting by model, for example sending a share of requests to a new version.

None of this applies to a commercial model, where the provider makes those choices out of sight. The layer belongs to the people who run the GPUs: platform operations, AI infrastructure and the AI factory team. F5's slide names Envoy as an example of the kind of product. The separate lesson Which GPU answers soonest takes the picker apart.

Chapter 13 of 17

When BNK hands the model choice to a router

The ai-llm-router annotation, and what it drives

Model choice is not only the AI gateway's business. BNK can make one too, inside the cluster, and it does it by handing the question to a router of another make. BNK 2.3 turns on its AI features with annotations on a Gateway object, short key and value notes that its controller reads. The owner's notes from the BNK 2.3 documentation list three. k8s.f5.com/ai turns on semantic caching against an external cache, with semantic_cache, semantic_cache_ip_port and a receive timeout. k8s.f5.com/ai-token-counting turns on token governance, which counts and enforces: per user and per model token limits over a window, with names matched by globs such as gpt-4*, a concurrent session limit, an action such as drop, and usage logs sent to a pool. k8s.f5.com/ai-llm-router turns on LLM routing, delegated to an external router.

The router annotation names the router with llm_router_type, which takes litellm, routellm or nvidia. LiteLLM is an open-source proxy that gives one OpenAI-style API in front of many providers. RouteLLM is an open-source project from LMSYS that picks between two models per request. The value nvidia selects a router from NVIDIA. The owner's notes record only the value, not which NVIDIA product it names, so this lesson does not name one. A strategy sets what the router aims for: auto, cost, speed, quality or balanced. A max_tokens_cap limits how long an answer may be. And peak hours windows shift the RouteLLM threshold during set hours. The plain conclusion of the owner's notes is that BNK 2.3 drives LiteLLM and does not replace it. Statements that F5 is taking LiteLLM out and that it ships a LiteLLM integration are about different releases: in 2.3 BNK configures LiteLLM, and in 2.5, by the owner's notes, BNK becomes the router itself. Releases matter here in another way too. The router annotation is documented for 2.3, which has no InferencePool or endpoint picker. The picker of the previous chapter arrives in 2.4, and the owner's notes do not show whether the router annotation is still there in 2.4 or later. This factory draws both on one BNK for teaching, and a real cluster has only the features of the release it runs.

The threshold is the whole mechanism of RouteLLM, so it is worth working through. RouteLLM routes between a strong, expensive model and a weak, cheap one. For each prompt its router estimates the strong model's win rate, the chance that the strong model's answer would be preferred over the weak one's. If that estimate is at or above the cost threshold, the request goes to the strong model, otherwise to the weak one. A higher threshold sends fewer requests to the strong model. The RouteLLM README calibrates it on the public Chatbot Arena data: for its recommended mf router, a threshold of 0.11593 sends about 50% of calls to the strong model, and a client asks for that by naming the model router-mf-0.11593. The README warns that the real share differs with the real queries, so the threshold should be calibrated on traffic like your own. In this factory the strong model could be a commercial one and the weak one qwen3-32b on the factory's GPUs, and a peak hours window would move the threshold for the hours when the GPUs are busiest.

Chapter 14 of 17

One word, many control points

Ask what traffic is controlled before naming a product

The word gateway confuses because vendors came to the market from different places. F5's field deck sorts them into six. Some started as developer tools that give one API across providers, with routing, keys and budgets. Some extended API management so that the existing API platform governs AI traffic. Some are Kubernetes data planes for model, MCP and agent traffic. Some are operations platforms for AI that track prompts and add observability for AI apps. Some are global edge services that drop in front of a provider. Some are one cloud's own pattern for its managed models and its identity system. All of them call themselves AI gateways.

Customers use still more words: AI firewall, guardrails, AI proxy, model gateway, LLM proxy, model router, agent gateway, MCP gateway. The useful questions are about the traffic. Which traffic is being controlled. Who or what makes the request. Which destination is reached. Where policy runs today. Whether the job is to allow, deny, inspect, route or audit. And whether it concerns application traffic, the workforce, agents or all of them.

F5 maps the answers to three control points. Governing sanctioned use of AI across models, agents and tools is one central point: F5 AI Gateway, led by IT security and the enterprise AI platform team. Optimizing delivery to private inference on GPUs is per cluster: BNK's LLM router with the endpoint picker, led by platform operations, AI infrastructure and the AI factory team. Delivering applications, APIs and self-hosted inference in Kubernetes is also per cluster: NGINX Gateway Fabric, led by platform engineering. One customer may need more than one. A fourth tool, Workforce AI Security, governs what employees send to public AI services from the network path. Apps never call it, so it is a different kind of control point.

The field deck puts a vendor name and one discovery question on each of the six kinds, and says each card reflects that vendor's own documentation. Chapter 17 of 17

Three ways to combine them

Pick by where your models are

F5's layered design stacks the three with complementary jobs, and puts guardrails, policy enforcement, audit and telemetry beside all of them. Layer 1, the API gateway, exposes and protects APIs and AI endpoints, and it is optional. Layer 2, the AI gateway, does model abstraction and selection, budgets and AI policies. Layer 3, the inference gateway, does model delivery and performance. Which layers you need follows from where your models run.

SaaS first uses layers 1 and 2. It suits fast adoption, productivity uses and managed AI services. The company calls commercial model APIs with little infrastructure of its own, reuses its API gateway for authentication, access control and protection of the API, and puts model routing, fallback between providers, token quotas and cost management in the AI gateway, along with guardrails and observability. There are no GPUs to pick between.

Self-hosted uses layers 2 and 3. Models run on private infrastructure or in a controlled cloud, for sensitive data or for cost at scale. The AI gateway governs use and routing. The inference gateway adds routing that knows the model, management of queues and optimization of serving. The company keeps control over where data lives, which models run and on what hardware.

Hybrid uses all three. Commercial APIs and private models sit side by side behind one AI gateway that holds access to models, policies and usage controls. It routes each workload by cost, latency, capability, privacy and availability, and keeps security and observability the same everywhere, while each environment keeps the controls that are its own. Most large companies end here, with the AI gateway in the middle deciding for each request whether it leaves the building or goes to the factory floor.

What to keep

Three questions, in order

A request for a model passes up to three decisions. May this caller use this API at all: that is an API gateway, and you may already have one. Which model should answer, what may it cost and is the content acceptable: that is an AI gateway, and it is where AI use is governed, for tools as well as models, through one loop and one group policy. Which GPU should do the work: that is an inference gateway, and it only exists where you run the models yourself. Commercial models use the first two in F5's SaaS-first shape, and since the API gateway layer is optional, the AI gateway alone can carry them. Your own models need the last two. With both kinds F5's hybrid shape runs all three, and the AI gateway in the middle sends each request the right way.
