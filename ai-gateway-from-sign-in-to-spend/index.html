BNK and gateways

F5 AI Gateway from sign-in to spend

Running the AI endpoint as infrastructure, selling the control points, and where AI usage control goes next

A company that runs Qwen3-32B on its own GPUs, and also pays for hosted models, soon has a question that has nothing to do with GPUs: who may call which model, with which key, and who pays. The first answer is usually a small proxy that one engineer puts up over a weekend. This lesson follows that proxy until it becomes a production service, and lists what operating it really costs. It then walks one member of staff from their first sign-in, through their group's allowance, to the line on the bill, using the group settings F5 shows for its AI Gateway. It opens the design choices F5 made for that gateway and reads its two benchmark slides with their method. It turns to the people who buy AI usage control, what each of them worries about, the questions that open a conversation and the vendors F5 meets. It ends with where the owner of this site would take the platform next: agent identity checked per task, egress that opens for one task, a signed record per tenant, and a quarantine that can prove a workload went silent. Other lessons already teach the three kinds of gateway, the request loop inside an LLM gateway and the forward proxy for employees. This one builds on them.

Chapter 1 of 18

Two control points on one platform

What we build goes to the AI gateway, what we use goes through the forward proxy

F5 groups its AI security products into one platform with one control plane, the F5 AI Security Platform, AISP for short. Its slide has four rows. The use cases are AI usage control and AI application security. The security modules are Workforce AI Security, F5 AI Guardrails and F5 AI Red Team. The control points are F5 AI Gateway and a third-party AI gateway, so the platform's policy can also sit behind a gateway the customer already runs. The deployment options are SaaS, self-hosted and air-gapped.

This lesson is about AI usage control, and a later slide splits that between two control points that do not overlap: F5 AI Gateway and Workforce AI Security. The third-party gateway in the platform's row is not a third product of its own here, only another place the same policy can sit. F5 AI Gateway is a reverse proxy. Apps, internal services, CI pipelines and coding agents call it on purpose, because they were configured to use its address. It sends them only to sanctioned destinations: private models such as the qwen3 Deployment on gpu-node-1 and gpu-node-2, and the company's own subscriptions to hosted providers. Its jobs are token budgets, model routing, defence against prompt injection and hiding the real provider credentials. The people who run it are application developers, platform engineers and DevSecOps teams.

Workforce AI Security is a forward proxy at the network or endpoint egress. Employees' browsers and agents that act for a person reach it because traffic is steered there, and it sees every AI destination, sanctioned or not. Its jobs are discovering shadow AI, the audit trail and redacting personal data and intellectual property in real time. Its buyers are the CISO, security operations, corporate IT, HR and compliance. The deck sums it up in two phrases: the AI gateway governs what we build, Workforce AI Security governs what we use. The lesson what-employees-send-to-ai opens the forward proxy. Here the focus is the gateway, and later the people who buy both.

Chapter 2 of 18

Sam's weekend gateway

One proxy in front of the providers solves the first week's problems

F5 tells the story with two people. Alex is a builder who wants one stable AI experience: review code, build an AI feature, connect an agent, try a better model and still meet the release date. Sam works on the AI platform and security team and needs identity, policy, security, cost control and a record. Direct access to AI is easy for Alex, until everybody does it. Then every team holds its own provider keys, every app is coded to a different provider's API, and nobody can say who spent what.

So Sam says the sentence on the slide: let's put one proxy in front of the providers. Over a weekend Sam gets four things working. There is one endpoint, so apps call one address and the proxy forwards. The provider keys are central, so they live in the proxy's configuration and not in a hundred apps. There is basic routing, so a model name in the request picks a provider. There is usage visibility, because the proxy can read the usage block in each answer and add up tokens.

An open-source proxy makes this quick. LiteLLM's documentation, for example, starts the proxy from a list of models in a YAML file and exposes an OpenAI-compatible API on port 4000. Point it at the Service qwen3 on port 8000 and at one hosted provider, and on Monday Northlight's teams have a single address. Then the address works, so more people use it: Claude Code on laptops, applications, agents, internal tools and more teams. The deck's diagram shows exactly this. Every arrow points at one endpoint, and the weekend project is now in the path of everything. The next chapter lists what Sam now operates.

Chapter 3 of 18

Running it is a second job

Seven jobs come with the proxy, and none of them is routing

The slide's heading is that Sam's weekend gateway became a production service, and its subtitle says the proxy was easy and operating the 24 by 7 enterprise service became Sam's second job. The right side lists seven things Sam now runs. PostgreSQL. Redis and highly available workers. Secrets and key rotation. Upgrades and CVEs. Retries and provider failures. Alerting and incident response. Budgets and chargeback. Under the list sits the line the whole section turns on: any successful AI gateway becomes critical infrastructure.

LiteLLM's own production guide shows what each line means in practice. Virtual keys need a PostgreSQL database, given to the proxy as DATABASE_URL, and a master key in LITELLM_MASTER_KEY. Provider credentials stored in that database are encrypted with LITELLM_SALT_KEY, and the guide says not to change it after a model is added, because changing it makes the stored credentials unreadable. So a secret that cannot be rotated casually now guards every other secret. Spend updates are written to the database in batches, with proxy_batch_write_at: 60 for once a minute, because writing on every request turns the database into a hot spot. Above roughly 1000 requests per second the guide adds Redis as a transaction buffer to avoid exhausting connections and deadlocks. Jobs that must run once, such as resetting budgets or pruning spend logs, elect a single owner through Redis. Without Redis, every replica runs them.

The guide also says, plainly, that a stopped gateway cannot alert on itself, so health must be watched from outside. None of this is a fault of that project. It is what running any shared proxy at production volume needs, and it is the work that Thaki Cloud's insights series means when it says that open source can reduce license fees, but engineering, upgrades, security, incident response and billing integration are not free.

Chapter 4 of 18

Engineered like shared infrastructure

One name for callers, many nodes behind it, one control plane above them

F5's answer starts from the same picture as Sam's proxy and changes how it is built. Callers see one entry point, a single fully qualified domain name. It can be one F5 provides, or one in the customer's own domain with certificates the customer manages, such as api.northlight.example. Behind that name sit several gateway nodes, so one node failing is not an outage. Every LLM request and every MCP request goes to the same entry point, and it works the same way in SaaS and on-premises.

The gateways are a data plane. Above them is one management and control plane with a console and an API, where an administrator writes configuration and policy once and it applies to every gateway. Configuration and policy flow down. Logs, health and audit flow up, asynchronously, so a slow log pipeline does not slow a request. Each gateway, wherever it runs, carries four things from the control plane: the users and groups allowed to use it, its associated providers, its associated MCP servers and tools, and its guardrail policies. The deck's example draws three gateways, US East, US Central and US West, each either SaaS or self-hosted.

There are two ways to deploy. In SaaS, the control plane and the gateways both run as a service in F5's regions and there is nothing to install. On-premises, both run in the customer's own environment, the customer chooses where each gateway runs, and everything stays inside. The SaaS regions on the slide are North America and EMEA, with APCJ later. The closing slide's argument is a single sentence worth keeping: if hundreds of applications and thousands of agents depend on one endpoint, engineer it like infrastructure. That is the reply to the seven jobs of the last chapter.

Chapter 5 of 18

Signing in: the user appears, or was already there

SSO with just-in-time creation, or SSO with SCIM so leavers are cut off

Before anyone can spend, the gateway has to know who they are and which groups they belong to. F5 AI Gateway takes its users and groups from the platform, and the platform takes them from the company's identity provider. The slide names Microsoft Entra ID, Okta, Ping Identity and Google Workspace, and adds any OIDC or SAML provider. OIDC is OpenID Connect, the sign-in layer on top of OAuth 2.0, and SAML is the older XML-based single sign-on standard.

There are two ways to connect. With SSO and just-in-time creation, JIT for short, nothing exists in the platform until the person signs in. At the first sign-in the user is created, and their groups are read from the sign-in token. That is quick to set up. It also means the platform learns about a change in someone's groups only when they sign in again, and learns nothing about someone who has left.

With SSO and SCIM, the System for Cross-domain Identity Management, the identity provider pushes users and groups to the platform before anyone signs in. When someone leaves, the identity provider disables them, and the slide says what follows: leavers are disabled, and their keys and sessions are revoked. That last part matters for an AI gateway more than for most applications, because a virtual key outlives the browser session that created it. A developer who left on Friday still has a key in a shell profile on a laptop, and only a revocation tied to the identity provider ends it.

The groups that arrive here, such as Platform, Finance and Support in F5's example, are what the rest of the lesson hangs policy on. The lesson three-gateways-three-jobs covers the three ways a caller then proves who it is: a virtual key, an exchanged access token, or a device login.

Chapter 6 of 18

Three groups, three allowances

One policy per group, applied to every member

The group policy is the governance object. Alex calls one endpoint, and Sam attaches policy to the group. A policy names which gateways the members may use, which providers and models are allowed, with a block list that always wins, which MCP servers and which of their tools, a budget per member for a period, rate limits per member counted in requests, tokens or both with a period each, and key settings: how long a virtual key lives and how long an old and a new key overlap during rotation. A policy can be saved as a template and linked to many groups, so one edit updates all of them.

F5's example has three groups. Platform has 48 members. They may use all three gateways, US East, US Central and US West, all models, and all MCP servers and tools. Each member has a budget of $200 per month and 1,000 requests per day, and their virtual keys live 90 days. Finance has 12 members on US East only, with 3 chosen models, reporting tools only, $50 per month, 300 requests per day and 30-day keys. Support has 120 members on US Central only, with 2 chosen models and 1 blocked, ticketing tools chosen one by one, $20 per month, 100 requests per day and 7-day keys.

Add it up. Platform can spend at most 48 times $200, which is $9,600 a month. Finance can spend 12 times $50, $600. Support can spend 120 times $20, $2,400. Across 180 members the ceiling is $12,600 a month. The key lifetimes carry the same logic as the budgets. The group with the most people and the least trust gets the shortest key, so a key that leaks from a Support laptop is dead within a week even if nobody notices.

Chapter 7 of 18

A month of one member's calls

Whether the budget or the rate limit stops Mara depends on the model

Limits interact, and the arithmetic shows which one bites. Take one of Mara's typical requests: a ticket, the customer's history and her instructions add up to 1,840 prompt tokens, and the answer is 410 completion tokens, 2,250 in all. The gateway reads these counts from the usage block of the model's answer, as the lesson inside-the-llm-gateway walks through, and charges them to Mara, to Support, to the model and to the provider.

First case, the model is Qwen3-32B-FP8 on the factory's own GPUs, which Northlight prices internally at $0.40 per million prompt tokens and $1.20 per million completion tokens. The prompt costs 1,840 times 0.40 divided by a million, $0.000736. The answer costs 410 times 1.20 divided by a million, $0.000492. The request costs $0.001228. Mara's limit of 100 requests a day over a 31-day month allows 3,100 requests, and 3,100 of them cost $3.81. Her $20 budget would last 16,286 requests, so on the local model the rate limit is the limit that bites, and the budget never does.

Second case, the same request goes to a hosted frontier model at $3 per million prompt tokens and $15 per million completion tokens. The prompt now costs $0.00552 and the answer $0.00615, $0.01167 per request. Twenty dollars buys 1,713 such requests. At 100 a day she reaches that on day 18, after 17 full days of 100 and 13 requests into the eighteenth. From then until the period resets the gateway refuses her calls to that model. Same person, same limits, two different ceilings, and that is why the group policy carries both a rate limit and a budget, and why the model list per group matters as much as the dollar figure.

What ships first, and what follows

The core in early access, the richer controls at general availability

F5 AI Gateway reaches customers in two stages, and knowing which feature is in which stage avoids promising the wrong thing in a design. The early access target covers the core: platform users and groups, group governance, virtual keys, initial provider support, basic model routing, budgets and limits, basic guardrails integration, logging of LLM calls, guardrail verdicts and audit, and basic spend visibility. Access tokens, so an app passes the user's identity provider token and gets a short-lived platform token back. The MCP gateway, which extends governance from models to tools: server and tool authorization, tool access per group, on-behalf-of identity as a direction, and audit of every tool call. Routing policies and model aliases. Advanced guardrails integration, with selected policies, ordered checks, the four actions allow, redact, block and audit, checks on MCP and tool traffic, and streaming behaviour. Enhanced dashboards, notifications and alerts, cost centres and richer group ownership, custom alert rules and enforcement shared across nodes.

The guardrail point has a design consequence that the deck states directly: Sam can evolve security at the gateway without changing Alex's API integration. The app keeps calling the same endpoint, and new checks are added behind it. The MCP gateway slide is careful in a different way. It claims no final workload identity, no token exchange, no delegated identity and no JWT claim schema. Those are exactly the pieces the last four chapters of this lesson take further.

Chapter 9 of 18

Built on TMM, owned end to end

F5's design claims for its AI gateway, and how to read them

Two slides explain how F5 built this gateway, and they need to be read together. The first is titled TMM is the data-plane foundation, not the product. TMM is the Traffic Management Microkernel, the traffic engine inside BIG-IP and inside BNK. The slide draws a unified control plane for policy and configuration, an F5-owned data plane, and models, providers and tools behind it. It says the gateway is the larger system: control plane, identity, governance, security integration, provider and model objects, telemetry, and the gateway data plane. Its footer says F5 moved away from the earlier open-source implementations, and that the product is not a BIG-IP configuration model, not iRules, not all inside TMM, and not BNK. Our reading, which the deck does not spell out, is that an administrator configures groups, providers and policies, and does not need to write a virtual server or an iRule to get a gateway.

The second slide, titled Last and final AI Gateway, lists five claims. It is built on 100% F5-owned source code. There are no external dependencies in the data path and no third-party CVE in the path, which speaks directly to Sam's upgrades-and-CVEs job: the vulnerabilities to track are F5's own. The architecture is portable: a laptop, a VM, a private cloud or SaaS, with no Kubernetes dependency. iRules are supported natively on the data plane, the programmability F5 teams already know. The deck does not say how that fits with the earlier 'not iRules'. Our interpretation is that iRules are available as an extension while the product itself is configured another way. And it claims to be 5x better than the nearest competitor in added latency cost.

The last claim comes with no method on the slide. It does not say which competitor, at what load, at which percentile of latency, or what added latency cost means in milliseconds. Chapter 10 of 18

Two benchmarks, read with their method

1.5 times the throughput at the same CPU, and level with no gateway at all

The deck gives two throughput measurements, with their method in small print, and the method is what makes them readable. Both ran on a development cluster on 29 and 30 October 2026, with 1,024 concurrent workers. Each product got the same allocation: 2 pods, 2 vCPU and 5 GiB, one pod per node, on pinned cores. The same allocation did not suit every product equally, as the next sentences show. F5's gateway ran in the weaker Kubernetes QoS class and every competitor in the stronger one, each tuned by its own performance guide. Kubernetes gives a Pod the Guaranteed class when every container's requests equal its limits for CPU and memory, and Burstable when they do not. Guaranteed Pods are the last to be evicted and can get exclusive cores. LiteLLM's own guidance asks for more CPU than that allocation, so it ran at about half its recommended CPU, and the slide says to treat its result as a floor.

The first test measures peak sustained throughput. F5's gateway reached 51,441 requests per second at 59% CPU. agentgateway reached 34,938 at the same 59%. Bifrost reached 7,234 at 82%, and LiteLLM 644 at 96%. 51,441 divided by 34,938 is 1.47, which the slide rounds to 1.5 times. Per percent of CPU that is 872 requests per second for F5, 592 for agentgateway, 88 for Bifrost and about 7 for LiteLLM.

The second test streams answers and compares each gateway with a direct connection to the upstream. With no gateway the clients got 1,002 requests per second. F5's gateway got 1,011, which is 100.9%. agentgateway got 975, 97.3%. Bifrost got 638, 63.7%, and LiteLLM 32, 3.2%. The slide's title says F5's is the only gateway that runs faster than no gateway. A gap of 9 requests in 1,002 is small. The slide shows one number per product and no spread across runs, so the safe reading is that the gateway kept up with the baseline, and that the others fell behind by the stated amounts. For a model on a GPU, where one answer takes seconds, either way the gateway is not the bottleneck.

Chapter 11 of 18

Every step has an owner, and every owner is a buyer

Six steps of a governance loop, four roles who own them

F5's field session describes AI governance as a loop that restarts whenever a company reaches a new stage of AI use. The stages run from individual use of assistants, to integrated use with embedded AI, MCP and tools, to specialized use with developer tools and coding agents, to agentic AI with cloud agents and agentic processes, and finally to external use, the AI the company builds into its own applications. As adoption grows, risk, access and autonomy go up while visibility, security and control fall behind. The deck marks a tipping point where existing tools stop keeping up.

The loop has six steps. Discover: what AI is in use, by whom, with what data. Decide: policy, approved tools and risk tiers, which are the customer's call. Approved path: sanctioned tools before broad blocking. Enforce in steps: warn, then redirect, then redact, then block. Prove: audit, investigations and reporting. Optimize: adoption, licences and usage. Then the next stage begins at step 1 again.

The slide that follows names four buying roles. Security means the CISO and the security team. GRC means governance, risk and compliance: compliance, privacy and risk. IT/Ops means IT, network and infrastructure. AI Enablement, or the executive leadership team, means the CIO, a chief AI officer and the AI centre of excellence. Each step is owned by one or two of them. Discover belongs to Security and IT/Ops. Decide belongs to AI Enablement and GRC. The approved path belongs to IT/Ops and AI Enablement. Enforce belongs to Security and IT/Ops. Prove belongs to Security and GRC. Optimize belongs to AI Enablement. Hence the slide's title: every step has an owner, and every owner is a buyer. A proposal that speaks only to the CISO covers three of the six steps, and none of them alone.

Chapter 12 of 18

Three reasons to buy, in the customer's words

Visibility, risk and adoption, each with a pain, an answer and a question

The deck gives three reasons customers buy Workforce AI Security, and for each a pain in the customer's words, F5's answer and a discovery question that makes the customer measure their own gap.

Gain visibility covers GenAI discovery and monitoring, shadow AI detection and embedded AI detection. The pain: our AI visibility is scattered across web and firewall logs, and it doesn't show what people actually do. It also sounds like: we told people not to use AI yet, they're using it anyway. AI is turning up inside apps we already approved. We can't keep up with block lists. The answer is every AI request logged with who made it, which tool, and what they were using it for, the intent. The question: what share of your AI usage runs through sanctioned tools, and how confident are you in that number?

Mitigate risk covers policy and service enforcement and sensitive data protection. The pain: blocking isn't a strategy, they always find a way around it. It sounds like regulated data going into AI tools, leadership expecting controls and not just a dashboard, and an agent with the wrong permissions able to wreck systems. The answer is to turn policy into enforcement: allow, protect or block each service, account type and use of AI, for the whole company or team by team, starting in monitor mode so nothing breaks on day one. The question: your AI policy is written down, what enforces it today, and how would you know if someone broke it?

Increase safe adoption covers usage analytics, reporting and compliance. The pain: we're paying for Copilot, people still use whatever they want. It sounds like: we can't tell if the AI we bought is paying off, every control we add makes people's lives worse. The answer is to make the approved path the easy path and prove it works, redirect risky tools to the ones already paid for, and show adoption by tool, licence and team. The question: when someone asks to use a new AI tool, what does a yes take today, and how long?

Chapter 13 of 18

Opening the conversation, and what each role must see

Every conversation starts with visibility

The deck gives two openings, depending on who is across the table. With an existing F5 customer where AI has not come up, open with: are you blocking any AI today, and how do you decide what gets blocked? Then ask: how do you know what's getting through? With a new customer who is already thinking about AI, open with: what visibility do you have today, and where do you feel you're lacking it? Then ask: how do you set policy on what you can't see? Both pairs start from visibility, because a customer who cannot see their AI use cannot decide, enforce or prove anything about it.

The same platform then needs a different conversation per role. Security asks what data is going into ChatGPT, leads with mitigate risk, and needs to see one enforcement working in their own environment: a redaction, or an agent's write tools stripped. GRC asks how to prove the policy is followed, leads with risk and then the audit trail, and needs one user's AI activity from start to finish that they could hand an auditor. IT/Ops asks what breaks if it is blocked, leads with visibility, and needs to see that nothing breaks: no login prompts and no broken apps. AI Enablement asks whether people use the AI the company pays for, leads with adoption, and needs their own numbers on which AI tools and licences are actually used.

The proof of concept follows six steps: find pain points, scope the POC with a runbook of stakeholders, objectives and success criteria, monitor only with no enforcement, show AI usage across the organisation, build policy in stages, and close by tying the results to the success criteria. The typical cycle is 30 to 60 days from POC to close. Seven gates qualify it: an interception path through a PAC file pushed by device management or chaining from SASE, TLS trust through distributing F5's root CA, identity through SSO and ideally SCIM, coverage of the tools people really use, enforcement depth that matches expectations, a SaaS multi-tenant deployment, and a budget owner, an IT or network owner and a compelling event. One failed gate is a conversation. Two or more disqualify the POC.

Chapter 15 of 18

An agent's identity, checked per task

CEL evaluated over an ext_authz callout, next to iRules

A virtual key says which group pays. It does not say which agent is calling, for which task, on whose behalf. The owner's platform design moves that decision into policy. Every agent carries an identity of its own, and every call is authorized against the task it belongs to. The decision is made by a declarative layer that sits next to the iRules in TMM: an ext_authz callout, evaluated with CEL.

ext_authz is Envoy's external authorization API, and its contract is public. For each request the proxy sends a CheckRequest to an authorization service. The request carries an AttributeContext with the source and destination peers, the HTTP request with its method, path, host and headers, optional context extensions, and metadata from earlier filters, for example a validated JWT, selected with metadata_context_namespaces. The source peer has a principal, the authenticated identity of the workload. When an X.509 certificate asserts it, the principal is taken from the URI Subject Alternative Name first, and Envoy's own example of the format is a SPIFFE ID, spiffe://trust-domain/path. The service answers with a CheckResponse. Status OK allows the request. Any other status denies it, and the client gets 403 Forbidden unless the response sets another status. If the service cannot be reached, failure_mode_allow decides, and it defaults to false, so the request is refused, with status_on_error defaulting to 403.

CEL, the Common Expression Language, is the language the rule is written in. Its definition guarantees that programs terminate and that functions have no side effects, which is what a check on every request needs. Kubernetes already uses it in ValidatingAdmissionPolicy, as the lesson a-policy-says-no shows. A rule for Northlight's planning agent can say: the caller's principal is the planner's SPIFFE ID, the task it presents is still open, and the model or tool it asks for is in that task's grant. The agent's own key never needs to carry that much meaning.

Chapter 16 of 18

Egress that opens for a task and closes after it

The network follows the grant

Identity per task is half of the idea. The other half is that the network path follows the same grant. The owner's design puts it in one sentence: egress should follow the task, open only to what the task may reach, and closed when it's done. The lesson what-leaves-the-factory already shows the parts this rests on. All of a tenant's outbound traffic leaves through BNK, from an address that belongs to that tenant. A firewall policy, an F5BigFwPolicy, is an ordered list of rules with actions accept, accept-decisively, reject and drop, and with the firewall on an egress forwards nothing that no rule accepts. TMM applies a changed policy to new connections in place, and a reject resets the connection before translation, so a capture on the far side stays empty.

A task grant turns that static list into a timed one. When the planning agent's task t-4821 starts, the grant says what it may reach: the qwen3 model inside the factory, one hosted provider's API, and nothing else, for 20 minutes. The platform adds an accept rule for exactly those destinations, tied to the agent's identity and the task, above the tenant's reject-everything rule. When the task closes, or the 20 minutes pass, the rule is removed. Any later connection from that agent to the same provider meets the reject rule and is reset.

The difference from a key or a token is what happens when the agent misbehaves. A token can be copied, and a model can be persuaded to send data somewhere new. The lesson nothing-wrong-with-the-words shows an agent doing exactly that with permitted tools. A path that is closed in the network does not depend on the agent behaving. If the destination is not in an open grant, the packets do not leave.

Chapter 17 of 18

A signed record per tenant

Every decision written where the tenant cannot quietly change it

Every chapter so far ended in a record: spend charged to four layers, an audit line for each guardrail verdict, a log entry for each refused connection. The owner's design asks more of that record. Every decision should land in a signed record per tenant, produced from the path and kept by the platform owner, not by the tenant whose agents it describes. The reason is separation of duties. A record the workload writes about itself is only as honest as the workload.

Tamper evidence comes from two standard tools. The first is a hash chain. Each entry includes the SHA-256 hash of the entry before it, which is 32 bytes. Change one old entry, even one digit of a token count, and its hash changes, so the next entry's stored hash no longer matches, and neither does any entry after it. An auditor who recomputes the chain from the start finds the first broken link. The second is a signature. Periodically the platform signs the latest hash with its private key, for example with Ed25519, whose signatures are 64 bytes and public keys 32 bytes. Anyone with the public key can check that the chain up to that point is exactly what the platform signed, without trusting the storage it sits on.

The owner's design names what such a record can hold for each inference, a signed receipt: a hash of the model weights that answered, the pool, the region, the attestation of the machine, the tokens counted and a hash of the policy that allowed it. For Mara's first request in this lesson that means Qwen3-32B-FP8, the pool behind the qwen3 Service, 1,840 and 410 tokens, and the Support group's policy at that moment. For the planning agent it means the principal, task t-4821, the grant and every connection it opened. One record per tenant keeps one tenant's evidence apart from another's, the same way their networks are.

A quarantine that can prove a stop

Stop the workload, keep it for forensics, and show that it sent nothing after

The only quarantine elsewhere on this site is NVIDIA's fault quarantine in many-sites-one-platform, which cordons a node with a failing GPU so no new work lands on it. This one is different. It is for an agent or a model server that behaves badly, and its goal is to stop it and to prove the stop.

The owner's blog shows how a model server is taken out of service without deleting it. The InferencePool that the endpoint picker serves selects Pods by labels, and one of them can be a trust label such as trust.example.com/state: trusted. A controller fed by eBPF events from Tetragon and by the sandbox's audit trail can relabel a GPU worker that misbehaves. The Pod no longer matches the pool's selector, and the endpoint picker stops choosing it within seconds, while the Pod stays up for forensics with its memory and logs intact. The same controller can have BNK answer a compromised caller with 403 and add its address to the firewall.

The provable part is what turns a stop into evidence. The design states it as a quarantine with a provable stop, so you can show a workload sent nothing after it was stopped. The stop has a time, written into the signed record. From then on, every flow record for that workload's addresses comes from the same path that enforces the block. A record showing that every attempt after the stop time was refused, and that no byte was forwarded, is the proof. Because the record is signed and chained, the claim does not rest on the workload's own logs, which a compromised workload could have changed. The lesson a-dpu-the-host-cannot-touch shows how the same enforcement can move onto a DPU, where even root on the host cannot lift it.

What to keep

From a weekend proxy to an evidence trail

An AI gateway starts as a proxy anyone can stand up in a weekend and becomes critical infrastructure the moment everyone uses it, with a database, Redis, secrets, upgrades, retries, alerts and budgets to run. F5's answer is one entry point backed by many nodes, one control plane, identity from the company's own directory, and a group policy that turns into ceilings such as $12,600 a month across 180 members, with the rate limit or the budget binding depending on the model. Its design claims, F5-owned code with no third-party CVE in the path and no Kubernetes dependency, sit next to two benchmarks whose method is printed and one latency claim whose method is not. Selling it means talking to four roles who each own steps of one governance loop, opening with visibility, and being clear where other vendors are stronger. The next step on this platform carries the same idea down to each agent and each task: an identity checked with CEL over ext_authz, egress that opens for one task and closes after it, a signed record per tenant, and a quarantine that leaves proof that nothing left.
