BNK and gateways

Many sites, one platform

What decides the site and the tier in an AI Grid, and how power becomes an input with DSX

One name, three factories ended with DNS choosing a site on residency, health, distance and spare capacity. That keeps a service up. Placing inference well takes more. A prompt that needs a small model belongs near the user. A long reasoning task belongs on a large factory. A coding agent on turn twelve belongs wherever its conversation is already held in GPU memory. NVIDIA's name for the answer is an AI Grid: many sites in many places that behave as one inference platform. This lesson follows what decides the site and the tier beyond plain GSLB. Then it follows NVIDIA DSX, where the power a site may draw becomes one more input to routing and scheduling. F5's AI Grid session opens with a contrast. The hyperscalers spent twenty years building three big clouds for web traffic. The telcos built about ten thousand small ones for mobile access without setting out to, and those sites now put them in a position to sell tokens. So the lesson also covers what power and cooling allow a site to be, the slot in NVIDIA's partner reference architecture that BNK fills, the protocols of a telco's edge, what a telco has to add before its sites become a service, and which tier F5 ships first.

Chapter 1 of 16

Many sites, one platform

What NVIDIA means by an AI Grid

One name, three factories showed three sites behind one name. NVIDIA's AI Grid reference design turns that picture into a general design. It defines an AI Grid as a set of geographically distributed, interconnected AI infrastructure sites that operate as one platform, with each workload placed where it runs best for performance, cost and latency. The sites are of very different sizes: a central AI factory, regional hubs, and edge nodes in telephone exchanges and content delivery buildings.

F5's AI Grid session puts the business case in two lines. The first is a contrast. The hyperscalers spent twenty years building three big clouds for standard web traffic. The telcos built about ten thousand small ones for mobile access, by accident. The deck's conclusion is that the telcos are now the ones in a position to sell tokens. The second line is about cost. Shipping every prompt like 'what's the weather' to a $40 billion liquid-cooled factory in Texas, which the deck calls a cathedral, was never a business model. A weather question needs a small model and a short answer. It gains nothing from the most expensive GPUs on the continent, a long round trip, or a place in a queue behind long reasoning jobs. The ten thousand is a round figure for effect. NVIDIA's own count, below, is about 100,000 distributed network data centres.

The design rests on four properties. Distributed means accelerated computing sits in many telco and content delivery sites, close to where data is produced and answers are used. Interconnected means a fast network with low latency links the sites, so data, models and workloads can move between them. Orchestrated means a control plane keeps weighing SLAs, latency, cost and policy, and routes each workload by what it is. Unified means one deployment model, so an application behaves the same in a central factory, a regional hub or an edge node.

The scale is what makes this more than a diagram. NVIDIA counts about 100,000 distributed network data centres worldwide, made up of regional hubs, mobile switching offices and central offices, with enough spare power for more than 100 gigawatts of new AI capacity over time. Northlight's three sites are the same idea in small. In this lesson Madrid is the small site, Amsterdam the middle one and Frankfurt the large factory.

Chapter 2 of 16

Three groups of problems

Capacity, placement and operations

The deck sorts what an AI Grid is for into three groups. The first is capacity. The buildings, power and network of those 100,000 sites already exist and sit idle or underused. A single factory also has a ceiling: it cannot grow past its grid interconnect, the connection to the electricity network that fixes how much power the site may draw. More capacity then has to come from more sites. For a telco, radio access network sites and central offices are points of presence it already owns and can earn from.

The second group is placement. Interactive inference, vision and speech cannot wait for a round trip to a distant factory. Serving everything the same way wastes hardware in both directions. A simple prompt answered on a GB300 rack is misplaced, and so is a reasoning task sent to an edge box. Regulated tenants add sovereignty and residency: their data, models and logs have to stay inside one jurisdiction.

The third group is operations. Sites differ in hardware, so deployment is inconsistent. Without one control plane, each site is run by hand on its own. Selling inference needs more than running it: hard multi-tenancy, which the reference design defines as strict isolation at hardware and software level, role-based access control, token metering, and a marketplace for AI as a service, where model APIs are sold as metered services. The deck sums it up as the right inference in the right places, and it still has to be good inference.

Chapter 3 of 16

Distance is part of the first token

Where inference runs sets two of five delays

Placement matters because distance is part of the answer time. NVIDIA's AI Grid blog takes voice as the example. Once a reply takes longer than about 500 milliseconds, a conversation feels laggy, so the time to first token at the client becomes a hard objective. That time is the sum of five parts: the network round trip, the wait in a queue before the GPU starts, tokenization, which for voice includes speech recognition and speech synthesis, prefill and decode in the model, and voice activity detection, which finds where a speaker starts and stops. The first two are set mostly by where inference runs.

NVIDIA published an operator's benchmark of this. The same small voice model ran on 4 RTX PRO 6000 GPUs in total in each of two layouts: one central cluster, and a grid spread over 4 sites. Both took a burst of sessions that arrive together. The grid kept end to end latency inside 500 milliseconds from normal load to burst. Its throughput rose to 42,362 tokens per second at burst, 80.9% above its own baseline, while the central cluster lost throughput. Cost per token was 52.8% lower on the grid at baseline and 76.1% lower at burst.

The blog gives the reason. A central cluster spends much of its latency budget on the round trip, so it has to run its GPUs at lower utilization to keep slow responses rare. A grid site keeps the round trip short and can drive its GPUs harder at the same target. NVIDIA adds that results vary with model, workload and network.

Chapter 4 of 16

A control plane that reads the work

Orchestrator, global load balancer, LLM router

The reference design puts a control plane over the sites and gives it three working parts. The AI Grid Orchestrator keeps the state: which clusters are healthy, where capacity is free, and which pods, clusters and models are deployed where. When a node's heartbeat stops, it provisions the model somewhere else. The global server load balancer handles incoming requests and decides where each is sent. It may keep a ranking list built from criteria the provider sets, it looks at hardware signals such as connection counts and CPU or GPU utilization, and it can take periodic updates from the Orchestrator. The LLM router reads the request itself: context, complexity and metadata, and directs it to the most suitable model. It may also answer a repeated question from a semantic cache. NVIDIA Dynamo, which has its own lesson, serves the model behind these.

The design lists what this control plane routes on that a plain load balancer does not. Prompt awareness: simple interactions go to lighter nodes and complex reasoning to dense GPU clusters. Resource and utilization awareness: no node becomes a hotspot. Multi-tenancy: the grid is sliced, with guaranteed performance for high priority tenants. Data governance: residency and sovereignty rules are enforced at request time. Security: access control, and routing decisions that can be audited. It also steers requests to instances likely to hold their KV cache.

One limit follows from the earlier lesson. DNS never sees the prompt. Anything prompt-aware has to be decided at a tier that terminates the request.

F5's deck on BNK and NVIDIA draws the same control plane with four functions in place of three. The Orchestrator holds global cluster state, health, quotas, the mapping of models to sites, and lifecycle. Resource and Intent translates what a tenant asked for, its SLA, its region and its budget, into placement and into scaling out or in. Decision and Policy applies tenant priorities and data governance, and coordinates with software-defined networking (SDN) and with NVIDIA Cloud Functions (NVCF), the NVIDIA service that exposes inference endpoints and platform APIs. GSLB and LLM routers are the global way in, with prompt-aware and KV-cache-aware model selection. The two functions in the middle are the ones the reference design's three parts leave implicit. A tenant states a target: answers within a second, inside the EU, for at most a set amount each hour. Something has to turn that target into a number of replicas at a site, and something has to check that tenant's rules before any site is ranked.

The deck then splits the work into two tiers. Tier 1 is global site selection by F5 CNF DNS or GSLB. It decides which region, cluster or cloud serves a request, from policy (sovereignty, cost, tenancy), from AI telemetry (GPU load, queue depth, time to first token), and from round trip time and health. Tier 2 is local inference routing by BNK. It decides which GPU, model or Pod handles the request, from per-Pod GPU utilization, queue depth, KV cache locality, model affinity and priority class. Telemetry flows up from tier 2 to tier 1, and ratios or weights flow down. Governance sits above both as a hard constraint. In the deck's words, a request tagged EU-only cannot leave EU infrastructure, regardless of latency or cost.

Chapter 5 of 16

The edge-terminated design

Tiers stacked the way a CDN stacks them

The deck draws two ways to arrange these decisions across sites. The first is stratified, or hierarchical, and follows the pattern of a content delivery network: the request terminates at the edge. At the top is the far edge, which holds devices only. Below it is a tiny factory at an edge or radio site, with RTX servers and air cooling. Then come small factories, also RTX and air cooled. At the bottom is the AI factory, with HGX systems and liquid cooling. The reference design's hardware fits these tiers: RTX PRO Servers in 2U and 4U for regional hubs and edge sites, in racks under 15 kW or under 25 kW, and HGX B300 systems at the centre.

Every tier makes its decisions locally. In this design GSLB uses proximity and health only. Its answer moves when a site's health changes, and the deck notes that its policy changes only through configuration. The border of the edge tier does factory routing on model, complexity, latency and security. The borders of the lower tiers decide on model and security. At every tier the gateway decides tenancy and availability, and inference follows the endpoint picker's policy.

The intelligence sits in the border at the edge, because that is the first place that can read the prompt. A request the tiny factory cannot serve is passed down to a larger tier. So a long reasoning task still enters at the edge and crosses the tiers above the factory before it reaches a GPU that can run it. In the scene Madrid plays the edge tier and Frankfurt the factory.

Chapter 6 of 16

Racks a building has to be built around

Why power density and cooling decide what a site can be

The tiers of the stratified design are named for their cooling as much as for their GPUs: RTX and air cooled at the edge, HGX and liquid cooled at the factory. Power density is the reason. Every watt a server draws ends up as heat in the room, so a rack's electrical draw is also the heat the building must carry away. Uptime Institute's 2024 survey of data centre operators found racks of 4 to 6 kW still the most common. The average of typical racks across respondents was 8 kW, and 7.1 kW once 11 sites reporting more than 50 kW were set aside. Most facilities had no rack above 30 kW, and those that did had only a few.

AI hardware sits far above that. One NVIDIA DGX H100, a single 8U server with eight H100 GPUs, is rated at 10.2 kW maximum and has six 3.3 kW power supplies. That one server draws more than the average enterprise rack of 7.1 kW. A Vera Rubin NVL72 rack is allocated 227 kW at MaxP, which is 227 ÷ 7.1, about 32 average enterprise racks, in one rack position. Thaki Cloud's insights series (thakicloud.com/en/insights) states the consequence: modern AI racks run at a much higher power density than conventional enterprise racks, which makes power delivery and advanced cooling a core design constraint of an AI factory. The reference design's edge classes, under 15 kW and under 25 kW per rack, keep the tiny and small factories inside what a telco building's air handling can manage.

Air runs out first. The heat a coolant carries is its mass flow times its specific heat times the rise in its temperature. A cubic metre of air near room conditions holds about 1.2 kJ for each degree, and a cubic metre of water about 4,180 kJ, so water carries about 3,500 times as much heat per unit of volume. With air warming by 12°C across the rack, a 7.1 kW rack needs about 0.49 cubic metres of air each second, a 25 kW rack 1.73, and a 227 kW rack 15.7. Water warming by 10°C carries the same 227 kW at about 5.4 litres a second.

So the factory tier is liquid cooled. NVIDIA's MaxLPS documentation describes the chain. At the rack, a technology cooling system (TCS) is designed for liquid supplied at 45°C. A coolant distribution unit (CDU) moves that heat into a separate facility loop, which carries it to heat rejection equipment on site, such as dry coolers that give the heat to outside air. A separate, cooler air loop still serves the computer room air handlers (CRAHs) and air-cooled support equipment. Liquid at 45°C can often be cooled by outside air with little help, so less of the site's fixed power goes to cooling and more is left for GPUs. NVIDIA notes that this depends on climate: a cool site such as Ireland may need extra cooling only in the hottest part of the year, and an Arizona site for much of it. Power and cooling decide which tier a site can be before any routing decision is made.

Chapter 7 of 16

The factory-terminated design

Peers of different size, and a GSLB that knows more

The second design is isotropic, or peered. Every site is a full factory, the sites are peers, and a request terminates at the factory that will serve it. The deck draws three peers of 50, 200 and 500 megawatts and notes that the tenant has the same models deployed in all regions. Here those are Madrid, Amsterdam and Frankfurt, 750 megawatts together. Since any site can run any request, the first choice carries more weight, and GSLB is given more to decide with.

Its inputs in this design are health, capacity, the tenant's SLA, the energy a site spends per token in millijoules, the locational marginal price and the carbon footprint. The locational marginal price is the wholesale price of electricity at one point of the grid at one time, and it differs between places and hours. Below GSLB the tiers decide what they decided before: the border on model, complexity, latency and security, the gateway on tenancy and availability, inference by endpoint picker policy. The deck's summary is intelligent routing at all regions and all tiers through BIG-IP programmability.

F5 has a product at each tier. CNF DNS Services is GSLB. The provider border is named for NICo load balancing and DPF in zero trust mode, on BlueField DPUs that NVIDIA Infra Controller and DPF manage, the subject of another lesson. BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes is the tenant gateway. F5 EPP picks the replica. F5 Terg supplies the energy measure. A tenant SLA needs DNS to know the tenant, and the deck's example name carries the tenant and the region as labels.

Chapter 8 of 16

An agent's next turn

Four tiers, and each one has to be right

Now take one caller: a coding agent that works for tenant acme. An agent resends the whole conversation on every turn. The model server keeps a KV cache, the stored intermediate results for tokens it has already read, in GPU memory. On turn 12 the prompt is 60,000 tokens, of which 58,500 were in turn 11 and 1,500 are new. A replica that still holds the prefix reads 1,500 tokens. Any other replica reads all 60,000, forty times the work, before the first token comes out.

The deck states the goal as a question of which copy of this conversation's cache is the closest, and adds that persistence is hard. Underneath one request it counts 3 regions, 9 AI factories, border gateways in each region, a tenant VRF, which is a private routing table for one tenant, several tenant gateway nodes per region and several inference GPUs per tenant. Four things have to be right. The turn should land in the region that served the last one. It should be delivered securely to the tenant's cluster, with the endpoint picker that already placed the conversation. It should arrive through a DPU that is still up and still serves that tenant's hosts. It should reach the replica that still holds the prefix.

The first tier is the hard one for DNS. An answer lives 30 seconds and turns are minutes apart. A GSLB ranking on price alone could answer Madrid for turn 12. So where the conversation already is has to rank above price and carbon for this request.

Chapter 9 of 16

Inside the region

Border, tenant gateway, picker, replica

Inside Frankfurt the other three tiers take over. The first is the provider's border, which is shared by all tenants. Its job is to deliver the request into the right tenant's cluster and nowhere else, and it does that through the tenant VRF. The deck names this tier for NICo and DPF. NICo, the NVIDIA Infra Controller, is an open source set of microservices for zero trust lifecycle management of bare metal, and DPF, the DOCA Platform Framework, deploys and operates services on BlueField DPUs.

The next tier belongs to one tenant: its gateway, BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes. A region holds several gateway nodes for a tenant. The turn has to come in through a DPU that is still up and still serves that tenant's hosts. If the DPU that carried turn 11 has failed, another node takes the connection, and it has to ask the same endpoint picker, because the picker is what remembers where the conversation was placed.

The last tier is inference. F5 EPP knows which replica read this prefix last and whether that replica has room. If the blocks are still in GPU memory, the request goes there and prefill covers 1,500 tokens. If they were evicted to make room for other work, the picker chooses by load and the full 60,000 tokens are read again. The picker's wiring is covered in its own lesson. The four tiers multiply. A turn that reaches the right region, the right cluster and the right gateway and then the wrong replica costs the same as one that went to Madrid.

F5 stages this stack as one control hierarchy from region to replica, in which the same decision repeats at every level. At the region or AI factory, F5 CNF GSLB answers which site, and whether the data has to stay in the country. At the tenant cluster, BNK at the region's border answers which cluster and model pool serve a business unit. At the model replica, BNK with F5 Endpoint Picker answers which replica can answer this request soonest. F5's Distributed Inference deck marks the replica tier Available and the other two Future. Its advice is to land at the replica tier today and lock in the customer's requirements for the multi-cluster, multi-region roadmap. That order matches where the value is in this chapter: the replica decision is the one that turns 60,000 tokens of prefill into 1,500, and it works inside one cluster with nothing above it. The partnership timeline in F5's AI Grid session then places BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes support for DPF trusted mode in September 2026, NICo with DPF zero trust in December 2026, and a BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes AI Grid reference architecture in March 2027.

Chapter 10 of 16

The slot the reference architecture leaves open

NVIDIA defines the networks and leaves the tenant's door to partners

NVIDIA publishes reference architectures for its cloud partners, the NVIDIA Cloud Partners (NCPs) that rent GPU capacity to others. The NCP hardware reference design names four networks. The Tenant Access Network (TAN), also called north-south or front end, joins all parts of the data centre, and storage is its main user. The Secure Management Network (SMN) is the out-of-band network used to configure and manage the whole site. The Cluster Interconnect Network (CIN), also called east-west or scale out, joins the GPU racks for GPU-to-GPU traffic. NVLink is the scale-up domain inside one rack. The TAN and SMN are always Ethernet, the CIN is Ethernet or InfiniBand, and NVLink is NVIDIA's own.

NVIDIA's inference reference architecture for these partners says what the TAN has to provide: north-south API, user, storage, registry and shared-service access. Its guidance is to expose inference and platform APIs through tenant-aware ingress, gateway, rate-limit and identity boundaries. It stops short of choosing a product. Storage, gateway, registry, provisioning, identity and automation tools are recorded as partner-selected decisions outside the base reference architecture. F5's deck on BNK and NVIDIA puts it as a note on NVIDIA's documents: no vendor solutions are specified in the NCP reference architectures. Partners publish their own reference architectures beside them, for storage, for compute software and for networking. F5's is BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes for the NCP Common Networking reference architecture.

The tenant's access network is the slot BNK fills, and the owner's description of BNK as an AI platform puts it at each boundary the guidance names. At the provider border it runs on BlueField DPUs in NVIDIA's zero trust mode, managed out of band through DPF and NICo, with a VRF for each tenant in the fabric, and F5BigFwPolicy and F5BigDdosProfile objects for firewall and DDoS protection. As the tenant gateway it is a VXLAN tunnel endpoint on BGP EVPN, with a VRF and a VXLAN network identifier (VNI) for each tenant, its own egress addresses, API keys, token budgets and model routing. For inference, F5 EPP chooses the replica. The same TMM runs on the node's CPU or on the DPU.

The control path and the request path stay apart. The provider's console turns an order into Kubernetes objects, a controller turns those into TMM configuration, and only TMM is in the path of a request. BNK lives on the TAN. The CIN, where collective operations and KV cache transfers run between GPUs, stays NVIDIA's fabric, and NVLink stays inside the rack.

Chapter 11 of 16

The telco edge speaks other protocols

Tunnels, subscriber sessions, policy and zero-rating

An AI Grid's smallest sites sit in a telco's buildings, at radio sites and central offices. Traffic there is a mobile network's traffic. It arrives inside tunnels, the caller is a subscriber record, and what the caller pays depends on rules the operator sets for each subscriber. The owner's list of what BNK carries includes a service provider toolkit for this edge: deep packet inspection (DPI), policy enforcement and zero-rating, with the protocols Diameter, GTP, SIP, NGAP and RADIUS. F5's documentation places it. BNK on Host, the deployment formerly called Service Proxy for Kubernetes, runs TMM as a software Pod on the host CPU and targets 5G and communication service provider workloads with SCTP, NGAP, Diameter and GTP. BNK on DPU targets AI traffic. Both use the same TMM and the same custom resources.

In a 5G network the base station, the gNB, talks to the core in two ways. Its control signalling goes to the Access and Mobility Management Function (AMF) as NGAP, the NG Application Protocol, carried over SCTP on port 38412 with payload protocol identifier 60. The phone's own IP packets go to the User Plane Function (UPF) inside GTP-U, the GPRS Tunnelling Protocol for user data, on UDP port 2152. Each end of a tunnel is named by a Tunnel Endpoint Identifier (TEID). The receiving end picks the TEID the sender must use, and the two ends exchange them in control plane messages. The inner packet keeps the phone's address and the outer header carries the addresses of the base station and the UPF. GTP-C, the control version used in 4G cores, uses UDP port 2123.

A subscriber session ties traffic to a person and a plan. In F5's documentation a RADIUS Accounting-Request with Acct-Status-Type Start, value 1, from the network access server creates the session. Interim-Update, value 3, refreshes it and Stop, value 2, ends it. RADIUS authentication uses UDP port 1812 and accounting 1813. A subscriber is known by IP address, IMSI, MSISDN or RADIUS attributes. Policy then comes over Gx, the Diameter interface between the Policy and Charging Rules Function (PCRF) and the Policy and Charging Enforcement Function (PCEF), which here is TMM. Diameter runs on port 3868 over TCP or SCTP. When the session starts, the enforcement module sends a Credit-Control-Request with CC-Request-Type 1 (CCR-I, command code 272) and gets back a CCA-I carrying policy and charging control (PCC) rules. The rules are either sent in full or name rules already configured on the PCEF. Each packet is then checked against the rules' service data flow filters in order of precedence.

DPI is what lets those rules name applications. F5's Policy Enforcer classifies flows by application and URL category and then acts: DSCP marking for quality of service, redirection, TCP optimization, UDP rate pacing, flow filtering and reporting. Charging goes over Gy to the online charging system, where usage is counted per rating group, carried in the Rating-Group AVP, code 432. F5 calls a rating group a quota bucket. Zero-rating means the traffic of a chosen service does not draw down the subscriber's data allowance. DPI puts that service's flows in their own rating group, and the operator rates that group at zero, so the subscriber's main bucket is untouched. RFC 8506 even names zero-rated services as what a subscriber may still reach after credit runs out, through filter rules in the answer. For a telco selling tokens, its own AI assistant served from the edge site is the obvious candidate.

SIP, the Session Initiation Protocol, sets up voice and video calls on port 5060. F5's CNF documentation configures it in an F5SipSetting resource on the Message Routing Framework, with persistence keyed on Call-ID, the client address or a custom value, Via header insertion and loop detection. A voice AI agent answering calls needs every message of one call to reach the same server, which is what Call-ID persistence does, and it needs the 500 millisecond budget the voice benchmark set.

Chapter 12 of 16

From owned sites to a service

What a telco adds before its GPUs become a product

Thaki Cloud sells software for running a GPU cloud, Thaki NeoCloud OS, so its insights series (thakicloud.com/en/insights) is a vendor's view, and the list below matches what its product offers. It still makes a point about telcos that routing alone does not settle. A telco may already run data centres, networks, enterprise service delivery, regional or edge sites, and GPU infrastructure. Those assets shorten the distance to an AI cloud, and the layer that is often missing is the service operating model. The transition it describes has three steps: infrastructure, then a product, then a self-service, on-demand cloud service. A GPU inventory becomes a cloud business only when customers can consume it again and again as a defined service.

Nine things are typically added on top of the hardware: a service catalogue with product definitions, customer, organisation and identity management, policy and quota, capacity and reservation logic, provisioning, service state, usage metering with billing integration, lifecycle recovery, and support with observability. Dedicated hardware still needs most of them. A customer with its own GPU servers still needs separate identity, credentials, network, storage, quotas, service records and billing records, because dedicated hardware and multi-tenant operation are different problems. For a telco with central, regional and edge sites, the advantage comes from running them through one service and policy model, with placement decided by latency, data location, capacity, network conditions and customer policy.

Two measurements are easy to confuse. GPU telemetry shows how a GPU is running. Commercial metering records who received which resource, for how long, under which product and contract, and a bill is built from that record. Recovery matters as much. When a service ends, credentials, network, storage, data and server state may have to be cleaned before the capacity returns to the pool, and that recycle time reduces what can be sold. Some answers are short. Hosting and on-demand products can coexist. A telco AI cloud does not have to include edge AI. Dedicated bare metal can be sold as a cloud service. Sovereign AI now covers control of data, operations, infrastructure, administration and legal jurisdiction, so a regional operator such as a telco has a part in it, though local hosting alone does not make a service sovereign.

Two operators show where the market is heading in 2026, and neither is a blueprint for every telco. On 2 March 2026 SoftBank announced its Telco AI Cloud vision. It joins large AI data centres on a GPU cloud, mainly for training, an AI-RAN-based multi-access edge computing (MEC) platform with an orchestrator for low-latency inference, and a software stack that manages both. AI-RAN shares accelerated computing between the radio access network's own processing and AI work, and MEC places compute at the edge of the mobile network. On 7 June 2026 NVIDIA and SK Telecom announced that SK Telecom plans a gigawatt-scale AI Cloud in Korea on the NVIDIA DSX platform, with its first AI factory planned to come online in 2027, for sovereign, physical and agentic AI, and that SK Telecom will become an NVIDIA Cloud Partner.

Chapter 13 of 16

What DSX is for

A site's megawatts were treated as fixed. They are not

Every chapter so far treated a site's capacity as given. NVIDIA DSX is about where that capacity comes from. NVIDIA describes DSX as a platform of reference designs, APIs, software libraries and technologies to design, simulate, build and operate AI factories. The deck's one line is that DSX puts every layer of the factory under software control, from the reference design to the grid connection, with tokens per megawatt as the objective.

The deck lists four groups of problems behind it. Design: every operator worked out compute, fabric, power and cooling from scratch, which at gigawatt scale is an expensive mistake waiting to happen, and thermal and electrical errors were found after the concrete was poured. Power: static provisioning strands capacity, because sizing every rack for its nameplate peak, called MaxP, wastes the site's envelope. The power grid sees AI as a hostile load, and resistance to granting an interconnect delayed new builds.

Operations: IT and OT did not speak. OT, operational technology, is the building management system, cooling and power, and it lived apart from the software stack. Fleet work was done by hand: provisioning and tenancy, configuration drift and fault remediation were home grown and costly to keep. Multi-tenancy on bare metal was immature. Ecosystem: OT vendor products are very expensive, integrators who know real data centre automation are scarce, and NVIDIA's cloud partners want open parts they can adopt one at a time. Hyperscalers spent decades and millions on these problems.

Chapter 14 of 16

Design, simulate, deploy

The layers of DSX and what is in each

The deck lays DSX out in three layers. Design is a set of validated reference designs to rack a factory: GB300 NVL72, HGX B300 and Vera Rubin NVL72 systems, and rack classes under 15 kW, under 25 kW and over 25 kW. Simulate is NVIDIA Omniverse digital twins of the site before it is built: air cooling, water cooling, power distribution, the power grid interconnect, and the network, with a Clos fabric north-south and InfiniBand or Ultra Ethernet east-west. NVIDIA's public blueprint for this contains the geometry of a 50 acre reference site, a fluid dynamics simulation of hot aisles and an electrical loading simulation.

Deploy is software in five groups. Infrastructure and tenancy is NICo, which provisions bare metal and manages its lifecycle for many tenants. Runtime consistency is AICR, the AI Cluster Runtime, which generates validated, version-locked configuration for GPU Kubernetes clusters. Health and resiliency is NVSentinel, which detects GPU faults, cordons the node, drains it and starts repair without a person, together with Fleet Intelligence, which tracks inventory, integrity and health of the GPU fleet. Workload serving is Dynamo for inference, and Run:ai or NVIDIA Cloud Functions to share GPUs and scale workloads across clusters. Power and grid is MaxLPS with Dynamic Power Software, DSX Flex and DSX Exchange.

The deck's diagram stacks the same parts from the ground up: the facility, the racks, the DSX OS control layer, the tenant clusters, and on top Fleet Intelligence, NICo's API and the AI Grid control plane. That top line is where one site joins the grid.

Chapter 15 of 16

Power the software can move

MaxP, MaxQ, MaxLPS and a target from the grid

The power group is what turns megawatts into something software can move. NVIDIA's documents start with two static settings. MaxP is the highest GPU power setting, and rack and row power and cooling are designed for it. MaxQ is the setting with the best performance per watt. It caps the GPU below MaxP, and on its own it coordinates nothing across the factory. A Vera Rubin rack is allocated 227 kW at MaxP. That figure is a planning number, and observed draw sits below it most of the time, because tenants and workloads do not all peak together. The gap is stranded power.

DSX MaxLPS manages that gap inside a fixed site envelope. It has three parts: a facility designed for liquid cooling supplied at 45°C, which lowers cooling overhead where the climate allows, Dynamic Power Software, and techniques that raise performance per watt. DPS runs a loop every few seconds. It reads GPU and rack power telemetry, finds unused allocation, and sets a power limit for each GPU at the server's BMC, its management controller, over Redfish. Spikes of milliseconds are left to hardware and electrical protection. DPS manages power and leaves job placement to the scheduler. NVIDIA puts the gain at up to 40% more GPUs in the same envelope.

DSX Flex adds the grid. A grid-facing provider publishes a load target for a period, DSX Exchange carries it as an event, and DPS brings the managed GPUs under it. The site reports its state back as a stream of snapshots. A site that can do this is easier for a utility to approve and connect.

Terg: energy per token, per tenant

What power policy lacks, and what routing gains

One thing is missing from that loop. DPS sees GPUs, racks and groups. A load target names kilowatts on a feed. Neither knows whose tokens a watt is producing. The deck says it directly: DSX has no way to base power changes on tenancy, and its tokens per megawatt objective is priced but never measured.

F5 Terg fills the gap. It joins three things into one measurable unit: energy in millijoules, tokens, and the state of the silicon. The result is millijoules per token, known per tenant. The unit converts cleanly. One megawatt is a billion millijoules each second, so tokens per second per megawatt is a billion divided by the millijoules per token. At 250 mJ per token a megawatt yields 4.0 million tokens per second, and at 400 mJ it yields 2.5 million. The deck adds that Terg sees processes bound to the host, which the GPU's own management view misses, and that it plugs into DSX Exchange natively over NATS and MQTT.

With that number, energy policy can know tenants, and power policy can include a tenant's SLA. Take a target that cuts a Frankfurt feed from 860 kW to 500 kW. A cut spread evenly slows acme's coding agents as much as a batch tenant. A tenant-aware cut takes the 360 kW from the batch tenant and leaves acme whole. The same number travels up to GSLB as a site's energy per token. New conversations then lean toward the site that wins when energy per token, price and carbon are weighed together, and that need not be the site with the lowest millijoules per token. The agent's next turn, turn 13, still goes back to its cache.

What to keep

Site, tier, replica, and the power under them

An AI Grid makes many sites behave as one inference platform, and that takes more than a DNS answer by distance and health. The site is chosen with the tenant, the model's location, energy price and carbon in view. The tier that reads the prompt, the border, decides what DNS cannot. For an agent's next turn the region, the tenant's cluster and its endpoint picker, and the replica all have to be the ones that served the last turn, or the cache is lost. The gateway node only has to be one whose DPU is up and serves the tenant's hosts. If it fails, another node takes the connection and asks the same endpoint picker, so the cache survives. Under all of it, DSX makes a site's power something software sets, and F5 Terg measures what that power yields in tokens for each tenant. Routing and power policy then work from the same number. Where to start follows F5's staging: the replica tier, BNK with F5 Endpoint Picker, is available now, so a provider lands there and grows to the cluster and region tiers as they arrive. A telco brings sites with power, buildings and subscribers already in place, the tunnels and subscriber policy of its edge, and NVIDIA's open slot on the tenant access network for BNK to fill. It still has to add a service operating model before those sites sell tokens.
