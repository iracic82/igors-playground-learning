Operations

Keeping GPUs busy and healthy

Utilization, memory, ECC and the two Xid codes worth an alert

Running web servers teaches you to chase availability and keep spare machines around. GPUs turn that upside down. One costs so much that an idle hour is the expensive failure, and the number that pays the bill is how busy the fleet is. At the same time a GPU fails in ways a web server does not. Its memory develops faults, it overheats and slows down, and now and then it vanishes from the server it is plugged into. It reports all of this, in numbers and codes that mean nothing until you know them. This lesson goes through the handful worth knowing, on gpu-2 in the factory: what the utilization gauges really count, which memory number moves, how heat shows up, how corrected and uncorrected memory errors are reported, how to read an Xid line, what Kubernetes does with one, and what the factory loses when a worker goes. It also covers what owning a GPU costs against renting one, how routing that ignores load leaves some GPUs throttling while others idle, how the memory ceiling leads teams to buy GPUs they cannot fill, and the scorecard a factory is judged by.

Chapter 1 of 15

An idle GPU costs the same

Utilization is the number that pays the bill

Start with money. The talk this lesson follows, an O'Reilly primer on running your own AI infrastructure, puts an H100 at 2 to 4 dollars an hour on demand, and 1 to 2 dollars on spot capacity. Spot is 50 to 70 percent cheaper and can be taken back at short notice, which suits work that saves checkpoints. Every one of those prices is charged whether the GPU works or waits.

Take 3 dollars an hour. At full use an hour of useful work costs 3 dollars. At 70 percent use it costs 3 divided by 0.7, which is 4.29. At half use it costs 6, because you paid for two hours to get one. At a quarter it costs 12. A month is about 730 hours, so one GPU at 3 dollars costs about 2,190 dollars a month busy or not, and the four GPUs of gpu-node-2 cost 12 dollars every hour the node is up.

Buying the hardware changes the sums and not the logic. By the same talk, owning pays off at around 70 percent sustained use over three years. So the goal of a GPU platform is different from the goal of a web platform. The web habit is 99.99 percent availability with spare machines idling for safety. The talk's number for busy comes from training: there, 70 percent is a good figure, because jobs stop and start while models are changed and readied for the next run. This lesson borrows it as the target for the inference pool as well. The machines you pay for should be busy about 70 percent of the time, and spare capacity is the expensive thing. Everything in the factory that sends a request to the right worker is in service of that number. The rest of this lesson is about measuring busy honestly and about the failures that take a worker out of the count.

Chapter 2 of 15

Owning the hours or renting them

The hourly price is one term of a longer sum

The talk's 70 percent from the last chapter is a break-even between two ways of paying for GPU hours, and the comparison behind it is easy to get wrong. A rented GPU is priced by the hour, so it is tempting to set that hour beside the purchase price of a card divided by its hours of life. Thaki Cloud's insights series (thakicloud.com/en/insights) warns against exactly that: do not compare only hourly GPU price. It gives two fuller sums instead. For owned, on-premises capacity: CapEx + utilization + operations + software + data movement + lifecycle. For public cloud: workload duration + utilization + operations + data movement + software + support.

Take the owned side term by term. CapEx, capital expenditure, is the purchase: GPUs, the servers around them, networking and storage. Operations is everything that keeps them running: the data center space, power and cooling, and the staff who replace failed parts, update drivers and plan capacity. Software is the platform on top. Lifecycle is the hardware ageing out: repairs, replacement and upgrades on a schedule of its own. Utilization sits in the middle because it divides all of the others. The owned GPU costs the same in every hour of its life, so the cost of a useful hour is the whole bill divided by the hours that did work, the same arithmetic as the 4.29 and 6.00 of the last chapter.

The rented side has its own traps. The series names the public cloud's cost drivers: long-running GPUs, data transfer, storage, managed services and premium support. Long-running GPUs matter most for this factory. Renting is cheap for work that ends. A fine-tuning run on 8 GPUs for two weeks is 8 × 336 hours × 3 dollars = 8,064 dollars, and then the bill stops. A model server has no end. The qwen3 Deployment keeps Pods qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-x7k2p and qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-m9q4t up day and night, so their GPUs are rented every hour, busy or not. Over three years, 26,280 hours, one GPU at 3 dollars an hour costs 78,840 dollars, and the four of gpu-node-2 cost 315,360. The utilization term only lowers a rented bill when capacity is actually released in quiet hours.

Data movement belongs on both sides. Every new Pod loads 34.3 GB of Qwen3-32B-FP8 weights, the BF16 build would be 65.5 GB, and checkpoints, datasets and logs move too. In a public cloud, transfer out of a region and the storage that holds those copies are billed lines. On premises they cost network and storage capacity that was bought up front.

The 70 percent is the O'Reilly talk's rule of thumb: on-prem starts to pay off at about 70 percent sustained utilization, over about three years. The series gives no break-even figure of its own. Its sums are how to test the talk's number for one fleet. Renting only the busy hours at 70 percent use for three years costs 0.7 × 26,280 × 3 = 55,188 dollars per GPU. Owning wins when its whole three-year bill per GPU, all six terms, comes in below the rented bill for the same work. The owned card is billed for every hour of its life, so at low use most of its bill pays for empty hours. As use rises, the owned cost of a useful hour falls, and at some level of use it drops below the rented price of that hour. Where that happens depends on the purchase price, the power bill and the rented rate. On-demand, reserved, subscription and contract prices all move that line, which is why the series insists on the whole sum.

Chapter 3 of 15

What the utilization gauge measures

Busy some of the time is not busy

Go inside the server. The number everyone looks at first is GPU utilization, and it is easy to misread. NVIDIA defines it as the percent of time, over the last sample period, during which one or more kernels was running on the GPU. A kernel is one program launched on the GPU's cores. The definition counts time and nothing else. It says nothing about how many of the 132 streaming multiprocessors, the SMs, that kernel used, or how hard. The GPUs in this lesson are H100 SXM modules, joined by NVLink, which have 132 SMs. The H100 PCIe card has 114.

So one small request trickling tokens keeps the gauge near 100 percent while most of the cores idle. A server that answers a single user and a server that answers fifty in one batch can show the same 0.97. Batching is exactly what makes the second one cheap per token, and this gauge cannot see it.

A second gauge, memory utilization, is the percent of time device memory was being read or written. For decode, where each new token needs the whole model read from memory once, that one is closer to the truth, and it has the same blind spot: time, not amount.

Neither number comes from the application. On each node a collector, the DCGM exporter, reads them through NVIDIA's management library from the driver, and the driver asks the GPU. Prometheus then scrapes the exporter. Its default list exposes them as DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL_RATIO and DCGM_FI_DEV_MEMORY_UTIL_RATIO, alongside temperatures, power, clocks, framebuffer memory, PCIe replays, the last Xid and the row remap counters. DCGM is NVIDIA's Data Center GPU Manager, and the exporter is the usual first step in GPU observability on Kubernetes.

Chapter 4 of 15

Looking inside the busy time

Profiling counters count cycles and cores

DCGM also reads the GPU's profiling counters, which measure cycles and cores where the utilization gauge measures only time. Each is an average over the sampling interval, between 0 and 1.

SM activity, field 1002, is the fraction of time at least one warp was active on a multiprocessor, averaged over all multiprocessors. A warp is a group of 32 threads that run together, and a warp waiting on memory still counts as active. NVIDIA's own example: a kernel that uses a fifth of the SMs for the whole interval reads 0.2, and so does a kernel that uses all of them for a fifth of the interval. On gpu-2, a kernel that keeps 33 of the 132 SMs busy for a whole interval reads 33 divided by 132, which is 0.25, while GPU utilization reads 1.0. NVIDIA calls 0.8 or more necessary, though not enough on its own, for using a GPU well, and below 0.5 likely poor use.

SM occupancy, field 1003, is how many warps sit on a multiprocessor compared with the most it can hold. Higher is not always better, and it matters most for work limited by memory bandwidth. Tensor activity, field 1004, is the fraction of cycles the tensor pipe was busy, where 1 means a tensor instruction every other cycle. DRAM activity, field 1005, is the fraction of cycles data went to or from device memory, and about 0.8 is the most that can be reached in practice.

The exporter's default list switches on graphics engine activity, tensor activity, DRAM activity and PCIe bytes sent and received. SM activity and SM occupancy are in the list, commented out, until you enable them. For a decode-heavy model server, DRAM activity near its practical ceiling is the sign of a GPU doing the job it was bought for.

Chapter 5 of 15

Memory is a wall

And the memory gauge is the wrong one to watch

A server that runs short of memory pages to disk and gets slow. A GPU's memory has no such escape. Its 80 GB is all there is, and a model or a batch that needs more than is free does not fit. System memory cannot stand in for it, because of bandwidth: the O'Reilly talk puts regular DDR5 at about 50 GB/s, close to what one DDR5-6400 channel carries (51.2 GB/s), against more than 3 TB/s for the GPU's HBM. So GPU memory is the hard limit that sizes everything: which models fit, and how many conversations run at once. By the same talk's rule of 2 bytes per parameter at 16-bit precision, a 70-billion-parameter model needs 140 GB, two H100s, before any cache.

It would seem natural to alert on memory used. For a model server that tells you nothing. vLLM claims its share of GPU memory when it starts. Its gpu_memory_utilization setting defaults to 0.9, and 0.9 of 80 GB is 72 GB. The model here is Qwen3-32B in its FP8 build, Qwen3-32B-FP8, which stores most of its 32.8 billion parameters in 1 byte and keeps some layers at higher precision, so its weight files take 34.3 GB of that. The 16-bit BF16 build, at 2 bytes per parameter, is 65.5 GB of files, which would leave only about 6.5 GB. With FP8, most of the 37.7 GB left becomes the KV cache, the stored keys and values of every token in every running conversation. The framebuffer gauge therefore reads about 72 GB at midnight with no traffic and at noon under full load.

The number that moves is the one vLLM reports itself. vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc is the share of the KV cache in use, where 1 means 100 percent. Next to it, vllm:num_requests_running counts requests in the current batch and vllm:num_requests_waiting counts those queued for room. When the cache is full, vLLM has to preempt running requests to make space, and vllm:num_preemptions_total rises. Those three numbers are also what the endpoint picker reads when it scores gpu-2.

That ceiling is also where a lot of GPU money goes. Count it out with this lesson's sizes. Every token a conversation holds costs 262,144 bytes of KV cache in Qwen3-32B: 64 layers × 2 for keys and values × 8 KV heads × 128 numbers × 2 bytes. The 37.7 GB that the FP8 build leaves holds at most 143,814 tokens, before working memory takes its share. At 8,000 tokens a conversation that is 17 conversations at once. The BF16 build leaves about 6.5 GB, which holds 24,795 tokens, or 3 such conversations. Either way the cores are far from full. Each decode step reads all the weights from memory to produce one token for each conversation in the batch, so a batch of 3 or 17 leaves most of the arithmetic units waiting.

Then the server is full. New users wait in the queue, or vLLM preempts a running conversation to make room. The cores still have time to spare and the memory has no room. The reflex is to add GPUs, and each one brings another 80 GB and another full set of cores that the same ceiling keeps half idle. Serving 51 conversations of 8,000 tokens takes at least 3 GPUs with the FP8 build and at least 17 with the BF16 build, for the same answers. The KodeKloud course AI Infrastructure Explained, which this site's serving lessons draw on, says it plainly: memory decides how many people one GPU can serve, teams end up buying far more GPUs than the math calls for, and those expensive GPUs sit half idle. At 3 dollars an hour, the 14 extra GPUs of the BF16 plan cost 42 dollars every hour, about 30,660 dollars a month, and lift none of the work. Before buying, free memory first: a smaller weight build, or an FP8 KV cache through vLLM's --kv-cache-dtype fp8, which halves the cost of a token to 131,072 bytes and doubles the room, at an accuracy cost that has to be measured. The signature of a fleet that is memory bound is vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc at or near 1 while DCGM_FI_PROF_SM_UTIL_RATIO stays low.

Chapter 6 of 15

Heat, power and clocks

A GPU that slows down raises no error

A GPU protects itself by slowing down. It carries many temperature sensors, between 10 and 70 by the talk's count, and it watches its own power draw. When it runs too hot or draws too much, it lowers its clocks. Nothing crashes, nothing is logged as an error, and every request on that GPU simply takes longer.

nvidia-smi names the reasons a clock is held down. SW Power Cap means the power scaling algorithm is pulling clocks below the requested value because the GPU is drawing too much power. SW Thermal Slowdown means the clocks are capped because the temperature is above the maximum operating temperature. HW Slowdown is a hardware brake that cuts the core clocks by a factor of 2 or more. Idle means nothing is running and the clocks are dropping on purpose, which is normal.

The DCGM exporter's default list carries the raw inputs: DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_TEMP_CELSIUS, DCGM_FI_DEV_MEMORY_TEMP_CELSIUS for the HBM, DCGM_FI_DEV_BOARD_POWER_WATTS, DCGM_FI_DEV_SM_CLOCK_HERTZ and DCGM_FI_DEV_MEMORY_CLOCK_HERTZ. Counters of seconds spent below the application clocks, split by cause, sit in the same file commented out: DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_POWER_THROTTLE_SECONDS_TOTAL, the thermal one and others. Turning on the thermal and power ones costs nothing and answers the question the gauges cannot: is this worker slow because of its work, or because of its cooling.

This matters to routing as much as to health. A worker whose clock was halved drains its queue at half the rate. A picker that scores by how fast a queue drains sees that directly, and sends it less, even though every health check on the node is green.

Chapter 7 of 15

Hotspots that load-blind routing makes

Some GPUs throttle while others idle, on the same bill

The previous chapter followed one GPU that runs hot. Across a pool, the usual cause is the router. Inference is uneven and bursty. F5's deck on its scorers gives the example: a 100-token "hi" and a summary request of 128,000 tokens arrive through the same API. The second carries 1,280 times as many tokens, and its prefill takes more than 1,280 times the work, because attention compares each token with every token before it. Round robin and least connections count requests. They have no view of how many tokens each request carries, how full a worker's KV cache is or how long its queue has grown.

So the work lands unevenly. A worker that draws a few long prompts runs its cores flat out. Its temperature and power draw rise, and the GPU pulls its clocks down as the previous chapter showed. Now it drains its queue more slowly. A load-blind router keeps sending it its equal share, so the queue grows, the batch stays full and the card stays hot. The workers that drew short requests finish early and sit idle. The cluster pays for all of them every hour.

F5's deck on BNK and NVIDIA draws this as two pictures of the same four GPUs. Under standard traffic routing, two GPUs run at 97 and 95 percent and throttle, while the other two idle at 10 and 8 percent. The deck shows a figure it calls cluster yield, without defining it further. Here it is 62 percent, which the deck labels stranded capital. Under inference-aware traffic control from the F5 AI Service Plane, the same four run at 62, 64, 65 and 68 percent, and the cluster yield is 98 percent, which the deck labels max aggregate throughput.

The averages explain why utilization alone hides this. The four GPUs on the left average 52.5 percent busy, those on the right 64.75 percent, a gap of about 12 points. The yield differs by 36. A throttled GPU reports itself busy for every second it runs, at a lower clock, so its busy time is worth less work, and an idle one produces nothing. Balanced, every card runs at full clock and below its limits, close to the 70 percent target of the first chapter.

Inference-aware routing is the endpoint picker. For each request the data plane asks it which worker to use, and it scores the candidates from their own reports: vllm:num_requests_waiting through a queue scorer, vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc through a KV cache utilization scorer, and which worker already holds the prompt's prefix. In the upstream Gateway API Inference Extension these are queue-scorer, kv-cache-utilization-scorer and prefix-cache-scorer, and F5's picker adds its own, such as token-weighted scoring, which measures each request in tokens with a tokenizer inside the picker. A throttled worker drains its queue more slowly, so its queue score falls, and new work goes to a worker with room before any health check turns red.

Chapter 8 of 15

Errors that are corrected, for now

Single-bit faults are a trend, not an incident

GPU memory is protected by an error-correcting code, ECC. When one bit of a stored value flips, the code notices, repairs the value on the way out and adds one to a counter. Nothing breaks and no application sees it. The counter is still worth watching, because of what a rising count says about that memory. The talk calls single-bit counts a degradation signal: memory that produces more and more of them is wearing out.

DCGM keeps two kinds of count. Volatile counts, such as DCGM_FI_DEV_ECC_SBE_VOL_TOTAL, start again from zero when the driver reloads. Aggregate counts, such as DCGM_FI_DEV_ECC_SBE_AGG_TOTAL, persist for the life of the GPU. All four ECC counters are in the exporter's default file, commented out, so they report nothing until someone enables them.

The GPU also repairs itself. Since the Ampere generation, which includes the H100, every bank of GPU memory has spare rows, and the row remapper swaps a degrading row for a spare one in hardware. It replaces the older scheme of retiring pages, which allowed at most 64 retirements. Row remapping allows up to 512 remappings for the whole memory. The spare rows are reserved per bank, and when a bank's reserved rows run out, a remap for an uncorrectable error can take the place of one made for correctable errors. A remap only takes effect after a GPU reset, and until then nvidia-smi -q shows Pending: Yes under Remapped Rows, next to the counts of rows remapped for correctable and for uncorrectable errors and a Remapping Failure Occurred flag. The exporter carries the counts and the failure flag by default. A GPU that is steadily using up its spares is one to move work away from on your own schedule, before it chooses the moment for you.

Chapter 9 of 15

Xid 48: two bits at once

The error the code can detect and cannot repair

The code can repair one flipped bit in a value. Two in the same value it can only detect. The data is wrong and the GPU knows it, so it does the one safe thing and stops the work that owned that memory. The driver writes a line to the kernel log that carries a number: Xid 48, a double-bit ECC error. An Xid is the NVIDIA driver's error code, and the kernel log is where they all surface. NVIDIA's catalog says the error is also reported back to the application, and that if the condition persists or recurs, the next step is to contact support. For both of its actions the catalog names WORKFLOW_XID_DBE, NVIDIA's guidance class for double-bit errors, which leads on to Xid 94 or 95.

On an H100 the damage is usually smaller than that sounds, because of error containment, added with the Ampere generation. When the driver can contain the error to the process that touched the memory, it logs Xid 94 alongside the 48, and only that process is ended. Its recommended action is to restart the application. Other processes on the GPU keep running and new ones can start. When containment fails, it logs Xid 95 instead, more than one application may be affected, and the GPU must be reset. In both cases dynamic page offlining marks the page with the bad memory as unusable, so no new allocation lands on it, and the row remapper queues a spare row. The catalog lists Xid 63 for a remap entry recorded successfully and Xid 64 for a failure to record one, which calls for a reset and a support case.

For a model server, either way, the vLLM process on gpu-2 dies with whatever it was answering. The node is not necessarily lost. The page is fenced off now, and at the next planned reset the row is replaced and the page comes back.

Chapter 10 of 15

Reading an Xid line

Find the code, then look it up

Every Xid goes to the kernel log buffer, and from there to the journal or to files such as /var/log/messages or /var/log/syslog, depending on the distribution. Searching for the string NVRM: Xid finds them all. The first one in a log is preceded by a line naming the GPU at that PCI address and its GUID, an identifier that never changes for that card. Each Xid line then carries the PCI bus, device and function, the code after a colon, and details that depend on the code. NVIDIA's own example reads NVRM: Xid (PCI:0000:5a:00): 79, GPU has fallen off the bus.

NVIDIA publishes a catalog of Xids for Ampere and newer GPUs. For each code it gives a description, whether it applies to A100, to the H-x00, B-x00 and GB-x00 families, and to Rubin, and two actions. The immediate action, the Tenant In-Operation Action column, is meant to be automated, to get the machine back into service. It is one of a set of guidance classes, such as RESTART_APP, RESET_GPU, RESET_GPU_DEFERRED for a reset at the next maintenance window, RESTART_BM for a bare-metal restart, CONTACT_SUPPORT or IGNORE, and for some codes a named workflow such as WORKFLOW_XID_DBE. The investigatory action is for when the code keeps coming back, and often reads CONTACT_SUPPORT or CHECK_APP/CUDA.

Many codes are the application's fault and say nothing about the hardware. Xid 13 is usually a program reading past the end of an array. Xid 31 is a memory page fault, usually a bug in the program. Xid 43 means a program hit a fault and had to stop, and the GPU stays healthy. That is why an alert on every Xid is noise, and an alert on the hardware codes this lesson follows, 48, 79, 94 and 95, is signal.

The exporter's DCGM_FI_DEV_XID_ERROR holds only the last code seen, so two Xids between scrapes show as one. The same file lists DCGM_EXP_XID_ERRORS_COUNT and DCGM_EXP_XID_ERRORS_TOTAL, commented out, which count them. When NVIDIA support is needed, nvidia-bug-report.sh, run as root, collects the logs into nvidia-bug-report.log.gz.

Chapter 11 of 15

Xid 79: the GPU fell off the bus

The link itself is gone

The other code to know is worse. Everything the host does with a GPU goes over one PCIe link: every command, every doorbell, every read of a counter. Xid 79 means the driver tried to reach the GPU over that link and found it was not accessible. The card is still in the slot. NVIDIA's catalog says the event is often caused by hardware failures on the PCI Express link, which bring the link down and leave the GPU unreachable.

From the host's side a read of the GPU's registers now comes back as all ones, 0xFFFFFFFF, the value a PCIe read returns when no device answers. Nothing running on that GPU can continue, and the process that was using it cannot bring it back. The catalog gives CONTACT_SUPPORT as both its immediate and its investigatory action. There is no automated fix listed for it, because the card, its slot or the link between them is suspect. While support looks at it, the operator drains gpu-node-2 so no new work lands there, and that takes all four of its GPUs out of service.

There is sometimes an earlier hint. PCIe protects every packet with a link-level CRC, and a packet that arrives damaged is sent again. DCGM_FI_DEV_PCIE_REPLAY_TOTAL, in the exporter's default list, counts those resends. A count that keeps climbing on one GPU while its neighbours stay flat says that link is struggling before it fails. The same file lists the current link generation and width, commented out. A card that trained at fewer lanes than its slot has is moving data more slowly every second, without any error at all.

Chapter 12 of 15

What Kubernetes does with an Xid

The device plugin marks the GPU unhealthy

Kubernetes learns about GPUs from the NVIDIA device plugin, which runs on each GPU node and advertises the resource nvidia.com/gpu. gpu-node-2 advertises 4. The plugin also checks health. It asks NVIDIA's management library for critical Xid events on every GPU and waits for them.

When an event arrives it checks the code against a list of Xids that are the application's fault and leave the GPU healthy: 13, 31, 32, 43, 45, 68 and 69. Those are skipped. Any other code, which includes 48, 79, 94 and 95, marks that GPU unhealthy. If the plugin cannot tell which GPU the event came from, it marks every GPU on the node unhealthy. Two environment variables change the list. DP_DISABLE_HEALTHCHECKS takes a comma-separated list of extra Xids to ignore, or all to switch the checks off. DP_ENABLE_HEALTHCHECKS names Xids to check even when they were disabled.

An unhealthy device is reported to the kubelet, and the node's allocatable count of nvidia.com/gpu drops from 4 to 3. The scheduler will not place a new Pod on the failed GPU. The device plugin does not stop the Pod that already holds it. That Pod fails by itself, because vLLM's next call to the driver fails, and its readiness probe then stops passing.

The plugin sees a GPU through the driver, so it reports what the driver reports. Slow failures, such as rising single-bit errors, a remap pending or a GPU that runs hot, never trip it. Draining a node because its counters trend badly is a decision someone has to automate on top, from the exporter's metrics.

Chapter 13 of 15

What the factory loses with it

A worker is not interchangeable, so its loss is not free

Back out to the factory. gpu-2 is gone, and with it every KV cache block it held. This is where the O'Reilly talk's line that inference is stateless stops being true. The conversations that lived on gpu-2 still exist, in the applications that will resend them. The work of having read them does not.

The picker drops gpu-2 as soon as its Pod stops being ready, and the endpoint leaves the pool. If no endpoint in a pool were ready at all, the picker would have no GPU to choose, and the request would fail with an error before it reached any model server. Here two remain. F5's AI Gateway workflow deck, on its endpoint picker slide, describes it as a continuous telemetry loop that keeps large generation jobs away from failing nodes, and that spills traffic from a warm server to cold ones when memory runs past a threshold. That is the same loop at work now. The next turns of gpu-2's conversations land on gpu-1 and gpu-3, where each thread is read again from its first token. The other workers get busier and slower at the moment the pool is one short.

A replacement does not arrive at once. After an Xid 48 the kubelet restarts the vLLM container inside the same Pod, which still holds the same GPU. When that GPU is the problem, the container keeps failing until the Pod is deleted. Only then does the Deployment create a new Pod, which the scheduler can place on one of the 3 healthy GPUs left on gpu-node-2, and it spends minutes loading the 34.3 GB of Qwen3-32B-FP8 weights before it can answer anything, about three in this lesson's illustrative world. An Xid 79 is harsher: the node is drained while the card and its link are looked at, and all four of its GPUs are out of service for that time, the 3 healthy ones included. The replacement Pod then has to go to another node, such as gpu-node-1. gpu-1 and gpu-3 run on other nodes, as their Pod addresses 10.244.1.21 and 10.244.3.23 show, so the drain leaves them in the pool.

In a training job the effect is harsher again. Every step waits for the slowest GPU before the results are combined, so one flaky card sets the pace for all of them, and losing one can stop the whole job until it restarts from a checkpoint.

Chapter 14 of 15

Where the GPU sits matters too

The same server can be near or far

One more thing is decided before any request arrives. A large server has two processors, each with its own memory, and each GPU is wired to one of them. That layout is called NUMA, non-uniform memory access, because the cost of reaching memory depends on which processor asks. If the process feeding a GPU runs on the other processor, or its buffers were allocated in the other processor's memory, every transfer crosses between the two sockets first. The talk puts that at two to three times slower.

Nothing reports an error. The GPU is healthy, the gauges look normal, and the worker is simply slower than its twin in the next slot. This is the kind of failure only a measurement finds. nvidia-smi topo -m prints, for each GPU, the CPU cores and the NUMA node it sits closest to, and the model server can be pinned there.

The same idea repeats at every scale. Inside a server, GPUs talk over NVLink, which on the H100 runs at 900 GB/s per GPU. Between servers they use the network, where one InfiniBand NDR port carries 400 Gb/s, which is 50 GB/s. In the same rack that is one switch away, and in another rack two or more. Training jobs feel it most, because every step exchanges results between GPUs. Schedulers that know the layout exist for this reason, and the talk's advice for observability includes the mapping from each Pod to its GPU and its NUMA node, so that a slow worker can be traced to where it sits. For inference the same thinking applies to cache: the request that lands on the worker already holding its prefix is the near one.

Chapter 15 of 15

The factory's scorecard

Eight production numbers, and tokens are only one output

The word factory is borrowed from manufacturing on purpose. Thaki Cloud's insights series (thakicloud.com/en/insights) sets it out the way a plant manager would. The inputs are data, energy, compute capacity, and models and algorithms. The processes are data preparation, training, fine-tuning, evaluation, deployment and inference. The outputs are trained models, predictions, recommendations, generated content, AI services, and tokens and other inference outputs. What makes it a factory is producing those outcomes repeatedly, efficiently and reliably, which is why factory metrics such as throughput, utilization, reliability and unit economics are used to judge it.

NVIDIA often describes the output of an AI factory as intelligence, and uses token throughput as an important production metric for generative AI. The series accepts that framing and adds a caution: it should not be treated as the only possible output or metric. A factory may also turn out trained and fine-tuned models, predictions, recommendations, agent responses, inference results and simulation outputs. A training cluster's product is a checkpoint, and counting its tokens per second says little about it.

The series lists eight operational metrics. Each one reads from a real source in this factory. Throughput is the rate of vllm:generation_tokens_total and vllm:prompt_tokens_total, per worker and summed over the pool. Latency is three vLLM histograms: vllm:time_to_first_token_seconds for the wait before the answer starts, vllm:inter_token_latency_seconds for the gap between tokens, and vllm:e2e_request_latency_seconds for the whole request. GPU utilization is DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL_RATIO, read with SM activity beside it as the earlier chapters showed. Time to train is wall time from the start of a run to its final checkpoint, paced by the slowest GPU. Time to deploy is the time from a new model version to a ready Pod: scheduling, the image pull and about three minutes, in this lesson's illustrative world, to load 34.3 GB of FP8 weights. Availability is the share of time the pool has a ready endpoint, because with none ready the picker has no GPU to choose and the request fails with an error before any GPU is reached. Energy efficiency comes from DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_ENERGY_JOULES_TOTAL, a counter in joules in the exporter's default list, divided by the tokens produced. Cost per workload, inference or token is the hourly price divided by the work done in the hour.

A worked example, with illustrative rates. Suppose gpu-2 averages 1,000 generated tokens a second across its batch for an hour. That is 3,600,000 tokens. At 3 dollars an hour, a million tokens cost 3 ÷ 3.6 = 0.83 dollars. If the card drew 650 watts on average, against the H100 SXM's configurable maximum of 700, it used 650 joules a second, 0.65 joules a token, and 0.65 kWh for the hour. Halve the traffic and the hour still costs 3 dollars for 1,800,000 tokens, so a million tokens cost 1.67. Energy per token rises too, because an idle GPU still draws power. This is the cost-per-useful-hour table of the first chapter, written per token.

The eight pull against each other, so the scorecard reads as a set. Larger batches raise throughput and stretch the gap between tokens. Spare workers raise availability and lower utilization. Throttled GPUs report high utilization and lower throughput, as the hotspot chapter showed. The useful question is the one the series ends on: can this infrastructure run the AI lifecycle repeatedly, from data and training through deployment and inference, and what does each unit of output cost?

What to keep

Busy, and watched

A GPU fleet is judged by how busy it is, because an idle GPU costs as much as a working one. The gauges need reading with care. GPU utilization is the share of time anything ran, and SM activity shows how much of the GPU it used. The memory gauge is flat on a model server, and the model server's own KV cache figure is the one that moves. Heat shows up as lower clocks and no error. Health problems show up in two ways. Slowly, as single-bit memory errors that are corrected and counted, and rows queued for spares. Suddenly, as an Xid code in the kernel log: 48 when memory returned data that could not be repaired, 79 when the GPU stopped answering on its link. The device plugin takes that GPU out of the schedule, the picker takes its Pod out of the pool, and the rest of the pool pays for its lost cache in repeated work. Around all of it sit the economics. Memory, more than compute, caps how many conversations a GPU holds, so free memory before buying cards. Compare owning and renting on the whole sum, never the hourly price. Route by load, or some cards throttle while others idle. And judge the factory on all eight of its numbers, with tokens as one output among several.
