Running a GPU cloud

A cloud with no way out

What it takes to run GPUs and models behind an air gap

Northlight Compute builds a site for a customer whose rules forbid any connection to the outside. Three bare-metal servers with four H100 GPUs each form one Kubernetes cluster and serve the model Qwen3-32B with vLLM, in its 8-bit release Qwen/Qwen3-32B-FP8. An ordinary install of that stack downloads almost everything it needs: container images, kernel headers, Helm charts, Python packages, 34.3 GB of model weights, the time of day and licence leases. Here none of those downloads can happen.

This lesson starts from the definition of an air gap and shows what breaks on day one. It then goes through each dependency in turn: how the kubelet decides whether to pull at all, how an inside registry is filled without changing a single digest, the pause image that the runtime names apart from kubeadm's list, the GPU driver's kernel packages, Python wheels, the Hugging Face cache, time, certificates and licences. It follows one model update through the transfer station with numbers, shows how signatures are checked with nobody to ask, and ends with what an air gap leaves unprotected. Along the way it separates a true air gap from network separation, builds a RAG pipeline and an agent's tools with nothing outside to call, walks F5 BNK's disconnected licence from the DigitalAssetID to the TMM's readiness gates, and places governance on the board from the first day.

Chapter 1 of 19

What the words air gap mean

The definition is stricter than a firewall rule

NIST's glossary defines an air gap as "an interface between two systems at which (a) they are not connected physically and (b) any logical connection is not automated (i.e., data is transferred through the interface only manually, under human control)". The glossary takes the wording from CNSSI 4009-2022, which takes it from RFC 4949. Two conditions have to hold together. No cable or radio joins the two sides. And nothing moves data across by itself: a person carries it.

The glossary gives the definition and no examples, so applying it to real sites is a reading, and this lesson's reading goes like this. A site behind a firewall with one allowed proxy fails the first condition. A link that copies files across on a schedule is automated, so it fails the second. A copy that a person starts by hand, such as an scp command from a laptop, depends on what the laptop is joined to. A laptop that is online and on the site network at the same moment joins the two systems, and the first condition fails. A laptop that is never connected to both at once is a carrier, in the same way as a drive, and a drive is also physically connected while it is plugged in. What matters is that nothing joins the outside and the site at the same time, and that a person moves every byte. Vendor procedures for offline installs often allow USB media or a network copy. A carrier that is never on both sides at once, whether a drive or a dedicated laptop, fits the definition most plainly.

Other names for a closed network describe different things, and customers use them loosely. A private network is one organisation's own network, and it can still have controlled links to the outside. Network segmentation splits a network into zones with rules between them, and traffic still crosses those rules automatically wherever a rule allows it. Korea's network separation, 망분리 (mangbunri), is a family of implementation models, and the models differ from one another. Physical separation gives a worker two networks and two PCs, one for internal work and one for the internet. Logical separation keeps one physical PC and puts one of the two sides in a virtual desktop. Files still have to move between the two sides, so sites run a network linkage system, 망연계 (mangyeongye), that passes a file across after an administrator approves it. That system is wired to both networks and moves the bytes over a network. On this lesson's reading it fails condition (a), however carefully each transfer is approved.

The rules behind network separation are also moving. Korea's Financial Services Commission published a roadmap on 13 August 2024 to ease the ten-year-old network separation rule in finance. Through a regulatory sandbox exemption, financial companies may reach the internet to use generative AI once they have security measures in place, cloud software as a service is widened from some back-office functions to security and customer relationship management, and the supervisory regulation is to be revised to ease physical separation for research and development. For public institutions, Korea's National Intelligence Service has introduced the National Network Security Framework, N2SF. It grades information as classified, sensitive or open and applies different controls to each grade, and a 2026 press report describes the national cybersecurity basic guidelines being revised to adopt it. So a customer who says the site is network-separated may be working under a rule that allows approved automated transfers, or even internet access for some work. Ask which model the rule names before designing for a strict air gap, and before assuming that one exists.

The definition also rules out whole classes of product. A service that is delivered from the vendor's cloud, such as a hosted monitoring tool or a hosted AI governance service, needs a connection to work at all. The F5 deck on AI usage control says this plainly about its own hosted service. The AI services it governs are on the public internet, so it needs internet access to reach them, and so there is no air gap. An on-premises or local deployment is, in the deck's words, the best that can be done, and even that still reaches the internet services it governs. Every component of the closed site has to run entirely on the site.

On the board, the road between the public sources and the transfer station is a person walking with a drive. Every other road is a network.

Chapter 2 of 19

Day one: the first image pull fails

Every Pod names an image on a registry the site cannot reach

The cluster is installed the ordinary way and the first Pod never starts. The kubelet asks containerd for an image on nvcr.io or registry.k8s.io, the pull fails, and the container waits in ImagePullBackOff. Kubernetes retries with a growing delay that stops at 300 seconds.

The fix has two halves. First, every image is copied into a registry inside the site. Second, each server is told to use it. In containerd that is one hosts.toml file per upstream registry, in the directory named by config_path. If no registry options are set at all, containerd's CRI plugin defaults config_path to /etc/containerd/certs.d:/etc/docker/certs.d. A file lists hosts to try in order and a server to fall back to. When hosts are listed, they are tried first in order, and the server is used only when all of them have failed. Setting server to the internal registry means the upstream is never consulted. Changes under that directory need no containerd restart. Pod specs keep their original image names.

A catch-all exists. A directory called _default is used when no other registry directory matches, so one _default/hosts.toml can send every registry, docker.io included, to the inside copy. That matters because an image named only busybox means docker.io/library/busybox:latest, a registry nobody wrote a file for.

The same files can be tested without the kubelet: ctr images pull --hosts-dir /etc/containerd/certs.d with the image name pulls exactly as the CRI plugin would.

Chapter 3 of 19

Filling the inside registry without changing a digest

Copy every architecture and every signature, and keep the digests

A digest is the SHA-256 of an image's manifest, written as sha256: followed by 64 hex characters. A Pod spec, a Helm chart or a policy can pin an image by digest, and then only that exact content will do. The OCI distribution specification says a manifest can be requested by tag or by digest, and that when it is requested by digest the client should check that the manifest it got matches. If the copy inside has a different digest, a pinned pull fails even though an image with the right name and tag is there.

Digests change more easily than people expect. A tag such as v26.7.3 often points at a list of images, one per CPU architecture. skopeo copy, a tool that copies images between registries and directories, copies only the image for the current operating system and architecture unless it is given --all. A list cut down to one architecture is a different manifest with a different digest. Recompressing layers changes digests too. skopeo's --preserve-digests makes the copy fail when the digest cannot be kept, which is what a closed site wants.

skopeo sync is the bulk form. Its documentation names the use directly: populating registries that run inside air-gapped environments. Outside, it copies a list of repositories into a directory on the drive. Inside, the same command copies from the directory into the internal registry. skopeo copy carries the manifest, the layers and the signatures.

Trust at the registry matters as well. containerd treats resolving, turning a tag into a digest, as a trusted operation. A host allowed only pull cannot change what a tag means. The internal registry is the site's source of truth, so it gets both pull and resolve, and its certificate is checked against the internal CA. skip_verify exists and is documented as being for testing only.

Chapter 4 of 19

Kubernetes' own images, and the pause image the runtime asks for

kubeadm lists and pre-pulls its images, but the runtime names its own pause image

kubeadm documents a section for running without an Internet connection: the control plane images have to be pulled in advance. kubeadm config images list prints them and kubeadm config images pull fetches them. The list command's defaults are --image-repository registry.k8s.io and --kubernetes-version stable-1. stable-1 is a label that kubeadm resolves over the network, so inside the gap the version has to be given exactly, for example in kubernetesVersion in the configuration file.

Image paths can change when a custom repository is used. The documentation gives the example of an image that lives under a subpath at registry.k8s.io and is expected at the top level of a custom repository. The documented order handles this: pull from the default paths outside, push to the paths that kubeadm config images list --config=config.yaml prints, and pass the same config.yaml to kubeadm init. The configuration has imageRepository for all images, and its own imageRepository and imageTag for etcd and for CoreDNS.

One image needs a second look. Every Pod starts inside a sandbox held open by a small pause image. kubeadm's list does include a pause image: its code appends <imageRepository>/pause with its own pinned tag, 3.10.2 at the time of writing, so kubeadm config images pull fetches it too. The container runtime does not read that list. It uses its own sandbox setting, and kubeadm's documentation says a custom pause image has to be set in the runtime. In containerd 2.x that is the pinned_images section, with the example sandbox = 'registry.k8s.io/pause:3.10'. In CRI-O it is pause_image. So a site that mirrors exactly what kubeadm lists holds pause:3.10.2 under its internal path, while containerd asks for registry.k8s.io/pause:3.10. The name and the tag both differ. If the image the runtime asks for is not inside, no Pod sandbox can start, even with every other image in place, and every Pod on the node fails the same way. The fix is to make the two agree: set the runtime's sandbox image to the one that was mirrored, or mirror the one the runtime names as well.

Chapter 5 of 19

Which pulls happen at all

imagePullPolicy decides whether the registry is asked, and a tag can decide the policy

Not every container start contacts a registry. imagePullPolicy decides. With IfNotPresent the image is pulled only if it is not already on the node. With Never the kubelet does not try to fetch it, and startup fails if it is missing. With Always, every container start asks the runtime to contact the registry, resolve the tag to a digest and download any missing layers. Inside the gap that means an Always Pod fails whenever the internal registry is down, even when the image is already on the node.

The default depends on the name. When the field is left out, a tag of :latest or no tag at all gives Always. Any other tag gives IfNotPresent, and so does a digest. The value is fixed when the object is first created and does not change if the tag is edited later. With a tag and a digest both given, only the digest is used for the pull.

Pulls are also serial by default. The kubelet sends one pull request to the image service at a time. A large image, such as a GPU driver or a vLLM image of several gigabytes, holds up every other pull on that node until it finishes. serializeImagePulls: false allows parallel pulls, and maxParallelImagePulls caps them.

Finally, images can leave a node. The kubelet collects unused images every five minutes. When disk use passes HighThresholdPercent it deletes them until it reaches LowThresholdPercent, and imageMaximumGCAge removes images unused for a set time. In an open site a deleted image comes back with the next pull. Here it comes back only if the internal registry still has it.

Chapter 6 of 19

Packages, the GPU driver and the charts

The driver wants kernel headers, and the chart wants a repository

The NVIDIA GPU Operator installs the driver, the container toolkit and the device plugin as containers. NVIDIA's air-gapped install page names two things it needs from the internet. The first is its images. Each one is pulled outside, pushed to the internal registry, and its repository field in values.yaml is changed. The general image syntax is <repository>/<image>:<version>, and the driver image's tag also carries the operating system, for example 595.91.07-ubuntu22.04. If the GPUCluster resource is used, one more image has to be mirrored, the DRA driver from registry.k8s.io.

The second is operating system packages. The driver container compiles a kernel module, so it downloads the kernel headers that match the running kernel. On Ubuntu that is linux-headers, linux-image and linux-modules for the running kernel version. On the RHEL family it is kernel-headers, kernel-devel and kernel-core, plus the gcc version that built the kernel. Inside, those come from a local package mirror. NVIDIA names apt-mirror as one way to build it. The mirror is described in a file called custom-repo.list, with the jammy, jammy-updates and jammy-security suites for Ubuntu 22.04, and that file is loaded into a ConfigMap that driver.repoConfig names.

Name resolution is part of the install too. NVIDIA's page says the nodes must reach the local DNS servers, which here resolve the internal registry and mirror names. When the nodes move to a newer operating system, the page says to update custom-repo.list and the ConfigMap. A node that reboots into a kernel whose headers are not mirrored cannot build its driver. Precompiled driver containers remove that need.

The Helm chart is fetched outside with helm pull and installed from the .tgz file. helm pull --prov also fetches the chart's provenance file, and --verify refuses a chart without one.

Chapter 7 of 19

Python packages without an index

Resolve outside for the inside machine, install inside from files

Python tooling expects to download packages from an index at install time. Inside the gap there are two ways to replace it. One is an internal index: a server that speaks PEP 503, the simple repository API, and that pip is pointed at with index-url in pip.conf or --index-url on the command line. The other is a plain directory of files. pip install --no-index ignores every index, and --find-links with a local path makes pip look for archives in that directory listing.

The files are gathered outside with pip download, which does the same dependency resolution as pip install and keeps the files in place of installing them. The download machine is rarely the same as the GPU server, so pip download takes --platform and --python-version, which tell it to choose wheels for the inside machine. A wheel is a prebuilt package file, and wheels are often built for one CPU architecture and one Python version.

Hashes protect the trip. With --require-hashes, every requirement must carry a hash, and pip refuses a file whose hash does not match. That mode switches on by itself as soon as any line in a requirements file has a --hash option. It turns a requirements file into a checklist of exact files, which is what the transfer station wants to compare against.

One option deserves a warning. --trusted-host tells pip to accept a host even though it has no valid HTTPS or none at all. Inside a closed site it is tempting to use it for a mirror with an internal certificate. The better fix is to install the internal CA's root, the same one containerd and the driver container already trust.

Chapter 8 of 19

Model weights and the hub that is not there

The largest download of all is the model

A model server normally fetches its weights by name from a model hub. vLLM is given a name such as Qwen/Qwen3-32B-FP8, and the Hugging Face library downloads 34.3 GB into a cache directory. The name matters for the size: the same model in BF16, the repository Qwen/Qwen3-32B, is 65.5 GB, twice as much to fetch, carry and check. Inside the gap that call has nowhere to go. Two things replace it. The weights sit in an internal model store, and the server is started with a local path in place of the name. And the environment variable HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1 is set. Hugging Face documents its effect: no HTTP calls are made to the Hub, only cached files are used, and a missing file raises an error.

Without the variable the library still asks the Hub whether a newer version exists, even when the file is already cached. That question waits up to HF_HUB_ETAG_TIMEOUT, 10 seconds by default, and then falls back to the cached file. In a closed site every one of those questions has to fail first, and each failure costs time on every start. With offline mode set, any call through the HfApi client raises an OfflineModeIsEnabled exception, so a hidden call shows up as an error and never as a silent wait.

The Hugging Face libraries also send usage data by default. HF_HUB_DISABLE_TELEMETRY=1 switches that off. Inside a gap the data could never leave, so the setting removes one more failing outbound call.

The weights come from somewhere outside first. On the download machine, snapshot_download fetches a repository at an exact revision. Inside, code that loads a model can pass local_files_only=True to from_pretrained, which has the same effect as the variable for that call.

Chapter 9 of 19

Carrying a Hugging Face cache in whole

Blobs named by their ETag, snapshots made of links, refs that name a commit

A site can carry plain files and load them by path, or it can carry a Hugging Face cache and point HF_HUB_CACHE at it. The second keeps revisions and lets code that uses model names keep working in offline mode. The cache has a fixed layout. Each repository gets a folder named after its type and name, for example models--Qwen--Qwen3-32B-FP8, and inside it are three folders: refs, blobs and snapshots.

blobs holds the downloaded files, and each file is named by its ETag, the identifier the Hub gave it. For a large file stored in Git LFS, such as a safetensors shard, that is the SHA-256 of its content. For a small file kept in plain git, such as config.json, vocab.json or merges.txt, it is the git blob SHA-1. In Qwen/Qwen3-32B-FP8, 8 of the 17 files are in LFS, the seven safetensors shards and tokenizer.json, and the other 9 are plain git files. snapshots holds one folder per revision, and the files in it are symbolic links to the blobs. Two revisions that share a file share one blob. refs holds small text files named after branches. refs/main contains the commit identifier that main pointed to when the files were fetched.

This layout sets two rules for the transfer. First, the copy must keep symbolic links. A tool that follows them writes full copies into snapshots, doubling the size of a 34.3 GB model, or drops them and leaves snapshot folders empty. Second, the commit in refs/main is the revision the site has. Offline mode will not move it, because it cannot ask what main means today. That is exactly what a closed site wants: the revision changes only when a new transfer brings a new one.

The checksums the station computes fit the layout in part. Every weight file is stored in LFS, so its blob name is its SHA-256, and the station can check each weight blob against its own name, with the manifest the operator writes outside listing the same values. The small files are named by git SHA-1, which sha256sum will never match. git hash-object computes that value for them, or the manifest simply lists their SHA-256 separately.

Chapter 10 of 19

RAG and agents with nothing outside to call

Every stage of retrieval, and every tool an agent uses, has to run on the site

RAG, retrieval-augmented generation, lets the model answer from the site's own documents. When a question arrives, the pipeline finds the passages that match it and puts them into the prompt next to the question. Building that pipeline takes six stages: parse the documents into text, read scanned pages with OCR, cut the text into chunks, turn each chunk into a vector with an embedding model, store the vectors in a vector database, and at question time search that database and pass the best chunks to Qwen3-32B-FP8. In an open deployment several of those stages are often hosted APIs: an embedding API, a managed vector database, a cloud OCR service. Inside the gap every stage is a service on the site, and its software, its model files and its data files come in through the transfer station like the 34.3 GB chat model did.

OCR, optical character recognition, turns a picture of a page into text. A scanned contract has no text layer, so without OCR it never reaches the index. Tesseract, a widely used open source OCR engine, shows what has to be carried in. It reads one data file per language, named <lang>.traineddata, from its tessdata directory, and the command chooses languages with -l, for example -l kor+eng with kor.traineddata and eng.traineddata in place. The project publishes three official sets. tessdata_fast is the fastest and least accurate, and it is what Linux distributions ship. tessdata_best is the most accurate and the slowest. tessdata is the only set that still supports the legacy engine. A language whose file is missing cannot be fetched at run time, so the language list is decided before the gap closes.

An embedding model turns a piece of text into a fixed-length list of numbers, a vector, so that texts with similar meaning get vectors that lie close together. Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B is one example from the same family as the chat model. It has 0.6 billion weights in 1,191,586,416 bytes of safetensors, about 1.19 GB, reads up to 32K tokens, and returns up to 1,024 numbers per text, with shorter outputs from 32 to 1,024 allowed by its model card. vLLM serves it as a pooling model, started with --runner pooling, and answers on the OpenAI-compatible /v1/embeddings endpoint. It is loaded from a local path with HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1, exactly as the chat model is. It is a second model with its own transfer, checksum, approval and revision, and the question at search time must be embedded by the same model that embedded the documents.

The vector database is the third new service. pgvector, an extension for PostgreSQL, is one example: CREATE EXTENSION vector adds a column type, and vector(1024) holds one embedding. Each vector takes 4 × dimensions + 8 bytes, so 4 × 1,024 + 8 = 4,104 bytes. A corpus cut into 1,000,000 chunks holds 4,104,000,000 bytes of vectors, about 4.1 GB, before the chunk text, the row overhead and the index. The index type HNSW builds a graph for fast approximate search, with m 16 and ef_construction 64 by default, and searches with hnsw.ef_search 40 by default. HNSW indexes the vector type only up to 2,000 dimensions and halfvec, half-precision numbers at 2 bytes each, up to 4,000. Qwen3-Embedding-8B returns 4,096 numbers, so at full size it cannot be indexed in either type. Its model card allows shorter outputs, and 2,048 dimensions stored as halfvec take 2 × 2,048 + 8 = 4,104 bytes, the same as the 0.6B model's 1,024 at full precision.

The embedding model and the index are tied together. Vectors from two different embedding models, or from two revisions of one model, do not share a meaning, so changing the embedding model means embedding every chunk again and rebuilding the index. In a closed site that makes an embedding model update a larger change request than a chat model update: the 1.19 GB of weights take seconds of machine time at the station, and the re-embedding of the whole corpus is the long part.

Agents need the same treatment for their tools. An agent is a model that calls tools in a loop: it asks for a tool, a program runs it, the result goes back into the prompt. vLLM turns tool calls on with --enable-auto-tool-choice and a --tool-call-parser, and Qwen's own documentation serves Qwen3 with --tool-call-parser hermes. Tools are commonly offered through MCP, the Model Context Protocol, whose two standard transports are stdio, a local process, and Streamable HTTP, a server on the network. The Qwen3-32B-FP8 model card's agent example starts two MCP servers with the command uvx: mcp-server-time and mcp-server-fetch. Inside the gap all three parts of that line fail. uvx downloads the package, and a Python interpreter if it needs one, when it starts, so the server is installed from carried wheels instead, with pip install mcp-server-fetch on Python 3.11.5 or newer. The fetch tool exists to read web pages, and there are none to read. And pointed at an internal wiki, fetch refuses by default, because it rejects private, loopback and link-local addresses, and every address inside the site is private. --allow-private-ips lifts that guard for every internal address at once. On its own it gives the model a way to reach any service on the site. The README also offers --allowed-hosts, which names the hosts fetch may reach, and a request must pass both checks. So --allow-private-ips together with --allowed-hosts wiki.northlight.internal reaches that one host and nothing else, and listing a host in --allowed-hosts does not by itself lift the private-address guard. The better design gives the agent narrow internal tools: a search over the RAG index, the internal ticket system, the internal code repositories, each its own MCP server inside the site with credentials scoped to what that tool needs. Any agent built around a SaaS API needs an on-site equivalent or loses that tool.

Chapter 11 of 19

Time and certificates

Two dependencies that fail weeks or months later

Some dependencies fail later, which makes them harder to trace. Time comes first. Servers normally correct their clocks from public NTP servers. Inside, one internal time source serves everyone. chrony's local directive exists for this case. It lets a time server appear synchronised to its clients on an isolated network, where machines have to agree with one another and not necessarily with real time. The stratum it reports is 10 by default. The documentation says the server can be kept vaguely in line with real time by manual input.

The details catch people out. chronyd serves no clients by default and acts only as a client itself, so the time server needs an allow line for the site's networks. Several time servers can share the local setting with the orphan option. They then poll one another, and only the one with the smallest reference ID uses its local clock. If it stops answering, the next takes over. A client also ignores a source whose root distance is too large, 3 seconds by default. A server that starts with a clock far off corrects it slowly unless makestep allows a step.

Clocks matter because certificates and tokens carry validity times, so a server whose clock is far enough off rejects a valid certificate. Certificates are the second dependency. No public certificate authority can be reached, so the site runs its own and installs its root certificate on every server. kubeadm's client certificates expire after one year and its CA after ten. kubeadm renews them during a control plane upgrade, which keeps a cluster safe only if upgrades come less than a year apart. A closed site that upgrades slowly runs kubeadm certs renew all itself and restarts the control plane Pods. kubeadm does not rotate CA certificates out of the box, and Kubernetes cannot revoke a certificate, so an issued one stays valid until it expires.

Chapter 12 of 19

Licences that cannot phone home

A lease server inside, a licence file, or a report carried out and a receipt carried back

Licensed software usually checks in with its vendor. Inside the gap that check-in has to be replaced, and vendors offer three shapes of answer.

The first is a licence server inside the site. NVIDIA's License System has a Cloud License Service, hosted on the NVIDIA Licensing Portal, and a Delegated License Service, DLS, that runs on premises. Licences are downloaded from the portal outside and uploaded to the DLS by hand. An air-gapped DLS cannot send its registration token to the portal, so its details are entered on the portal manually. Clients reach the DLS on port 443 to acquire, renew and release licences. Port 80 has to be open too: Windows VMs use it to return their licences when they shut down. Ports 8081 and 8082 are still accepted for backward compatibility.

The second is a licence file on each machine. NVIDIA calls this node-locked licensing: a client with no network, or on an air-gapped network, reads its vGPU licence from a local file. It was introduced in vGPU software 15.0.

The third is a receipt carried both ways, and the owner's own offline procedure for F5 BNK uses it. With no internet on the deployment host, the licence step pauses after it writes a configuration report. A person copies the report to a machine with internet access and posts it to F5's entitlement service. The answer is a licence manifest of about 16 KB. The person carries the manifest back in and applies it as a receipt, and the deployment continues.

All three need the same planning. Every product in the stack is checked for this kind of dependency, the date its lease or file expires goes in the site's calendar, and the outbound report is reviewed like any other data that leaves.

Chapter 13 of 19

F5 BNK's disconnected licence, step by step

A DigitalAssetID, one POST outside, a 16 KB manifest and two readiness gates

F5 BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes, BNK, is licensed through a Kubernetes object. From BNK 2.3 the licence is a License resource in the API group k8s.f5net.com/v1, with the licence token, a JWT, in spec.jwt and a condition called LicenseActive. The licence is checked when it is applied. Until it is active the controller logs License is not enabled.. skip Resource controllers and never creates a TMM, the Traffic Management Microkernel Pod that forwards BNK's traffic. The control plane comes up complete and nothing carries a packet. In an online install the cluster reaches F5 to activate the licence. The owner's offline procedure for BNK 2.3 on BlueField-3 DPUs, deployed with F5's dpubnkctl tool, replaces that call with a round trip that people carry.

The outward leg starts with deploy cne. With no internet on the deployment host, the licence flow pauses after it extracts the configuration report. It writes the report to artifacts/license-config-report.json inside the project and prints two things: the DigitalAssetID, which identifies this deployment to F5, and a ready-made curl command. The report is reviewed and carried out. On a machine with internet access the command sends it as an HTTP POST to https://product.apis.f5.com/ee/v1/entitlements/telemetry with four headers: Content-Type: application/json, F5-DigitalAssetId with the printed ID, User-Agent: SPK, and Authorization: Bearer with the JWT. curl writes the answer to license-manifest.json.

The next check is the file's size, about 16 KB. The command runs curl with -s, which hides progress and error messages, and without --fail. Without --fail, curl writes the body of an HTTP error response into the output file exactly as it would a good answer, and still exits with status 0. So a file that exists proves nothing. The procedure gives only the expected size. A file far smaller than about 16 KB is not the manifest, and on this lesson's reading it is most likely an error body from the service. Carrying it in would waste a trip through the station. The printed command also carries -k, which tells curl to skip checking the server's certificate. A machine with ordinary internet access can normally check it, and then the flag adds nothing but risk.

The inward leg applies the manifest as a receipt: dpubnkctl-airgap license apply-receipt --poc <poc-name> --manifest <path to license-manifest.json>. Then the data plane has to become ready, and here the procedure leans on a Kubernetes mechanism. A Pod can list extra conditions in spec.readinessGates. The Pod counts as Ready only when all its containers are ready and every listed condition is True, and a condition that has not been written yet counts as False. When the containers are ready and a gate is still False, the kubelet sets only ContainersReady. The f5-tmm Pods carry two gates, and kubectl get pods -o wide shows them as 2/2 when both have passed. If they stay short of 2/2 after the receipt, the procedure restarts the DaemonSet with kubectl rollout restart -n f5-bnk daemonset/f5-tmm and waits with kubectl rollout status and --timeout=10m.

Five checks close the step. The License in f5-cne-core shows STATE Active. The VLAN objects, f5-spk-vlans in f5-bnk, show READY True. The GatewayClass shows ACCEPTED True. The f5-tmm Pods run with 2/2 readiness gates. The CNEInstance shows Available True.

The procedure itself moves both files with scp, between the deployment host and a laptop with internet access. That is the laptop of the first chapter, and on this site it would join the two sides. Here both files cross through the transfer station on media, and the station's record shows the report going out and the manifest coming in. In the licence step, the report is the one file that leaves the site.

Chapter 14 of 19

Stage everything once, then count it

An offline install is an online install done earlier, somewhere else

Every chapter so far adds an item to a list. The practical way to make sure the list is complete is to run the whole install once with internet access, let the installer download everything, and keep what it downloaded. The owner's offline procedure for F5 BNK on BlueField-3 DPUs works exactly this way. A temporary staging project runs the download phase online. Its artefacts directory is backed up, carried to the closed site, and restored into the real project. The deployment then runs in offline mode, and nothing is downloaded.

The backup is checked by counting. For the release in the procedure, the expected numbers are 55 container images, 29 images for the DPUs, 6 Debian packages, 9 other files, 4 Helm charts, 1 manifests file and a release manifest. A short loop prints the count in each folder before anyone leaves the connected room, and again inside after the restore. A missing folder or a short count is found in minutes, where a missing image would otherwise surface as one Pod in ImagePullBackOff hours into the install.

The procedure also shows the tools problem. Docker has to be installed on the deployment host before it goes offline, from the internet or from a local .deb package. skopeo is needed only for the online download phase. Every tool the inside steps call has to be present before the gap closes.

The release manifest ties the bundle to a version. When the next release comes, the same staging run produces a new bundle with new counts, and the transfer station compares it with the manifest it came with.

Chapter 15 of 19

The way in: one model update, with numbers

Sixteen minutes of copying, a day of process

A new revision of the model is published: 34.3 GB. Outside, an operator downloads it on a machine kept for that purpose and computes a SHA-256 checksum, a fingerprint that changes if any byte changes. It has to match the value the publisher lists. The files and a manifest of checksums go onto a removable drive.

At the transfer station the drive is read, the checksums are computed again, the publisher's signature is verified where the publisher provides one, and the files are scanned for malware. Two people approve the import and the approval is recorded. Then the files are copied into the internal model store and hashed a third time. The drive never goes back out.

The arithmetic uses illustrative rates. Downloading 34,300 MB at 125 MB/s, which is 1 Gbit/s, takes 274 seconds. Hashing at 500 MB/s takes 69 seconds each time. Writing and reading the drive at 250 MB/s takes 137 seconds each. Scanning at 200 MB/s takes 172 seconds. Copying into the store at 1,250 MB/s takes 27 seconds. Each figure is rounded to the nearest second. The total is 480 seconds outside, 378 at the station and 96 inside, 954 seconds, about 16 minutes.

People take far longer. Approval takes 4 hours here, evaluation on one GPU 2 hours, and the wait for the maintenance window 18 hours, 24 hours together. Add the 3 minute load and the first answer from the new revision comes 24 hours 18 minutes and 54 seconds after publication. An open site would answer after 274 seconds of download and 180 seconds of loading, 454 seconds, about 8 minutes.

Chapter 16 of 19

Checking signatures with nobody to ask

A public key, a trusted root and the right flags, all carried in ahead of time

A checksum proves the bytes did not change on the trip. A signature proves who produced them, and inside a gap it has to be checked without asking any online service. That works when everything the check needs was carried in earlier and recorded. A public key is the start, and it is not always enough.

cosign, the signing tool from the Sigstore project, shows the pattern. cosign verify --key cosign.pub followed by an image name checks an image's signature against a key file. With --local-image it checks an image stored on disk, for example in the directory skopeo wrote. For other files, such as model weights or a chart, the signature and its verification material are kept in a bundle file made when the file was signed. cosign verify-blob takes the bundle with --bundle, which is required, and one way to say who should have signed. For a key that is --key publisher.pub. For a keyless signature, made with a short-lived certificate, it is --certificate-identity and --certificate-oidc-issuer, the identity and the identity provider expected in that certificate.

The bundle also carries material from Sigstore's services: certificates, transparency log entries and timestamps. A transparency log is a public, append-only record of signatures. cosign checks that material against a trusted root, the set of certificate authorities, log keys and timestamp authorities it trusts. It fetches the trusted root online through TUF, Sigstore's update framework, unless a file is given with --trusted-root. Inside the gap that fetch fails. So the site carries in trusted_root.json beside the publisher's key and passes it on every check. A signature made with a key and never uploaded to a transparency log needs --insecure-ignore-tlog instead, and cosign's reference notes that artifacts left out of a log cannot be publicly verified.

The station can also add its own signature. skopeo copy can sign an image as it copies it, with --sign-by and a GPG key ID or with --sign-by-sigstore-private-key and a key file. The inside registry then holds images signed by the station, and a policy inside can require that signature. When the destination cannot store signatures, --remove-signatures lets the copy go through without them.

Helm charts have their own form. A chart's provenance file holds its hash and a signature, helm pull --prov fetches it, and helm pull --verify refuses a chart that has none. --verify also needs the publisher's public key in a local keyring, ~/.gnupg/pubring.gpg by default or a file named with --keyring, so that key is carried in like any other. Not every chart publisher ships a provenance file. Check before planning on --verify, and fall back to a checksum the publisher lists when there is none.

The keys themselves become part of the supply chain. A publisher's public key is carried in once, recorded with a fingerprint, and replaced only through the same station, with the same two approvals.

Chapter 17 of 19

Governance as part of the design

Identity, access, keys, audit and approval, placed on the board from the first day

Security, access and governance are often treated as a policy layer added once the hardware runs. A planning checklist for enterprise AI infrastructure lists them as one design item of their own, with seven parts: identity, least privilege, network boundaries, access to data and models, keys, audit, and artefact approval. On this site each part has a concrete home. Identity is the IAM module: one account per person and per service, and no shared administrator. Least privilege means each role holds only the rights its job needs, so the person who requests an import cannot approve it and the operator of the model servers cannot change the station's policy. The network boundary is the gap itself, plus the segments inside it. Data and model access decides who can read the 34.3 GB of Qwen3-32B-FP8 weights in the model store, who can read the documents behind the RAG index, and which tool an agent may call. Keys are the internal CA's private key, the station's signing key, the publishers' public keys and the licence JWT, and each has an owner and a place it is kept. Audit is the log of who did what. Artefact approval is the station's record.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework, AI RMF 1.0, published as NIST AI 100-1 in January 2023, is the reference most such checklists point to. It is voluntary. It sorts the work into four functions: GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE. GOVERN is described as a cross-cutting function, infused throughout the other three, and the framework says that risk management should be continuous, timely, and performed throughout the AI system lifecycle. After the GOVERN outcomes are in place, it says, most users start with MAP and continue to MEASURE or MANAGE. The framework speaks in outcomes for policies, roles and inventories. It does not name RBAC, signing keys or transfer stations. A checklist like the one above is the step that turns those outcomes into parts of the infrastructure.

Several of its subcategories land directly on this site. GOVERN 1.6 asks for mechanisms to inventory AI systems: here that is the list of which revision of which model runs on which GPU, and which embedding model built which index. GOVERN 1.7 asks for processes to decommission and phase out AI systems safely, which is retiring a revision from the store and from the servers. GOVERN 2.1 asks that roles and responsibilities be documented and clear, which is the requester and the two approvers on every import. GOVERN 4.3 asks for practices that enable testing, identification of incidents and information sharing, which here is the evaluation on one GPU before a revision goes live and the records that trace a flawed import. GOVERN 6 covers third-party software, data and other supply chain issues. 6.1 asks for policies and procedures that address AI risks associated with third-party entities, and 6.2 asks for contingency processes when third-party data or AI systems fail. MAP 4.1 asks that the risks of third-party data and software be mapped and documented, and MANAGE 3.2 asks that pre-trained models used in development be monitored as part of regular monitoring and maintenance. Every image, wheel and model on this site is third-party, and Qwen3-32B-FP8 and the embedder are pre-trained models from outside.

The checklist's own advice is that governance can be added later, and that designing it early reduces the risk of rework. A closed site shows why with two examples. In the first, an advisory names a flawed image by digest. If every transfer record lists the digests it carried and every Pod pins its image by digest, the answer is a search over the records, 52 of them for a year of weekly transfers (illustrative), and a list of running Pods by image. If the records hold only names and tags, the digest has to be rebuilt from what is still present, and the kubelet's image garbage collection and any pruning of the internal registry may already have deleted the evidence. In the second, the cluster ran its first months with everyone sharing kubeadm's super-admin.conf. Kubernetes cannot revoke a certificate. Since kubeadm v1.29, the ordinary admin.conf is in the group kubeadm:cluster-admins, which gets its rights from a ClusterRoleBinding, so deleting that binding does cut it off. super-admin.conf is in the group system:masters, which bypasses RBAC, so no binding can be removed, and giving each person their own identity later does not withdraw it. On kubeadm before v1.29, admin.conf itself was in system:masters and behaves the same way. It keeps working until it expires, up to a year with kubeadm's defaults. Both repairs are cheap on the first day and expensive afterwards.

Chapter 18 of 19

Day two: living inside

Every routine task now passes through a person

After the install, the gap shapes every routine task. An update is a change request: someone fetches it, checks it, carries it in and schedules it. A security fix follows the same path, so a vulnerability published today is patched inside some days later, and the site has to decide in advance how many. With a weekly transfer cycle, a fix waits up to 7 days for the next transfer and a day more for approval, evaluation and the window, 8 days in the worst case.

Monitoring cannot be a hosted service. Metrics, logs, traces and alerts are collected, stored and read inside, and somebody on site has to be watching. The GPU metrics come from the DCGM exporter, one of the GPU Operator's own images. Storage for them is capacity the site has to plan, and retention is the site's own decision.

Vendor support cannot open a remote session. An engineer on site collects the diagnostics. If they have to leave the site, they go out through the same station in the other direction, after a review of what they contain.

Certificates, licences and time need a calendar. The kubeadm certificates are renewed before a year passes, the internal CA's expiry is known, and every lease or licence file has a date. The site therefore keeps spare parts, offline documentation and people who can repair it alone.

Chapter 19 of 19

What an air gap does not stop

One path is closed, and three stay open

An air gap removes one path: a network connection from outside. Three others stay open. The first is whatever was carried in. A checksum proves the file is the one the publisher released. It says nothing about whether the publisher's file is safe. A scanner finds only what its patterns describe. The second is the supply chain before the station. A compromised base image, package or model arrives correctly signed. The third is the people who are already inside, with their credentials, their physical access and their mistakes.

So the controls of an ordinary site still apply inside: least privilege, audit logs, separate network segments and patching. Media control is added to them. Every drive that can cross is known, numbered and stored, and the record of transfers says which drive carried what.

The station itself is the most valuable target on the site. It is the one machine that reads media written outside, so it runs as little software as possible, its own updates come through its own checks, and nobody uses it for anything else.

The gap also adds a cost that is easy to miss. Every fix arrives late, so a known flaw stays open inside longer than it would outside. A site that treats the gap as its main control tends to patch less, because nothing outside can reach it. An insider or a poisoned import finds that site easier to work in.

What to keep

A closed site runs its own supply chain

An air gap is a statement about one interface: nothing crosses it unless a person carries it. Everything else follows from that. Images, the pause image, packages, wheels, charts, model weights, the embedding model, OCR language files, the vector database, agent tools, time, certificates and licences each need a source inside, and each source has to be filled through the transfer station. The practical way to get the list right is to stage an install once online and count what it downloaded. The station checks what it can check: that the bytes are unchanged, that the publisher signed them when the publisher provides a signature, that no known malware is present. It then records who approved the import. What the site gains is the removal of every network path from outside. What it pays is speed, since each update and each fix waits for people, and the full weight of operating alone. The gap is one control among the others the site still needs.
