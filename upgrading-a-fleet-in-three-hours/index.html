BNK and gateways

Upgrading a fleet inside a three-hour window

Ten BIG-IP instances, three factories, one weekly window that must also hold the way back

Northlight serves Qwen3-32B from three AI factories, in Frankfurt, Amsterdam and Madrid. In front of the GPUs sit F5 devices: the BNK data plane at each border, and BIG-IP pairs that, among other jobs, carry the 34.3 GB of FP8 weights from object storage to every model server that starts. Those BIG-IP instances need new software often, because security fixes now arrive faster than they used to. F5's customers describe the same squeeze in three sentences: they want to change whole environments at once, maintenance windows are becoming weekly, and a window is 3 hours including the time to roll back. This lesson follows one such window for a fleet of ten BIG-IP instances managed from F5 Insight. It does the arithmetic of serial against parallel work, moves the slow copying out of the window, walks the readiness gates, upgrades a high availability pair without dropping traffic, deals with an rSeries appliance whose reboot takes every tenant down with it, and keeps a tested way back inside the same three hours.

Chapter 1 of 15

The maintenance window is changing shape

More change, more often, in less time

A maintenance window is the agreed period in which an operator may change production systems and the business accepts that something may briefly break. For years a BIG-IP upgrade was planned device by device, a few times a year, in a long night window. F5's own slide on fleet management lists three things its customers now say. The first is "We are moving towards working on entire environments and not per device." The second is "Maintenance windows are becoming a weekly thing." The third is "We have 3 hours including rollback time." The slide sums them up as more changes per window, more frequent changes and shorter change windows.

The reason is the security calendar. F5's blog on Insight v1.2 describes frontier AI models, and names Claude Mythos as one, that speed up the discovery of vulnerabilities and also the writing of exploits, compressing the time from issue found to issue exploited from weeks to hours. Teams still need to validate a fix before they install it. The gap between a known weakness and an installed fix is what F5 calls the exposure window. Patching becomes a security task that recurs every week.

Read the third quote carefully. Three hours including rollback means that the forward change and the way back must both fit. If going forward takes 2 hours and 50 minutes, there is no time to undo it. At Northlight the window is Thursday from 22:00 to 01:00. The fleet is ten BIG-IP instances in five high availability pairs: two pairs in Frankfurt, two in Amsterdam and one in Madrid. Each pair is two devices that share the same configuration, one Active and carrying traffic, one Standby and ready to take over.

Chapter 2 of 15

Ten devices one at a time take a whole night

The serial arithmetic

The old way of upgrading is serial: one device at a time, by hand, each one finished before the next begins. F5's table of where fleet management came from gives the number. Ten devices upgraded serially took 7.5 hours. That is 450 minutes, or 45 minutes per device on average. The minutes go into steps that each wait for the one before: copying a software image of a few gigabytes to the device, checking it, installing it to a spare disk volume, rebooting into it, waiting for the services and the configuration to load, checking that traffic flows, and for a pair, moving traffic across and repeating everything on the other member.

Put that next to the window. 180 minutes divided by 45 minutes is 4 devices, and that leaves no time at all for a rollback. Keep even one device's worth of time in reserve to undo a failed upgrade and only 3 devices fit. Northlight's ten devices would need three separate windows, or four with a rollback reserve, which with weekly windows means the fleet runs two different versions for two or three weeks. A fleet in mixed versions is harder to support and the fix reaches the last device last.

The table also names the other costs of the old way. Each device needed dozens of error prone inputs: image names, volume names, which member is Active, which commands to run in which order. There were no default readiness checks, so a missing license date or a full disk was found during the window, not before it. And knowing how to upgrade each architecture, such as a high availability pair, lived in the heads of a few engineers. A customer quoted in F5's v2.0 blog puts the cost in people: work that would normally take three to four engineers four hours each.

Chapter 3 of 15

Ten devices at once take one device's time

Parallel batches, and what the deck promises next

The fix for the arithmetic is to stop waiting. If ten devices can be upgraded at the same time, the whole fleet takes about as long as one device. F5's table puts it in three columns. From: 10 devices serially in 7.5 hours. To, today: 10 devices in parallel in 45 minutes. Will be: 10 devices in parallel in about 30 minutes, the star in the slide marking it as a target. 450 minutes against 45 is 10 times faster. Against the 30 minute target it is 15 times faster, and the step from 45 to 30 is a further 1.5 times.

In F5 Insight this is the Parallel (Batch Execution) pattern of a Standalone/Bulk installation job. The job lists its instances in order and has a batch size. The documentation's example is 12 instances with a batch size of 4: Insight upgrades the first 4 together, waits until all of them finish, then starts the next 4. A batch size of 10 for ten devices means one batch. The documented limits in the current release are 100 instances in one installation job and a batch size of at most 50. The scale row of the deck goes further: parallel up to 150 instances now, and up to 250 instances as the next step. Treat 100 and 50 as what one job accepts today and the deck's figures as what Insight runs across jobs and is heading towards.

Parallel has a price, and the documentation's comparison table states it. In a Standalone/Bulk parallel job all the instances in a batch are down at the same time. That is fine when instances are behind a load balancer or otherwise redundant. It is not fine for both members of one pair. So the order of the instances in the job, and how a pair is split between batches or jobs, is the operator's real decision.

Chapter 4 of 15

Move the copying out of the window

Decoupling and pre-staging

Of the minutes a device spends being upgraded, the copy of the image is the one that does not need a window. Nothing changes on the device while a file lands in its disk. F5's table calls the change "decoupling and pre-staging": the copy is a separate job, run days before, and the window only installs and reboots. The documentation splits the work the same way. A software distribution job copies images from Insight to the instances. A software installation job installs from images that are already there, and its readiness checks block an instance whose image is missing.

It starts with the image store. An operator downloads the ISO from my.f5.com and uploads it to Insight with its validation files. For TMOS that is a .pem certificate and a .sig signature, so the image can be proven to come from F5. For F5OS-A it is a .sha512 checksum file. Insight holds up to 50 GB of images in total, takes up to 10 files and 10 GB in one upload, and also ingests files copied by scp into /opt/f5insight/data/fleet-images/incoming/. The documentation's own example file is BIGIP-17.5.1.11-0.0.12.iso, and that is the image Northlight distributes.

The distribution job then pushes it. Its limits are 100 instances per job, up to 20 in parallel, up to 2 ISO files and 10 GB per job, and 2 distribution jobs running at once with a third one queued. On the BIG-IP the file arrives in /shared/images, the directory tmsh installs from. Disk space is checked per instance first, and an instance without room shows "insufficient space for distribution". That is where the cleanup from Insight v2.0 comes in. With Remove unused images ticked, the job deletes from each instance every image that is neither the running version nor part of this distribution, and the Cleanup Preview lists them before anything is deleted.

Chapter 5 of 15

Gates before anything changes

Readiness checks that block, and ones that only warn

The table's confidence row moves from no default readiness checks to checks built in for TMOS and F5OS, and then to customizable pre and post checks. In an installation job the operator selects Run preview checks before the window. Each instance comes back Ready or Failed. A blocking failure greys out Execute Job until the instance is removed from the job or the problem is fixed and the check run again. A warning is shown and does not stop the job.

For TMOS there are eight checks. Image Compatibility is blocking: a TMOS image goes on a BIG-IP and an F5OS-A image on an rSeries appliance. HA Sync Status is a warning that the pair's configurations match, for pair jobs only. Software Image Presence is blocking, and is where a skipped distribution is caught. Disk Space blocks when a new volume cannot be created and warns when installing over an existing one. Install Config warns when the target version is older than the running one. Upgrade Path is blocking and follows F5's list of supported upgrade paths, article K13845. License Compatibility is blocking. License Date is blocking: the instance's Service Check Date has to be later than the License Check Date of the new version, as article K7727 explains, or the licence will not let the new version run. The documentation adds that in practice this check only affects major or minor version upgrades, so a patch such as 17.5.1.10 to 17.5.1.11 passes it.

The Install Config warning deserves its own sentence. The BIG-IP tool cpcfg copies the running configuration onto the new volume, and it refuses to copy it to an older version. A downgrade therefore boots with a blank configuration: no virtual servers, no pools, and possibly no management settings, so Insight may lose the device and recovery may need the console. On Tuesday, two days ahead, Northlight runs the checks. bigip-ams-2b fails Software Image Presence: Monday's distribution did not reach it, because it was being rebooted for other work when the copy ran. Re-running the distribution for that one instance on Tuesday costs nothing. Finding it at 23:10 would have cost the window.

Chapter 6 of 15

The new version goes on the other volume

Boot volumes, cpcfg, and a job that can run every week

A BIG-IP keeps more than one complete installation of its software on disk. Each is a boot volume, named HD1.1, HD1.2, HD1.3 and so on, and exactly one is active at a time. The tmsh reference states the rule plainly: you cannot install software on the active volume. An install goes to an inactive volume while the device keeps running from the active one, and only the reboot moves it across. By hand that is install sys software image BIGIP-17.5.1.11-0.0.12.iso volume HD1.2, with create-volume added if HD1.2 does not exist yet, show sys software status to watch progress, and reboot volume HD1.2 to switch. The old version stays intact on HD1.1. That is what makes a fast rollback possible later.

Insight asks for a Set Target Volume rule instead of a volume name per device, which removes one of the error prone inputs. First Inactive (Numerically) picks the lowest numbered inactive volume. Last Inactive (Numerically) picks the highest. Next Sequential (New) creates a new volume one past the last install, HD1.3 after HD1.2. With Install Config on, the default, cpcfg copies the running configuration from the active volume onto the target volume during the reboot, so the device comes up on the new version with today's configuration.

The documentation shows why First Inactive is the rule for a recurring cadence. Its example uses two releases it calls Hardened Release 1 and Hardened Release 2, HR 1 and HR 2 for short. The first run installs HR 1 to HD1.2 while HD1.1 is active. A couple of weeks later the same job, with only the target version changed, installs HR 2 to HD1.1, because HD1.2 is now the active one. The two volumes take turns and the job is reused unchanged. In Northlight's terms, run 1 is this Thursday's 17.5.1.11 and run 2 is whatever next week's window brings, so at a weekly cadence the volumes swap every week. A BIG-IP with only one boot location cannot be upgraded this way at all, which is why single-slot instances are out of scope.

Chapter 7 of 15

A pair is upgraded standby first

Traffic keeps flowing through the member that is not being touched

On the factory floor the BIG-IP in front of object storage is the pair bigip-fra-1a and bigip-fra-1b. Model servers pull their 34.3 GB of FP8 weights through it whenever one starts, as the lesson on the road to the weights shows. Upgrading it must not stop those downloads. An HA pair installation job in Insight does five things in a fixed order. It finds out which member is Active and which is Standby. It upgrades the Standby. Once that member is back and healthy, it fails over, so the upgraded member becomes Active. It upgrades the formerly Active member, now Standby. It runs final health checks on both. Traffic always has one member that is not being worked on.

By hand the failover is run sys failover standby on the Active member, which hands the Active role to its peer. Insight adds options around it. Disable Auto Config-Sync stops configuration changes on the Active member from syncing to the half-upgraded Standby, and is turned back on at the end. Automatic failback returns the Active role to the original member after both are done, and Pause before failback lets someone look first. Four human-in-the-loop pauses can be placed along the way. The documentation's recommended pattern sets two: after the Standby reboots, before failover, to check it can carry traffic, and after failover, before the second install, to check the upgraded member is carrying it well.

This is the same idea as a Kubernetes rolling update in the lesson on replacing a model server: never remove the last copy that is serving. If the Standby upgrade fails, or the new software misbehaves while the upgraded member is Active, the job is paused or aborted before the second member is touched, and one member still runs the old version.

Chapter 8 of 15

Choosing how the fleet is grouped into jobs

HA pair jobs, serial bulk jobs and parallel bulk jobs

Insight has two installation types and the choice decides both speed and availability. An HA pair job runs its pairs one at a time, two devices each, with failover keeping traffic up. A Standalone/Bulk job upgrades each instance on its own, with no automatic failover, either Serial (Rolling Execution), one after another with optional pauses between instances, or Parallel (Batch Execution), with all instances in a batch down at once. The comparison table in the documentation sums it up. HA pair: traffic maintained via failover, moderate speed. Serial bulk: downtime per instance, slow. Parallel bulk: all instances down together, fast, best for bulk upgrades in a maintenance window.

There is a trap in the HA pair type. One job with five pairs runs them serially, a pair at a time, up to 50 pairs per job. To get the parallel speed of F5's 45 minute figure with pairs, the documentation says to create one job per pair and run the jobs at the same time. Northlight builds five HA pair jobs and starts them together.

Insight 1.2.2 added a second way. Before it, a Standalone/Bulk job refused any instance whose role was Active or Standby. Since then a bulk job accepts all roles and simply does not fail over. The documented example is to upgrade 10 Standby instances in one job and 10 Active instances in a second job, and to fail each pair over in between on the operator's own schedule. For a whole environment that can be faster still, because each job is one parallel batch. Active/Active setups with traffic groups are outside what the HA pair type supports in this release. Whichever grouping is used, the deck's change from per device to whole environment is this: the unit of work is a job, and a job carries its own order, pauses and checks.

Chapter 9 of 15

Under the tenants: F5OS on rSeries

One reboot takes every tenant on the appliance down

Madrid's pair is different. bigip-mad-1a and bigip-mad-1b are tenants: BIG-IP instances running as guests on two rSeries appliances, rseries-mad-1 and rseries-mad-2. The appliance runs its own platform software, F5OS-A, and that needs updates too. Insight treats an rSeries appliance as an instance of its own, always with the Standalone/Bulk type, because there is no platform level pair to fail over. A job installs one target version, so TMOS instances and F5OS-A appliances go in separate jobs.

The appliance does not have boot volumes. It installs the target version and boots straight into it, and install and reboot are one request. Before the job, Insight runs the appliance's own check of the target version, F5OS Check Version, which returns whether the version can be installed, an estimated duration, and the number of reboots. Two or more reboots means a platform firmware upgrade of BIOS, CPLD or FPGA follows the software, with a second reboot. F5OS-A does not report a percentage, so progress is estimated from the predicted duration. Its readiness list differs: Software Image Presence in the appliance's own catalogue, Disk Space on the images partition, Config Backup no more than 7 days old, Cluster, Cores, Service Pods, FPGA Status, Tenant Status, which blocks on a tenant stuck pending, and Version Support against F5's hardware matrix.

The important line is the warning. Rebooting an rSeries appliance affects every BIG-IP tenant on it. Insight today does not move, drain or fail over those tenants. The operator plans it: upgrade first the appliance that hosts the Standby tenants. Here that is rseries-mad-2, under bigip-mad-1b. The deck's next column, chain F5OS and tenant installations, is the step that joins the two jobs into one ordered run. Madrid is also the one site with a single pair, so for the appliance work the plan disables Madrid's member of the GSLB pool eu-factories, and callers are sent to Frankfurt or Amsterdam, as the lesson on one name and three factories describes. The tenant upgrade itself does not need this. bigip-mad-1a and bigip-mad-1b are upgraded by an HA pair job like every other pair, so one of them always carries traffic. The appliance work gets a window of its own, the week after the tenants.

Chapter 10 of 15

Proof of what changed

Pre and post flight snapshots, and a typed confirmation

A fleet upgrade that reports success on every device can still have broken something. A trunk that did not come back, a pool member that is now down, a virtual server that stayed offline. Insight records the state of each instance before the change and again after it, and shows the two side by side. These are the pre and post flight snapshots. For TMOS the operator picks the core checks to capture: trunk status, interface status, virtual server state, pool health, pool member health and the output of tmsh show sys performance all-stats. For F5OS-A the set is interface status, LAG status, alarms, health summary, cluster, firmware status, FPGA status, cores, tenant status and service pods. If one item cannot be collected, the error is recorded and the rest of the snapshot is kept, so a missing counter does not fail the job.

On the factory floor the comparison that matters is simple. Before the window, the storage virtual server on bigip-fra-1a was available and its pool had 40 storage nodes up. After the window, the same virtual server on bigip-fra-1b must show the same 40 nodes up. Any difference is a finding before anyone calls the window closed. The deck's next step is customizable pre and post checks, where an operator adds checks of their own, such as a real request through the virtual server.

The input row of the deck moves from dozens of error prone inputs to single-click execution, and next to an upgrade advisor that guides the choice of version and path. Single click still has a safety catch. To run an installation the operator must type BEGIN INSTALLATION in the confirmation box, and may enter a change request number, which stays with the execution record. The change board's ticket and the job that ran are linked.

Chapter 11 of 15

The way back fits in the window

Rollback is a reboot into the volume the device came from

Rollback is cheap on a BIG-IP because the old version was never removed. It still sits on the previous boot volume. Insight v2.0 adds a Rollback button to a finished installation execution. For a BIG-IP it switchboots the device back to the volume it ran before the install. For an F5OS-A appliance it runs f5-system-rollback on the appliance. Eligibility follows from that: only devices that reached the REBOOT phase, or later, can be rolled back. A device that failed during INSTALL or VALIDATION never left its old volume and has nothing to undo.

The rollback inherits the original run's HA sequencing, pauses and failover settings. For a pair that means the Standby is rolled back first, then the Active after a controlled failover, the same choreography as the upgrade in reverse. The operator selects devices, as pairs for an HA job or one by one for a bulk job, acknowledges that configuration changes made since the installation will be lost, and types BEGIN ROLLBACK. Over the REST API it is POST /api/v1/fleet/upgrade/jobs/software-installation/{jobId}/rollback with an empty body to roll back every eligible device. The answer is 202 Accepted with an execution of type ROLLBACK. A rollback cannot itself be rolled back. To go forward again, the job is run again.

For one device there is also Boot Other Volume, which reboots a BIG-IP into any complete volume, backward or forward. F5's example response shows a reboot task from HD1.1 to HD1.2 started at 19:00:05 and completed at 19:03:12, a little over three minutes for the task. Now the budget, as an estimate. Upgrading the fleet in parallel took 45 minutes. Rolling back has no image copy and no install, only a reboot per member, but it inherits the original run's pauses and failover steps, so it is not shown to be faster than the upgrade. This lesson budgets the same 45 minutes for it. That figure is illustrative, and the way to know it is to roll back one pair in a test and time it. With checks before and after, the whole plan then uses about two of the three hours. That is how three hours including rollback becomes possible.

Chapter 12 of 15

A backup taken before, and on a schedule

UCS archives, the master key, and restore options for a replaced device

Rollback undoes a software change. It does not help when a device dies, when a configuration change goes wrong, or when hardware is replaced. That is the job of backups. F5's v1.2 blog already speaks of backup and restore workflows in the Insight UI, and v2.0 brings backup and restore of BIG-IP and F5OS instances into fleet management, with manual or scheduled triggers, which is the feature this chapter describes. A BIG-IP backup is a UCS archive, the User Configuration Set, the full configuration of the device. By hand it is save sys ucs, and the file goes to /var/local/ucs. An F5OS-A backup holds the appliance's configuration and system state.

A backup job has a name, an optional schedule and an execution type: serial, or parallel with a batch size from 2 to 20. For a BIG-IP it can include private keys, validate the archive after it is made, and encrypt it with a passphrase that Insight does not keep. Retain and Encrypt Master Key stores the BIG-IP master key in Insight's vault. Without that key, encrypted objects such as TLS private keys cannot be decrypted when the archive is loaded onto a different or rebuilt device. The retention policy keeps backups forever, for a period, or as the last N copies, and a remote target on NFS or SMB keeps them off the appliance. A UCS can reach 1 to 2 GB, so the readiness check warns when /var on the device or Insight's own disk is short of space. A backup can also be part of the installation job itself.

Restore loads a chosen archive onto the original device or another one. For a replaced unit there are three switches. platform-migrate adapts the configuration to different hardware. keep-current-management-ip keeps the new unit's management address. no-license keeps the new unit's own licence. platform-migrate cannot be combined with either of the other two, so a restore onto different hardware brings the management address and licence from the archive. An F5OS-A restore always starts with a clean reset that deletes all tenants and users, and needs the Primary Key and its salt. Northlight schedules a daily backup at 12:00 UTC with the last 7 copies kept, so Thursday's window starts with a backup less than half a day old.

Chapter 13 of 15

Licences are part of the upgrade

A minor version can need a reactivated licence before it will run

A BIG-IP licence carries a Service Check Date. Each software version has a License Check Date. If the device's date is older than the version's date, the new version will not run on that licence until it is reactivated, which is what F5 article K7727 describes and what the License Date readiness check tests. The installation documentation says that in practice this only affects major or minor version upgrades, so patches inside one version line pass it. A minor upgrade, such as 17.1.x to 17.5.x, which F5's v2.0 blog and the fleet management overview list as supported from v2.0, can fail it. Insight therefore handles reactivation inside the installation job, and the default setting, Conditionally re-activate instances, reactivates only when the licence has expired or the dates require it. The other two settings are to reactivate every instance or none.

Reactivation goes through Insight. It asks the device for a dossier, a file that identifies the device and its keys, sends the dossier to F5's licensing server, accepts the end user licence agreement if the server asks, receives the licence text, applies it to the device and confirms the licence is active. That needs Insight to reach the internet. In a closed site, like the one in the lesson on a cloud with no way out, the manual method is used: Insight produces the dossier, a person takes it to the licensing portal and pastes the licence text back. Reactivating causes a brief traffic interruption, and on an F5OS host every tenant feels it.

For BIG-IP Virtual Edition, Insight v2.0 also keeps registration key pools: named sets of keys that can be assigned to a device, moved to another, reactivated or revoked from one page. Revoking a licence stops the device processing traffic, so it is done only to a device out of service. Deployment matters too. The deck offers Insight on-premises and air-gapped, on-premises connected to SaaS, and as a hosted service. It lists full fleet management and operations for the air-gapped model, centralized fleet management for the hosted one, and local control with fleet observability for the SaaS-connected one.

Chapter 14 of 15

The whole week, scheduled

Put every slow step before the window and keep the window for the change

Here is Northlight's week, end to end. Monday: the image and its validation files are uploaded, and a distribution job copies BIGIP-17.5.1.11-0.0.12.iso to all ten instances, cleaning out unused images on the way. Tuesday: the installation jobs are built and their preview checks run. The copy that missed bigip-ams-2b is re-run, and one licence is reactivated ahead of next quarter's minor upgrade. Every day at 12:00 UTC: the scheduled backup runs. Thursday 22:00: the window opens.

22:00 to 22:10: the preview checks are run once more, because a day can change things. 22:10: five HA pair jobs are executed together. Madrid stays in GSLB answers, because its tenant pair is upgraded like the others and one member always carries traffic. Each job upgrades its Standby member, pauses, fails over, pauses, upgrades the other member and runs its final checks. By 22:55, 45 minutes later, all ten instances run the new version. 22:55 to 23:25: the post flight snapshots are compared, a real model server is restarted in each factory to prove it can still fetch its 34.3 GB of weights through the storage pair. 23:25: the window has 95 minutes left, about twice the 45 minutes this lesson estimates for a rollback. The change is closed at 23:30, with 90 minutes of the window unused.

The rSeries platform update is not in this window. It goes into the following week's window on its own, the appliance under the Standby tenant first, because every tenant reboots with it. That is the window in which Madrid's GSLB pool member is disabled and enabled again. The deck's final column puts this whole week into scheduled jobs and operations. Then the distribution, the backups and even the window's installation start on a timer, and a person is needed only at the pauses.

Compare this with the serial night: four devices and no rollback reserve. The difference does not come from faster reboots. Copying, checking and licensing moved out of the window, and the devices that can be touched together are touched together.

Chapter 15 of 15

What customers reported

The results F5 has published, and how to read them

F5's public v2.0 blog, "Beyond visibility", reports one customer result and one lab result. The customer is a large financial services company that updated dozens of BIG-IP instances faster and with fewer engineers than before. According to that customer's feedback, one engineer using Insight did in 2 hours what would normally take three to four engineers four hours each. F5's v1.2 blog gives the background to why such a customer patches often: frontier AI models, and it names Claude Mythos as one, speed up the finding of vulnerabilities and the writing of exploits, and organisations are absorbing faster hardened release cadences as a result. That is the reason weekly windows appeared in the first place.

Read the customer quote two ways. On the clock, 4 hours became 2, so the work finished twice as fast. In people, 3 engineers for 4 hours is 12 engineer hours and 4 engineers for 4 hours is 16, and that became 1 engineer for 2 hours, 2 engineer hours. That is six to eight times less work. The two ratios come from the same sentence and measure different things: elapsed time, and the effort of the team. A change board cares about the first, because it decides whether the work fits the window. A manager cares about the second, because it decides who else is free that night.

F5's own lab gives a bigger figure. A full update cycle across 100 BIG-IP devices, including pre-flight checks, execution and post-flight verification, took under 90 minutes. F5 calls that a long lunch break instead of a lost weekend. 100 devices in 90 minutes is under a minute per device of window time, against 45 per device serially. None of these numbers will be yours. Reboot times differ between Virtual Edition and hardware, between versions, and with the size of the configuration. Measure one pair, then size the batches from the measurement.

What to keep

The window holds the change, and the way back

A three-hour weekly window cannot hold a serial upgrade of ten devices, which takes 7.5 hours. It can hold a parallel one with time to spare, if everything slow is done before it. The image is uploaded, checked against its signature and copied to each device days early, and old images are cleaned off to make room. Readiness gates catch a wrong image, a full disk, an unsupported path or a stale licence while there is still time to fix them. Inside the window each pair is upgraded standby first, so one member always carries traffic, including the model servers' downloads of their weights. The new version goes on a second boot volume, so going back is a reboot into the first. An rSeries appliance reboots all its tenants, so it is done on its own, under the Standby tenant first. Snapshots before and after prove what changed. Backups, licences and the schedule make the next week's window the same as this one.
