GPU and hardware

Three numbers on a GPU card

Compute, memory size and the road between them

A GPU card is sold on three numbers. How much arithmetic its cores can do in a second. How many gigabytes of memory sit on the card. And how fast bytes can move between that memory and the cores. Most people look at the first two. For a language model that is writing an answer, the third one sets the speed. This lesson opens gpu-2 and follows the bytes. You will see why a model has to live in the memory on the card, what happens if it lives in the server's ordinary RAM instead, why a full batch slows each user while the card as a whole makes many times more tokens, and why storing weights in fewer bits makes a model both smaller and faster. It starts from the round trip every computer makes between its processor and its memory, then follows a model whose memory size and speed come apart, a mixture of experts, and ends with how a platform of several sites puts small models where most of the requests are and stores them in as few as 4 bits.

Chapter 1 of 14

One card, three numbers

And a server around it with numbers of its own

A request reaches gpu-2. Open the worker and there are two computers on one motherboard. The host has CPUs and ordinary memory, DDR5 in slots. The GPU is an H100 in its SXM form, a module on a board of its own, with its own cores and its own memory soldered right beside them. It reaches the host over a PCIe Gen5 link of 16 lanes, the same link a card in a PCIe slot gets, and this lesson calls that link the slot. The H100 also comes as a PCIe card, with lower numbers: about 756 TFLOPS and about 2,000 GB a second. That memory is called HBM, short for high bandwidth memory, and people also say VRAM. The host CPU does not compute from it. It can reach that memory only across the slot, through a narrow window this lesson opens later, at a small fraction of the card's own speed.

Underneath all three numbers is one picture that every computer shares. The processor and the memory are two separate parts, and a road called a bus joins them. Take 6 × 7 in the simplest picture of a computer. Both numbers sit in memory, the RAM. The CPU reads them across the bus into its registers, the handful of storage cells inside a core. It multiplies them, and it writes the result, 42, back into memory across the same bus. In that picture every calculation is a round trip: read, compute, write. Real processors keep many numbers in registers and caches between steps, yet each number they work on still has to cross the bus from memory at least once. Stored as ordinary 64-bit numbers, the 6 and the 7 are 8 bytes each and the 42 is 8 more, so one multiply moves 24 bytes over the bus.

The multiply is the quick part. A core running at a few gigahertz finishes it in about a nanosecond, while a trip out to DRAM and back takes on the order of a hundred. CPUs hide that wait with caches, small fast memories on the processor chip that keep recently used numbers close to the cores. A model's weights do not fit in any cache. The largest server processors carry about a gigabyte of cache, most far less, and this model's weights are 32.8 GB. So every pass goes all the way out to memory and back, and the speed of that road decides how fast the model writes. Read from the host's DRAM at 307 GB a second, one pass over 32.8 GB takes 107 milliseconds. A GPU keeps the same pairing of a processor and its memory. It packs the two tightly together on one board, right beside each other, so that the road between them can be very wide. Over that road the same 32.8 GB takes 9.8 milliseconds.

Three roads carry bytes here, and they are very unequal. From the card's memory to its cores: about 3,350 GB every second. From the host's memory to its CPUs: about 307, which is eight channels of DDR5-4800 at 38.4 GB a second each. Across the PCIe slot between the two computers: about 64, from 16 lanes at 32 gigabits a second each. The widest road is 52 times the narrowest.

The cores are the first number. A server CPU does somewhere between 5 and 10 trillion operations a second, written TFLOPS. The H100 in gpu-2 does 990 on 16-bit numbers, about a hundred times more, because it has thousands of small cores that all multiply at once. That many cores have to be fed, and the rest of this lesson is about what feeds them. Vendor sheets sometimes print 1,979 TFLOPS for the same card. That figure assumes a sparse model in which half the weights are zero, so 990 is the one to compare.

Chapter 2 of 14

First question: does it fit?

Weights first, then whatever is left for conversations

Memory size is the number that says yes or no. There is no swapping to disk on a GPU. A model that does not fit does not start. The weights come first. Qwen3-32B has 32.8 billion of them, and this factory stores each in one byte, so they take 32.8 GB of the 80. This lesson counts every weight at exactly one byte, which keeps the sums clean. The real Qwen3-32B-FP8 build the factory serves is a little larger, 34.3 GB of files, because some layers stay at higher precision and the 8-bit weights carry small scale factors. What is left is for the KV cache, the working memory of the conversations being served. Each token of context costs 262,144 bytes there. So the second number of the card turns directly into how much conversation it can hold at once: about 180,000 tokens.

That figure is the best case. A model server does not hand the whole card to weights and cache. vLLM takes a fraction of the card set by --gpu-memory-utilization, loads the weights, measures what a pass needs for its own working values, and cuts the remainder into KV cache blocks. At a setting of 0.9 the budget is 72 GB. Take away 32.8 GB of weights and 39.2 GB is left, room for about 149,500 tokens before the working values are counted. The other 8 GB stays free for the driver and anything else on the card.

The failure is loud and early. If the weights alone exceed the budget, the process stops during start with a CUDA out of memory error and the Pod never becomes Ready. If the weights fit and too little is left for one request at the model's full context length, vLLM also refuses to start and says so. A card that is too small is found in the first minutes. A card that is only just big enough is found later, as a queue.

Chapter 3 of 14

Where 262,144 bytes a token comes from

Layers, heads and the width of a head

The KV cache holds what attention needs from earlier tokens. Every layer of the model turns each token into two small sets of numbers, a key and a value, and later tokens read them instead of working them out again. So the cache for one token has a fixed shape: layers, times two, times the number of key and value heads, times the width of a head. Only the count of tokens grows as a conversation gets longer.

Qwen3-32B has 64 layers. Each layer keeps 8 key and value heads of 128 numbers each. That is 64 × 2 × 8 × 128 = 131,072 numbers for one token, and at 16 bits a number it is 262,144 bytes, exactly 256 KiB. A conversation of 2,000 tokens holds 524 MB. One of 24,000 tokens holds 6.3 GB. The figures come from the model's config.json, where the fields are num_hidden_layers, num_key_value_heads and head_dim.

The 8 is a design choice called grouped-query attention. The model has 64 attention heads for asking, and they share 8 sets of keys and values. With a separate set for each of the 64 heads a token would cost 2,097,152 bytes, eight times as much, and the 47.2 GB beside the weights would hold about 22,500 tokens where it now holds 180,000. A smaller model without this sharing shows the same arithmetic: 32 layers, 32 heads and 128 numbers per head come to 524,288 bytes a token, about 2.1 GB for a 4,000-token conversation.

Chapter 4 of 14

Every token reads every weight

Why the third number sets the speed

Now the model writes. Each new token needs one pass through all 64 layers, and a pass uses every weight once. The weights do not sit in the cores. They are streamed out of GPU memory, through the cores, on every single pass. The arithmetic done on each weight as it goes by is small, and the cores finish it faster than memory can deliver the next bytes. So the cores wait on memory.

That makes the ceiling simple division. 3,350 GB a second, over 32.8 GB a pass, is 102 passes a second. No amount of extra compute raises that number. Only a wider road or a smaller model does. Turned around, it is 9.8 milliseconds between one token and the next. Benchmarks call that gap time per output token, or inter-token latency. A 200-token answer is 200 passes, close to two seconds of reading the same 32.8 GB over and over. This phase is called decode, and the streaming you see in a chat window is each pass finishing.

The cores have little to do while this happens. A pass for one token costs roughly two operations per weight, one multiply and one add, so about 65.5 billion operations. At 102 passes a second that is 6.7 TFLOPS, under one percent of the 990 the card can do. The usual gauge hides this. nvidia-smi reports GPU utilization as the share of time in which any program was running on the card, and during decode one always is, so it reads near 100 percent while the cores are mostly waiting for bytes.

Chapter 5 of 14

Reading the prompt is the other case

Prefill keeps the cores busy, so compute is the limit

Before the first token of an answer, the model has to read the whole prompt. The prompt is already there in full, so every token of it goes through the 64 layers together in one pass. The weights are read from memory once, 32.8 GB in 9.8 milliseconds, and are used thousands of times on the way through. Now the arithmetic is the large part and the cores are busy. This phase is called prefill, and the wait it causes is the time to first token.

The same rule of thumb gives its size. A 2,000-token prompt costs 2,000 × 65.5 billion operations, which is 131 trillion. At 990 TFLOPS that is 132 milliseconds at best, against 9.8 for moving the bytes. So here the first number on the card is the limit and the third hardly matters. On an A100, at 312 TFLOPS, the same prompt takes 420 milliseconds. On an H200 it takes 132 again, because the H200 has the H100's cores.

Prefill leaves something behind: the keys and values of every prompt token, written into the KV cache. Decode then adds one token at a time and never reads the prompt again. On one card the two phases take turns, so a long prompt delays everyone who is in the middle of an answer. A 24,000-token prompt is 1.6 seconds of compute at best. Model servers limit the damage by cutting a long prompt into chunks and letting decode passes run between them, which vLLM calls chunked prefill.

Chapter 6 of 14

Why the model cannot live in system RAM

The slot is 52 times too narrow

The server has far more ordinary memory than the card has HBM, often a terabyte. It is tempting to leave the weights there. Follow the bytes. To reach the GPU's cores from host memory they have to cross the PCIe slot, at about 64 GB a second. One pass would take half a second, so the model would write two tokens a second.

This is the whole reason GPU memory is priced the way it is. The card's memory is not special because it is large. It is special because of the road that leaves it. PCIe is for loading the model once and for moving cache blocks in and out. It is never in the path of a pass.

Two other shortcuts fail for the same reason. The host can reach GPU memory through a window the card exposes on the bus, called BAR1. Reads through it are uncached, and NVIDIA's GDRCopy library measures them at about 0.9 GB a second. Writes do better when the window is mapped write-combining, which lets the processor merge many small stores into larger PCIe writes. GDRCopy measures about 22 GB a second for writes that way on its test machine, and 6 to 8 GB a second on an older Ivy Bridge one. Even at 22 GB a second, 32.8 GB would take about 1.5 seconds for a single pass, slower than the slot's own copy engine. Running the model on the host CPUs avoids the slot altogether. Then the limit is the host's own memory road, 307 GB a second, which is 9 passes a second at best. A desktop with two memory channels has about 90 GB a second and manages fewer than 3.

Chapter 7 of 14

What the slot is for

Loading once, and moving cache blocks

The slot does two jobs, and neither is inside a pass. The first is loading. When a Pod starts, the weights travel from storage into host memory and then across the slot into GPU memory. The host does not push them. It describes each copy, and a copy engine on the card pulls the bytes over with direct memory access. Measured on an H100 PCIe card, which has the same Gen5 x16 link to its host as the SXM module, large copies from the card to the host run at about 55 GB a second, a little under the 64 the link could carry. This lesson assumes copies into the card run at about the same speed. At that rate 32.8 GB crosses in 0.6 seconds. The real FP8 build, 34.3 GB of files, crosses in about the same 0.6 seconds. The rest of the three minutes a load takes is storage, the file system and the server's own start.

The second job is moving KV cache. A conversation that has gone quiet can be copied out to host memory to free room on the card, and copied back when its next turn arrives. The pieces are small. 16 tokens of this model's cache come to 4 MiB, and a 4 MiB copy measured about 130 microseconds. vLLM groups tokens into blocks, 64 tokens a block by default in current source and 16 in older releases. It keeps a separate cache tensor for each layer, with keys and values apart, so 16 tokens are not one 4 MiB piece in memory. They are 64 KiB in each of the 64 layers, 32 KiB of keys and 32 KiB of values, and a real offload moves many such pieces. A whole 2,000-token conversation is 524 MB and comes back in about 10 milliseconds. That is far cheaper than the 132 milliseconds of prefill it replaces.

Small copies pay a fixed cost. Each one carries about 15 microseconds of overhead for submitting the command, waiting for the completion signal and waking up. That is 11 percent of a 4 MiB copy and 1 percent of a 64 MiB one. Measured speed rises from about 31 GB a second at 4 MiB to 55 at 256 MiB, and the overhead explains only part of that. At 55 GB a second the 4 MiB would take 76 microseconds, and 15 more would give about 46 GB a second. The series these measurements come from says the overhead does not explain the whole gap, and leaves the rest open. A benchmark that moves a cache in tiny pieces measures the overhead and says little about the link.

Chapter 8 of 14

A batch shares the road

One read of the weights serves everyone, but their caches ride along

One pass can produce a token for many requests at once. The weights go by once and every request in the batch uses them. That is why a GPU server is efficient at all. But each request also reads its own KV cache during the pass, and those bytes travel the same road.

Take 32 conversations of 2,000 tokens. Their caches add up to 16.8 GB, read on every pass on top of the weights. The pass is now 49.6 GB and the ceiling drops from 102 passes a second to 67. Each user gets 67 tokens a second where a lone user had 102. The card as a whole produces 2,162. Bandwidth is the thing they are sharing.

The trade is lopsided in the server's favour. Going from 1 user to 32 costs each of them a third of their speed and multiplies the card's output by 21. At 8 users each gets about 91 tokens a second, 90.6 before rounding, and the card makes 724. At 90 users, which fills the memory, each gets 42 and the card makes 3,769. The compute is still far from its limit: 2,162 tokens a second is about 142 TFLOPS of the 990. Without batching the whole model would be read out of memory for one person at a time, and nobody could afford to run the hardware that way.

Chapter 9 of 14

When the room runs out

Memory, and no shortage of arithmetic, caps the users on a card

Every request in the batch needs its own KV cache on the card for as long as it is being served. The weights take their 32.8 GB whatever happens, so the 47.2 GB beside them is all the room there is. Ninety conversations of 2,000 tokens fill it. The ninety-first has nowhere to put its first token. It waits in the server's queue, or the server throws out someone else's cache to make room and that conversation pays for a new prefill later. Memory runs out long before arithmetic does, which is why free GPU memory decides how many users one card serves.

The server reports this in its metrics. vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc is the share of cache blocks in use, from 0 to 1. vllm:num_requests_running is the size of the batch, and vllm:num_requests_waiting is the queue. vllm:inter_token_latency_seconds shows the other effect of a full card, the slower pass: about 24 milliseconds between tokens with 90 conversations in the batch, against 9.8 for the weights alone.

This is the signal the endpoint picker reads. It scrapes each model server's metrics about every 50 milliseconds. With the upstream defaults of the Gateway API Inference Extension, a server is out of room once its cache usage is above 0.8 or more than 5 requests wait in its queue. An operator can change both thresholds. gpu-2, at 1.0 with one request waiting, is well past the first and still under the second. A new conversation is then sent to gpu-1, where one block in four is in use, and does not join the queue on gpu-2. A router that only counts connections cannot see any of this. To it a card with 90 long conversations and a card with 90 short ones look the same.

Chapter 10 of 14

Fewer bits: smaller and faster

Quantization moves two of the three numbers

Weights are trained as 16-bit numbers. Stored that way, as the official BF16 build, this model is 65.5 GB. It fits in 80, but only 14.5 GB is left for conversations and each pass reads twice as many bytes, so the ceiling is 51 tokens a second. Storing each weight in 8 bits is what this factory does. The model halves, the room for KV cache triples and the ceiling doubles. At 4 bits it halves again.

This is called quantization, and it is the one change that helps on memory size and on bandwidth together. What it costs is precision. Each weight is rounded to one of fewer values: 65,536 possible values at 16 bits, 256 at 8 and 16 at 4. Good methods choose the rounding with care and lose little, and below 8 bits the answers start to show it. The usual formats are an 8-bit floating point called FP8 and several 4-bit ones. Vendor planning figures put the 4-bit format NVFP4 at about four times the throughput of full precision on the same hardware. Counted at a flat 4 bits a weight, the division here gives the same factor: 204 against 51. NVFP4 also stores one shared scale for every 16 weights, which adds half a bit to each, as the last chapter shows. At 4.5 bits this model is 18.4 GB and its ceiling is 182, about 3.6 times.

The cache can be shrunk the same way. vLLM's --kv-cache-dtype fp8 stores keys and values in 8 bits, so a token costs 131,072 bytes and the same 47.2 GB holds about 360,000 tokens. Sizes for other models follow from one line: parameters times bytes per weight. A 7 billion parameter model is 14 GB at 16 bits, 7 GB at 8 and 3.5 GB at 4. A 70 billion parameter model is 140 GB at 16 bits, more than an H100 holds and all but the last gigabyte of an H200.

Chapter 11 of 14

A model bigger than one card

Slice inside a layer in one box, by layers between boxes

Some models do not fit however they are stored. Take a model with 70 billion weights at 16 bits: 140 GB, against 80 on an H100. The answer is to slice it across cards. Two H100s hold 160 GB between them, so the weights fit and 20 GB is left for KV cache. That is thin, which is why such a model usually runs on four cards or is stored in 8 bits, 70 GB, on one.

There are two ways to slice. The first splits inside each layer: each card holds half of every layer's weights and the two halves of a result are combined at every layer. vLLM does this with --tensor-parallel-size 2. Each card then reads its own 70 GB per pass over its own road, so the ceiling is 3,350 ÷ 70 = 48 passes a second, where one road carrying all 140 GB would give 24. The cards exchange results at every layer, so inside one server they are joined by NVLink, 900 GB a second per card on the SXM H100. The PCIe card instead takes an NVLink bridge between pairs of cards, at 600 GB a second. This is how most large models run.

The second way splits by layers. One machine holds the first stretch of layers and passes a single small result to the machine that holds the next. The traffic is light, so it works across the ordinary network between servers. The rule is to keep the chatty split inside a box and the light one between boxes. Kubernetes runs a model spread over several Pods as one unit with a LeaderWorkerSet. Either way a second card is a second full set of the numbers on this lesson's label: its own memory, its own road, its own cores.

Chapter 12 of 14

Holding 35 billion weights, reading 3 billion

A mixture of experts pulls memory size and bandwidth apart

Every model so far in this lesson has been dense. Dense means that each token uses every weight, which is why a pass reads all 32.8 GB. A mixture of experts model, MoE for short, breaks that link. In each layer the feed-forward block, the part that holds most of the weights, is replaced by many smaller blocks called experts. A small layer called the router looks at each token and picks a few experts for it. The other experts in that layer do nothing for that token, and their weights are not read. So the model holds many weights, while each token reads only a few of them.

The lab behind this site runs one, served by vLLM: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-FP8. The name carries both numbers. 35B is the total count of weights and A3B means about 3 billion active for each token. Its model card lists 40 layers with 256 experts in each. The router picks 8 of them for every token, and one shared expert runs for every token as well, so 9 experts work in each layer. One expert is small: three matrices of 2,048 by 512, which is 3,145,728 weights, 3.1 MB at one byte a weight. The weights are stored in FP8, rounded in blocks of 128 by 128 with one scale for each block. A few parts are left at 16 bits: the input embedding, the output layer, the routers and the image encoder. The files come to 37.5 GB.

For memory size an MoE model counts in full. The router may pick any expert for the next token, so every expert has to be in GPU memory already. Suppose gpu-2 were loaded with it in place of this factory's Qwen3-32B-FP8. The 37.5 GB would leave 42.5 GB of the 80. That is a bigger model to hold than the Qwen3-32B-FP8 it replaces, whose files are 34.3 GB.

For bandwidth it counts only what runs. Worked out from the published configuration, one token reads 1.13 GB of experts, about 1.28 GB of attention weights and 1.02 GB for the output layer at 16 bits, and 0.04 GB for the routers. That is about 3.47 GB a pass, against 32.8 GB for the dense model. On gpu-2 the ceiling is 3,350 ÷ 3.47 = 965 tokens a second for one user, where the dense model gets 102. The arithmetic shrinks the same way. About 2 × 2.95 billion = 5.9 billion operations a token, against 65.5 billion, so a 2,000-token prompt is about 11.8 TFLOP and takes about 12 milliseconds at 990 TFLOPS, against 132.

The saving is largest for one user. A batch still shares one pass, but every token in it picks its own experts. If the router spread its picks evenly, a batch of 32 tokens would touch about 163 of the 256 experts in each layer, and the pass would read about 23 GB. At 128 tokens it would touch about 252, and the pass reads 34.1 GB, nearly all of the model. A busy MoE server therefore reads close to its full size every pass, much like a dense model of that size. What stays small is the arithmetic: 5.9 billion operations a token against 65.5, so its cores have far more room before compute becomes the limit.

The KV cache shrinks as well, for a separate reason. Only one layer in four is ordinary attention. The other 30 are Gated DeltaNet layers, a kind of linear attention that keeps one fixed-size state per conversation in place of a key and a value for every token. So only 10 layers store keys and values, each with 2 KV heads 256 wide: 10 × 2 × 2 × 256 × 2 bytes = 20,480 bytes a token, against 262,144 for Qwen3-32B, 12.8 times less. The fixed state is 30 layers × 32 heads × 128 × 128 values, about 15.7 million, and does not grow with the conversation. The practical rule for serving an MoE model is two numbers: size the memory by the total, 35 billion, and expect the speed of the active part, 3 billion, until the batch grows large.

Chapter 13 of 14

Reading a card's label

The same model on four cards

Now the three numbers can be read for what they decide. Memory size says whether the model fits and how many conversations fit beside it. Bandwidth says how fast it writes. Compute says how fast it reads a prompt. Put this model on four cards.

The A100 holds the same 80 GB as the H100, so the same 180,000 tokens fit. Its road is 2,039 GB a second, so it writes 62 tokens a second, and its 312 TFLOPS read a 2,000-token prompt in 420 milliseconds. The H100 writes 102 and reads the prompt in 132. The H200 has the same cores as the H100, so prompts are read no faster. It writes faster and holds more than twice the conversations, because the only things that changed are its memory and the road out of it. That is a whole product generation made of the second and third numbers alone. The B200 moves all three: 244 tokens a second, about 562,000 tokens of room and the prompt in 58 milliseconds.

This is also why the two phases can be given different hardware. Prefill wants compute and decode wants memory and bandwidth, so a platform can run a pool of prefill workers on compute-heavy cards and a pool of decode workers on memory-heavy ones, and hand the KV cache from one to the other over a fast link. For one card and one model the reading stays simple. Check the fit first, divide bandwidth by the bytes a pass reads, and remember that the cores only set the wait before the first word.

Chapter 14 of 14

Right model, right place, right cost

The cheapest tier carries the most requests

The three numbers decide which model a site can run. A platform with several sites can use that on purpose: match each task to a model size, and each model size to a tier of sites. F5's planning for an AI grid draws three tiers. At the edge, close to users, an executor tier runs Nano-class models. In the middle a regional hub runs Super-class models. At the top a cloud or factory tier runs Ultra-class models. Nano, Super and Ultra are the three classes of NVIDIA's Nemotron family. In its Llama-based generation they are Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B with 8.0 billion weights, Llama-3.3-Nemotron-Super-49B with 49.9 billion and Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Ultra-253B with 253.4 billion. NVIDIA's own cards place them by hardware: the Nano fits on a single RTX GPU, the Super was designed to run on one GPU, an H100 of 80 GB or an H200, where its BF16 files of 99.7 GB fit the H200's 141 GB and an H100 takes the 8-bit build of 52.0 GB, and the Ultra fits on a single node of 8 H100s. In this lesson's world Madrid is the edge, Amsterdam the hub and Frankfurt the factory.

Each tier has its own work. The edge executor handles tool calling and real-time interaction with users, data retrieval and sensor processing, personal assistant agents, routine actions and quick lookups. It costs the least per request and sees the most requests. The regional hub handles multi-step reasoning and putting context together, chains of agent tasks and code generation, reasoning over a domain with retrieved documents, and coordinating sub-agents. It costs more, sees middle volume, and is sized for the peaks of the business day. The factory handles complex planning and reasoning across domains, long-horizon decisions, coordinating sub-agents at scale, and the occasional call of high value. The planning has it run full-precision models, kept for what only they can do. For the Ultra in BF16 that is 506.8 GB of files, about 63 GB on each of 8 cards. It costs the most and sees the fewest requests.

That shape is called an inverted cost pyramid: the cheapest tier handles the majority of requests, and complex planning goes up to the factory only when it is needed. The third number shows why the shape pays. Each token a model writes reads its weights once, so bytes read per token is a fair measure of what a request costs. Count every tier in 8 bits to compare like with like. That is about 8.0 GB for the Nano, its 8.03 billion weights at one byte each, and the real FP8 files of 52.0 GB for the Super and 257.6 GB for the Ultra. Those files come to a little over one byte a weight, 49.9 and 253.4 billion weights, because some layers are kept at 16 bits. The ratio is 1 to 6.5 to 32. Take an illustrative split of 100 requests: 70 at the edge, 25 at the hub and 5 at the factory. They read 70 × 8.0 + 25 × 52.0 + 5 × 257.6 = 3,150 GB per token of answer, where sending all 100 to the Ultra would read 25,760 GB, about 8 times as much.

The edge has one more lever: fewer bits. NVFP4 is NVIDIA's 4-bit floating point format for its Blackwell GPUs. Each weight is 4 bits, one sign bit, two exponent bits and one mantissa bit, which can only say 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4 or 6 and their negatives. To make that enough, every block of 16 weights shares one scale stored in FP8, and each whole tensor has one more scale in 32 bits. That costs 8 bits per 16 weights, so a weight takes 4.5 bits on average. NVIDIA puts the memory saving at about 3.5 times against 16 bits and 1.8 times against FP8, with 1 percent or less loss of accuracy against FP8 on the model it tested. The Super shows it in real files: 99.7 GB in BF16, 52.0 GB in FP8 and 31.1 GB in NVFP4. On a B200 at about 8,000 GB a second its ceiling for one user goes from 80 to 154 to 257 tokens a second, 3.2 times from the bytes alone. Blackwell tensor cores also do 4-bit arithmetic at four times their 16-bit rate, about 9,000 TFLOPS against 2,250, so prompts are read faster too. F5's figure for the edge tier is about four times the throughput on the same hardware, which sits between those two. An H100 like gpu-2 has tensor cores for 8 bits and none for 4.

One constraint sits above cost and speed. A request tagged as EU-only cannot leave EU infrastructure, whatever the latency or the price. The same planning names GDPR, UK GDPR, national sovereignty rules, HIPAA, PCI DSS and the FCA among the rules that can pin a request to a place. So the tier a request lands on is chosen by three things: the size of the task, the cost of the tier, and where the data is allowed to go.

What to keep

The road matters more than the engine

A model that is writing an answer reads all of its weights for every token. So its speed is the bandwidth between GPU memory and the GPU's cores, divided by the size of the model. Memory size decides whether the model fits and how many conversations fit with it. Compute decides how fast a prompt is read. The weights have to live on the card because the slot to the rest of the server is 52 times narrower. Everyone in a batch shares that one road, and storing weights in fewer bits is the only change that buys both room and speed. A mixture of experts model is sized for memory by all of its weights, runs for one user at the speed of the few it reads and, once busy, reads close to all of them, and a platform of several sites keeps its biggest models for the few requests that need them.
