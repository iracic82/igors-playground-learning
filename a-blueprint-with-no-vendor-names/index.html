BNK and gateways

A blueprint with no vendor names

Reference architectures for GPU clouds, where BNK fits in NVIDIA's, and what shipped when

A GPU cloud is expensive to get wrong. A cabling choice, a missing management network or a storage path that crosses the wrong card can cost months after the racks are full. So NVIDIA publishes reference architectures: designs, tested at scale, that a provider can copy instead of deriving from scratch. The set NVIDIA writes for its cloud partners, the NCP reference architectures, describes compute, storage and networking by role and names no vendor's product. Partners then publish their own documents that fill each role with a product. F5's fills the common networking role with BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes (BNK) on BlueField-3 DPUs. This lesson reads that family of documents, follows the networking design down to the VLANs, BGP sessions and Kubernetes objects it prescribes, maps NVIDIA's published requirements onto BNK's objects, and then lays out the dates: what NVIDIA and F5 announced, what reached general availability and when, and which BNK release brings which feature from 2.0 in 2025 to the plans for 2027.

Chapter 1 of 16

A design you copy instead of deriving

Why reference architectures exist and how NVIDIA names a node

The owner's Tech Summit deck lists the problem plainly. Every operator was re-deriving compute, fabric, power and cooling design from scratch, and at gigawatt scale that is an expensive mistake waiting to happen. Thermal and electrical mistakes were found after the concrete was poured. NVIDIA's answer is the reference architecture, an RA for short: a design its architects build, test and operate, then package as patterns a partner can follow. NVIDIA's white paper gives the reasons in concrete terms. Cabling and transceiver choices change thermals and delivery time. CPU, GPU, storage and network ratios that look fine on a few nodes become bottlenecks across a cluster.

An RA is described around a reference configuration, one prescribed server design. NVIDIA names each configuration with four numbers, the C-G-N-B nomenclature: CPU sockets, GPUs, network adapters, and average east-west bandwidth per GPU in gigabit Ethernet. 2-8-5-200 is two CPUs, eight GPUs, five adapters (one north-south, four east-west) and 200 GbE per GPU. 2-8-9-800 is the HGX B300 node: two CPUs, eight GPUs, nine adapters (one BlueField-3 B3220 north-south, eight ConnectX-8 SuperNICs east-west) and 800 GbE per GPU.

The enterprise RAs are cut from the larger cloud partner design. NVIDIA says they are derived from the NCP Reference Architecture but right-sized: 32 to 1024 GPUs, 4 to 128 nodes, 1 to 8 racks. The two ends agree with the node: 4 nodes of 8 GPUs is 32, 128 nodes of 8 GPUs is 1,024.

Northlight's own nodes can be written the same way. gpu-node-1 holds four H100s. If it had two sockets and three adapters, one north-south and two 400 GbE east-west, it would read 2-4-3-200, because 2 × 400 GbE shared by 4 GPUs is 200 GbE each. That name is illustrative, since Northlight's nodes were never sized to a published configuration.

Chapter 2 of 16

NVIDIA Cloud Partners and their reference architecture

What the NCP RA contains and who builds to it

NVIDIA Cloud Partners, NCPs, are providers that rent NVIDIA GPU capacity to others. The owner's decks call them neoclouds, the word the earlier lesson on neoclouds and AI factories unpacks. NVIDIA wrote the NCP reference architecture for them and announced it on its blog on 26 June 2024 as a blueprint for high-performance, scalable and secure AI data centers.

The blog lists what the architecture includes. GPU servers from NVIDIA and its manufacturing partners, with Hopper and Blackwell GPUs. Storage from certified partners, including offerings validated for DGX SuperPOD and DGX Cloud. Quantum-2 InfiniBand and Spectrum-X Ethernet for the east-west network between GPU servers. BlueField-3 DPUs for north-south connectivity, storage acceleration, elastic GPU computing and zero-trust security. In-band and out-of-band management from NVIDIA and management partners. And NVIDIA AI Enterprise software: Base Command Manager Essentials to provision and manage servers, the NeMo framework, NIM microservices, Riva for speech and the RAPIDS accelerator for Spark.

The blog also names the way it is delivered. Under Build, Train and Go, NVIDIA's infrastructure specialists physically install and provision the cluster. An update records that in October 2024 NVIDIA added a Reference Platform NCP designation for partners that operate large clusters built with NVIDIA to a tested reference architecture.

NVIDIA has since published more of the NCP material openly. The NCP Software Reference Guide, at docs.nvidia.com/dsx/ncp, stacks infrastructure as a service for bare metal and virtual machines, containers as a service with managed Kubernetes, and an AI platform as a service for tenants. It calls itself a reference guide, not an implementation manual, and tells software vendors to map their offerings onto its layers. It says NVIDIA certifies the performance of third-party solutions so NCPs can choose their partner. On the gpu-cloud scene those layers are the provisioner, the scheduler and the portal, and the servers they run on.

Chapter 3 of 16

Three documents and no vendor names

The NCP RA family, and the partner RAs that fill each role

The owner's NVIDIA deck draws the NCP RA as a tree. At the top is the NVIDIA NCP RA. Under it sit three NVIDIA reference architecture documents: the High Performance Storage RA, the Compute Platform Software Stack RA and the Common Networking RA. A note on the slide states the rule that shapes everything below: no vendor solutions are specified within the NVIDIA NCP reference architecture documents.

So each NVIDIA document says what a role must do, how it connects and which terms to use, and stops there. Products arrive in a second layer, the partner RAs. A partner RA takes one of NVIDIA's roles and shows how a named product fills it, using NVIDIA's terms so the two documents read as one design.

The deck gives examples for each branch. For storage, VAST's high-performance storage RA for NVIDIA Cloud Partners, WEKA's high-performance storage for NCPs and DDN's A3I solutions for NCPs. NVIDIA's public data center page agrees on the shape: it assumes a file storage cluster and an optional object store, and names WEKA, VAST and DDN among AI storage offerings. For compute, NVIDIA's own compute services fill the box: NIM, NVIDIA Cloud Functions (NVCF), Run:ai, Cloud Native Stack, SLURM, Kubernetes and Base Command Manager Essentials, with Rafay's GPU PaaS reference architecture for cloud and enterprise private clouds as a partner RA. For common networking there is one example, outlined on the slide: F5, NVIDIA Cloud Partner: BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes for Common Networking RA.

The split is useful to an operator. A provider can keep NVIDIA's design and swap one partner for another in one branch without redrawing the rest. The owner's blog draft puts BNK's place simply: NVIDIA's cloud partner design leaves the tenant access network to partners, and that is the slot BNK fills.

Chapter 4 of 16

Four networks and one building block

TAN, SMN, CIN and NVLink, and the GPU POD

NVIDIA's public data center page for NCPs names four networks. The tenant access network, TAN, also called north-south or front end, joins every part of the data center, and storage is its main consumer. The secure management network, SMN, is out-of-band: it configures and manages everything. The cluster interconnect network, CIN, also called east-west or scale out, joins GPU racks for GPU-to-GPU traffic. NVLink is scale up inside one rack. TAN and SMN are always Ethernet, CIN can be Ethernet or InfiniBand, and NVLink is NVIDIA's own.

The page fixes the compute tray too. A GB200 tray has two Grace CPUs, four B200 GPUs with 720 GB of HBM3 together, four 400 Gb ConnectX-7 for CIN, one 1 GbE port for SMN and, for TAN, two 400 GbE BlueField-3 each configured as 2 × 200 GbE. A GB300 tray has four B300 GPUs with 1.152 TB of HBM3e, four 800 Gb ConnectX-8 and one BlueField-3 for TAN.

The building block is the GPU POD, which is not a Kubernetes Pod. A rack holds 18 compute trays and 9 NVLink switch trays. 18 trays × 4 GPUs = 72 GPUs, one NVL72 domain. A POD holds up to 1152 GPUs, which is 16 such racks. The design supports 1 to 64 GPU PODs, up to 64 × 1152 = 73,728 GPUs, plus one Core POD for control nodes, general purpose nodes, high-speed storage, a utility cluster that bootstraps the site, and the DC edge cluster with its firewalls.

BNK sits on the TAN. On the gpu-cloud scene that is the fabric between the servers and storage. A GB200 tray reaches the TAN through two BlueField-3 cards, and the next chapter shows that F5's RA gives them different jobs: storage uses one, BNK runs on the other. Northlight's Qwen3-32B weights travel this network too: the FP8 build's 34.3 GB is 274.4 Gb, which a 200 Gb/s port carries in about 1.4 seconds. Loading still takes about three minutes, so the wire is a small part of it.

Chapter 5 of 16

Two cards in every server, one for BNK

F5's RA puts one BlueField-3 in NIC mode and one in DPU mode

F5's partner RA is public at f5.com. It is 29 pages, dated March 2026, and written for BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes 2.1.0. It follows the NVIDIA Common Networking RA's hardware. Each compute shelf has two BlueField-3 B3220 cards, part number 900-9D3B4-00CV-EA0, crypto enabled with an integrated BMC, each with two 200 GbE ports. The NVIDIA RA requires both ports to be used.

The two cards have different jobs. One runs in NIC mode and carries RoCE and GPUDirect Storage traffic to the high-performance storage pod. The host bonds its two ports with ordinary Linux bonding, enp193s1f0np0 and enp193s2f0np1 in F5's example. The other card runs in DPU mode, with its Arm cores running their own operating system, and hosts BNK. That card carries in-band management, host to host traffic and every flow BNK manages in and out of the cluster. Both cards face the tenant access network, so storage traffic still crosses the TAN, but on the NIC-mode card. It never passes through BNK's card.

The switches are named too. The NVIDIA SN5600 serves the converged network with ports from 10 to 800 GbE: 256 × 200 GbE, 128 × 400 GbE or 64 × 800 GbE, which all come to 51.2 Tb/s. The SN2201 has 48 ports of 1 Gb/s for the out-of-band network, which connects each shelf's BMC, each DPU's BMC and each DPU's OOB port. BNK's installation needs those OOB ports connected, because kubeadm joins the DPUs to the Kubernetes cluster over that network, and cluster control traffic stays on it.

On the DPU-mode card the install creates an eSwitch LAG, bond0, over both ports for 2 × 200 = 400 Gb/s, a DOCA-accelerated OVS bridge br-lag, and two scalable functions, en3f0pf0sf0 and en3f0pf1sf0, that become TMM's interfaces 1.1 and 1.2. The lesson on a DPU the host cannot touch follows those steps in the owner's lab. Here what matters is the shape: one card for storage, one card for BNK.

Chapter 6 of 16

A node VLAN and an Exit VLAN

How the RA joins the DPUs to the fabric and to the cluster

F5's RA adds two VLANs on the leaf switches, with matching configuration on the hosts and on TMM. The first is the Kubernetes node network. Nodes and the TMMs on the DPUs use it to talk inside the cluster, and TMM uses it to reach the Pods it balances to. In F5's diagram its tag is 10 and its gateway is 10.10.10.254. The second is the Exit network, tag 20, gateway 10.10.20.254 in the diagram. Only TMM connects to it. Every packet into or out of the Pods passes TMM on the way.

Both gateways are VRR addresses, a distributed next hop configured on each pair of leaf switches. The leaves run active L3 LAG toward each DPU with a BGP EVPN multihoming segment. The host's VLAN tag can be set in two ways: OVS flow rules on br-lag at the pf0hpf port, or tagging in the host's Linux.

Two rules come with it. Each Kubernetes cluster needs its own node VLAN, because BNK's control is per cluster. And order matters. The cluster must exist before Exit connectivity is provisioned through BNK's resources, while the Exit VLAN itself must already work in the fabric before the cluster is built.

The RA gives the objects. An F5SPKVlan named external, tag 20, MTU 9000, on interface 1.1, with self IPs 10.120.20.201 and 10.120.20.202 in a /24. An F5SPKVlan named internal, tag 10, on interface 1.2, with 10.100.10.201 and 10.100.10.202. Each list holds one self IP per TMM, in order. Interfaces 1.1 and 1.2 come from NetworkAttachmentDefinitions sf-external and sf-internal of type sf, whose resources nvidia.com/bf3_p0_sf1 and nvidia.com/bf3_p1_sf1 the sriovdp ConfigMap defines from vendor 15b3, device a2dc, at PCI 0000:03:00.0 and 0000:03:00.1. Separate scalable functions are needed for hardware offload even though the eSwitch merges both ports.

Chapter 7 of 16

BGP tells the fabric where each tenant lives

TMM peers with the leaves and advertises VIPs and SNAT addresses

Horizontal scale for ingress comes from routing. TMM on every DPU peers with the fabric's routers and advertises every tenant's ingress addresses, the VIPs, on the Exit VLAN, placed in a global Exit VRF carried over BGP EVPN VXLAN. It advertises every tenant's egress SNAT addresses the same way. A tenant, defined by Kubernetes namespaces, becomes visible to the fabric as routes: its VIPs and its SNAT addresses. The RA peers with the leaves and notes that a spine or another route reflector works as well.

The appendix gives both ends. On each leaf, NVUE commands put interface vlan20 in VRF EXT, turn on EVPN for the VRF with VNI 3200, redistribute connected routes, export them to EVPN, and run BGP in autonomous system 65200 with router IDs 10.255.0.13 and 10.255.0.14. A peer group TMMS with remote-as external accepts dynamic neighbors from 10.120.20.0/24, at most 10.

On TMM, a ConfigMap named f5-tmm-dynamic-routing-template holds ZebOS.conf: router bgp 65202, router ID taken from the Pod IP through the %%POD_IP%% template, graceful restart time 120, no default IPv4 unicast, redistribute kernel, neighbors 10.120.20.13 and 10.120.20.14 in AS 65200, each activated in the IPv4 family with soft reconfiguration inbound.

The numbers fit together. TMM's self IPs 10.120.20.201 and .202 lie inside the leaves' listen range, so the leaves accept them without a line per DPU. 65202 is not 65200, so the sessions are external BGP. A leaf holds at most ten dynamic peers here, so an eleventh TMM would not peer until the limit is raised. Northlight's gateway address 203.0.113.80, served as a VIP on a TMM listener, would reach the fabric this way, as a route from TMM through VRF EXT into EVPN.

Chapter 8 of 16

Ingress, firewall, iRules and egress, as objects

The Kubernetes resources the RA uses for each service

Once the fabric is in place, the RA turns each service into Kubernetes objects. Ingress starts with a GatewayClass f5-gateway-class whose controllerName is f5.com/default-f5-cne-controller. The owner's lab notes record that this value follows a pattern, f5.com/<CNEInstance namespace>-f5-cne-controller, so default here is the namespace. An F5BnkGateway object holds the address range for listeners: base CIDR 198.19.19.0/24, start 198.19.19.50, end 198.19.19.59, ten addresses. A Gateway f5-l4-gateway in namespace red points at it through infrastructure.parametersRef and opens a TCP listener on port 80 that accepts L4Route objects from its own namespace. The L4Route sends to the Service nginx-app-svc port 80 and sets pvaAccelerationMode, which turns on DOCA flow acceleration.

With acceleration TMM handles the first packets: it applies the firewall, computes address translation, picks the Pod, then programs the DPU's flow engine to carry the rest of the connection. TMM still reports correct telemetry for flows in hardware, and resets are passed back to it, so connections close cleanly instead of waiting for timeouts. F5 states accelerated L4 balancing above 200 Gb/s.

The firewall is an F5BigFwPolicy, here accepting TCP to port 8000 from anywhere with logging on, bound to a Gateway listener by a BNKSecPolicy. iRules come as an F5BigCneIrule bound by a BNKNetPolicy to a listener section such as tenant1-http2. Egress uses an F5SPKSnatpool tenant1-snatpool whose address lists pair 10.230.10.x with 10.240.20.x, the first list for the first TMM, and an F5SPKEgress tenant1-egress with snatType SRC_TRANS_POOL that covers namespace tenant1-ns. Telemetry leaves as OTEL, logs as fluentbit streams per application, and a debug sidecar beside TMM gives support access and traffic captures under Kubernetes RBAC.

Chapter 9 of 16

NVIDIA's requirements, read against BNK's objects

What an NCP must provide, and which object provides it

NVIDIA also publishes what it requires from a cloud partner, openly, as NVIDIA Requirements for AI Clouds. Version 2.1 appeared on 26 February 2026, 2.2 on 10 April, 2.3 on 25 June and 2.4 on 1 September 2026, which added operational requirements. NVIDIA calls it the primary requirements document from NVIDIA to any NCP providing NVIDIA GPU and AI compute and software services. It covers the full stack of services and operations needed to run NVIDIA DGX Cloud, NVIDIA's own cloud service, on a partner's capacity, and NVIDIA publishes it as a reference for the industry too. NVIDIA tests what it can with its AI Cloud Ready suite and checks the rest by self-grading and review.

Several of its lines land on the north-south network. K8S29 requires Services of type LoadBalancer with public external addresses, private internal ones and static IP assignment. SEC12 puts BMCs on a dedicated, restricted network that is physically separate, VLAN-isolated or VRF-isolated, blocked from the public internet and the corporate network and reached only through a hardened bastion host. SEC14 allows no public internet access by default. SEC15 filters all traffic through security groups or user ACLs with 5-tuple rules. SEC16 makes the NCP name its edge enforcement technology, such as hardware firewalls, SDN, or DPUs and SmartNICs, and where it sits in the packet path. NET06 is about NVIDIA's own cluster on the partner's capacity. Its egress NAT addresses must come from a static pool dedicated only to the NVIDIA cluster, tenancy and VPC, used only for DGX Cloud traffic, and never shared with or carrying traffic from other NCP tenants. It also asks for redundant upstream paths.

Read against F5's RA, each has an object. The F5BnkGateway range and BGP advertisement give LoadBalancer addresses that stay put. The SN2201 out-of-band network matches SEC12. F5BigFwPolicy matches SEC15 with 5-tuple rules. SEC16's answer is a sentence: TMM on the BlueField-3 in DPU mode, in the path of every packet into and out of the Pods. NET06 is F5SPKSnatpool with F5SPKEgress. The requirement names only the NVIDIA tenancy, but the objects give each tenant's namespaces their own pool, so the same mechanism serves the NVIDIA tenancy and every other tenant alike.

This mapping is the lesson's own reading. Neither NVIDIA nor F5 publishes it as a table. It shows why a networking partner RA exists: NVIDIA states the requirement without a product, and the partner shows the object that meets it.

Chapter 11 of 16

GTC 2026 and the status of each NVIDIA piece

Announced, early access, GA, concept: four words that differ

March 2026 on the timeline holds three NVIDIA announcements at GTC: the DSX reference design, AI Grid, and NICo with DPF Zero Trust built in. NVIDIA writes NICo and expands it as NVIDIA Infra Controller. The deck spells it NiCO in places. Under the timeline the slide adds a status line for four NVIDIA pieces, and that line says more than the announcement dates.

AI Grid was a concept at GTC in March 2026. NICo Zero Trust was GA in DPF in March 2025, with NICo itself in early access at GTC in March 2026. Dynamo was in early access at GTC in March 2025 and GA at GTC in March 2026. NVIDIA's newsroom confirms Dynamo 1.0, announced on 16 March 2026, as the production release. DSX was in early access at GTC in March 2026 and GA in October 2026.

The words mean different things to an operator. Announced means a name and a design exist. Concept means the idea is published and no product is behind it yet. Early access, EA, means software selected customers can run, with interfaces that may still change. GA, general availability, means anyone can buy it with full support. NVIDIA's requirements document asks partners for exactly that: services delivered GA to all customers, not built for one.

DPF shows how version numbers carry dates. Its releases are named by year and month: v25.4.0, v25.7.0, v25.10.0, v26.4.0. The v25.4.0 notes already call that release the GA release of DPF, which matches the spring 2025 GA mark on the timeline. Later releases repeat the words: the v26.4.0 notes also call it the GA release, dated May 2026, with Host Trusted and Zero Trust modes and UEFI Secure Boot set during Zero Trust provisioning. The lessons on a server nobody has to trust, on a DPU the host cannot touch, on Dynamo and on many sites as one platform teach these pieces. This chapter only dates them.

Chapter 12 of 16

BNK 2.0 and 2.1: the card first, then programmability

Early and late 2025 on the BNK on NVIDIA DPUs roadmap

The owner's NVIDIA deck closes with the BNK on NVIDIA DPUs roadmap, one column per release. Release 2.0, general availability in early 2025, is titled Delivering Secure HW-accelerated Ingress/Egress Traffic Management. It brings DOCA-offloaded ingress and egress traffic management, DOCA-offloaded network security with ACLs and DDoS protection, and a Kubernetes-native deployment with Gateway API support and the F5 Lifecycle Operator.

The Lifecycle Operator, FLO, is the piece that installs everything else. It reads one resource, the CNEInstance, and builds the CNE core with its licensing controller and the instance with its controller and TMM. The lesson on a cluster with no data plane follows it in the owner's lab. The owner's notes add that on a DPU, FLO is the only way to install BNK, while BNK on Host can also use Helm.

Release 2.1, late 2025, is titled Programmability & AI Traffic Workload Optimization Features. It brings programmability through iRules, intelligent AI load balancing as early access, LLM routing and token counting written as iRules, and link aggregation. The link aggregation is the eSwitch LAG of the reference architecture, and F5's public NCP RA is written for 2.1.0, which is why its examples include F5BigCneIrule and BNKNetPolicy.

An iRule is a small TCL program that TMM runs on events such as HTTP_REQUEST or HTTP_RESPONSE. In 2.1 the AI features are such programs: an iRule that reads the model from the request body and picks a pool, or one that reads usage.total_tokens from the response and counts it per caller. Later releases turn the same jobs into declared features.

Chapter 13 of 16

BNK 2.2 and 2.3: certification, tenancy and AI traffic

The first half of 2026

Release 2.2, first quarter 2026, is titled NVIDIA NCP RA 1.0 Certification & Semantic Model Caching. It brings full VRF support with API-based configuration, a DPF deployment in Trusted mode as early access, EVPN VXLAN over BGP as early access, ConnectX-7 on x86 hosts, and semantic model caching as early access. The Tech Summit timeline marks the same DPF step for BNK in September 2026 as DPF Trusted Mode Support. In Trusted mode, which DPF's own guide calls Host Trusted, the host is a trusted entity and a worker node in the Kubernetes cluster, and the DPUs are managed in band through the host, without their out-of-band interface.

Supporting ConnectX-7 matters because a ConnectX is a SmartNIC, a network card without the DPU's own Arm operating system. DOCA flow offload runs there too, so BNK's hardware path is no longer tied to a BlueField.

Release 2.3, second quarter 2026, is titled Enhanced AI Traffic Optimization. It brings intelligent AI load balancing, prefix-aware routing, LLM routing, token governance, semantic model caching, ConnectX-8 on x86, an AI stats dashboard and tenant-aware telemetry. The owner checked 2.3 against its 964-page documentation. Intelligent load balancing is switched on with intelligentLB.enabled on the CNEInstance, which deploys an Analyzer. The other AI features are annotations on the Gateway: k8s.f5.com/ai for an external semantic cache, k8s.f5.com/ai-token-counting for token governance with real enforcement, and k8s.f5.com/ai-llm-router delegating to an external router of type litellm, routellm or nvidia.

NCP RA 1.0 certification sits in 2.2 on the roadmap, while the Tech Summit timeline already shows a BNK NCP RA in October 2025. The two are different steps: F5's RA document was written for 2.1.0, and certification against NVIDIA's RA is the later milestone the roadmap names.

Chapter 14 of 16

BNK 2.4: NCP RA 2.0 and agents

Third quarter 2026, and what 2.4.0 brought

Release 2.4, third quarter 2026, is titled NVIDIA NCP RA 2.0 Certification & Agentic Security. The roadmap lists EVPN VXLAN over BGP, a zero-trust DPF deployment, HBN support, MCP security with Agentic IAM as early access and A2A and MCP persistence profiles, and ConnectX-7 and ConnectX-8 on Arm hosts. HBN is NVIDIA's host-based networking, a routing service that runs on the DPU. The slide marks zero-trust DPF in red as 2.4.1, early access.

The owner checked the 2.4.0 features deck from F5's product management, dated 5 October 2026, against the release notes. Five areas: intelligent AI load balancing and multi-tenant networking with EVPN VXLAN over BGP, both available, secure agentic delivery for MCP and A2A as early access or proof of concept, DOCA hardware offload on ConnectX-7 and ConnectX-8 for Arm hosts in regular NIC and SuperNIC modes, and zero-trust DPF with NICo moved to 2.4.1 early access. The owner's lab verified on 2.4.2 that BNK now uses the Gateway API Inference Extension path: an InferencePool that names F5's own picker, an F5EPP object at inference.k8s.f5.com/v1alpha1, plus an AIGateway resource.

Agentic security means traffic between agents and tools. MCP, the Model Context Protocol, carries an agent's calls to tool servers. A2A, agent to agent, carries tasks between agents. Persistence keeps a session on the right backend: on the MCP session ID, and on the A2A context and task IDs. The deck says this fails closed on misconfiguration. The lesson on an agent that keeps its session goes through it.

In zero-trust DPF mode, BNK is deployed as f5-ingress instead of f5-cne and narrows its role to provider fabric ingress, L4 to L7 and security. Tenants are isolated by per-tenant fabric VRFs and get an accelerated VPC load balancer, managed out of band through DPF and Argo, never through the tenant's host.

Chapter 15 of 16

BNK 2.5 and after: gateway, grid and hardware security

The fourth quarter of 2026 and the plans for 2027

Release 2.5, fourth quarter 2026, is titled BNK as LLM Gateway & AI Grid Solution. It brings BNK as an LLM router, AI Grid phase I, integration with F5 AI Guardrails, BlueField-4 support, secure S3 AI data storage access, MCP security with Agentic IAM and agentic application delivery, agentic firewall policies as early access (the slide marks only this item EA), an orchestration extension through Mirantis integration, and integration with the AI Gateway of F5 Distributed Cloud, F5XC.

Each item has a place on the factory scene. As an LLM router BNK chooses a model or a hosted provider itself, instead of calling an external router as in 2.3. AI Grid phase I joins factories as one platform, with F5 DNS picking the site and BNK picking the GPU inside it. Guardrails integration sends prompts and answers to F5 AI Guardrails for inspection. Secure S3 access protects the path where weights and datasets come from object storage. The Tech Summit timeline puts BNK with NICo and DPF Zero Trust, and BNK's NCP RA with NICo and DPF Zero Trust, at December 2026, and a BNK AI Grid reference architecture at March 2027.

Chapter 16 of 16

Reading a date with its source

Two decks, a public RA and lab notes, and where they differ

This lesson used four kinds of source, and each has its own weight. The F5 roadmap slides carry a 2025 copyright and give quarters. The Tech Summit timeline carries 2026 and gives months. The public documents, F5's RA and NVIDIA's pages, carry exact dates and exact versions. The owner's notes record what was checked against the documentation or run in a lab. When they disagree, the later and more exact source wins for the fact, and the difference is itself worth knowing.

Four differences show the pattern. BNK's GA on BlueField-3 is at the March 2025 mark on the timeline, in April 2025 on the NVIDIA slide, and on 25 April 2025 on F5's blog. The NCP RA appears for BNK in October 2025 on the timeline and as a 1.0 certification in 2.2 on the roadmap, because an RA document and a certification are separate steps. A third step follows in December 2026 on the timeline, a newer BNK NCP RA built on NICo and DPF Zero Trust. Zero-trust DPF is in 2.4 on the roadmap column and in 2.4.1 as early access in the red mark and the 2.4.0 product deck. The BlueField-3 date in the deck's extracted text belongs to a different event once the slide picture is read.

For an operator the method is short. Take a feature from a roadmap. Find the release it lands in. Check the release notes or documentation for its object or field: intelligentLB on the CNEInstance in 2.3, InferencePool and F5EPP in 2.4. Note the status word, EA or GA. Plan production on GA.

One more line from the NVIDIA slide frames all of it: validation is one thread, and the through-line is making accelerated infrastructure securely consumable and production ready. The NCP RA family says what a GPU cloud needs, without vendor names. The partner RAs, F5's among them, show how a product meets it. The dates say when each part could be bought.

What to keep

A design by role, products by partner, dates by source

NVIDIA's cloud partner reference architectures describe a GPU cloud by role: compute and its software stack, high-performance storage, and common networking, with four networks (TAN, SMN, CIN and NVLink) and GPU PODs of up to 1152 GPUs. They name no vendor. Partner RAs fill each role with a product: VAST, WEKA and DDN for storage, NVIDIA's compute services and Rafay for compute, and F5 for common networking. F5's RA puts BNK on the DPU-mode BlueField-3 of every compute shelf, beside a NIC-mode card for storage, joins TMM to a node VLAN and an Exit VLAN, peers it over eBGP into VRF EXT, and turns ingress, firewall, iRules and egress into Kubernetes objects that answer NVIDIA's published requirements. The dates run from SPK for 5G in December 2021, through BlueField-3 in 2023 and BNK GA in April 2025, to releases 2.0 to 2.5 and plans for 2027, each to be read with its source and its status word.
