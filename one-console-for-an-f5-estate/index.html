BNK and gateways

One console for a whole F5 estate

F5 Insight: observe every BIG-IP, understand what it says with AI, and act on the fleet in one change window

Northlight runs more F5 than the BNK data plane at its border. Each of its three factories has a BIG-IP HA pair in front of the object store that holds the model weights, and two BIG-IP DNS devices answer for the factory name. That is eight BIG-IP instances in three places, on two software versions, each with its own web interface, its own logs and its own upgrade to plan. F5 Insight is F5's console for an estate like this. It collects telemetry from every BIG-IP, uses AI to turn the telemetry into findings and plain answers, and runs fleet jobs that stage, install, roll back and back up software across many devices, with checks before and after and pauses where a person decides. F5 sums it up in three words: Observe the signals, Understand the patterns, Act with purpose. This lesson follows Northlight's operator through all three, with the real job forms, limits and API calls. It ends with how Insight is deployed, what changes when the site has no way out, and where F5 is taking the product next.

Chapter 1 of 16

Signals are not clarity

Why an estate of F5 devices needs a console of its own

Northlight's F5 estate is small by enterprise standards and already awkward. Frankfurt has a BIG-IP pair in front of its S3 store and the BIG-IP DNS device that answers for the factory name. Amsterdam has the same pair and the second DNS device. Madrid has only the S3 pair. The Frankfurt pair and both DNS devices run TMOS 17.5.1. The Amsterdam and Madrid pairs still run 17.1.2. Every one of them has its own Configuration utility, its own statistics and its own log files.

F5's deck for Insight names three problems that such an estate shares with much larger ones. Apps are everywhere: they are built for and deployed in different environments, so visibility is hard and still essential. Many tools give signals and little clarity: SIEM and APM tools consume everything and report on it, and they do not always surface what matters. Expertise is short: all the visibility in the world does not replace an F5 expert who knows the environment. Insight's answer to the first two is observability with AI-written reporting and operational narratives. Its answer to the third is an assistant that carries some of that expertise.

The same deck quotes operators about change. They are moving to work on whole environments instead of one device at a time. Maintenance windows are becoming a weekly thing. One team has three hours, including the time to roll back. More changes per window, more often, in less time: that is the job Insight's fleet management is built for, and Northlight's upgrade later in this lesson has to fit exactly such a three hour window.

Chapter 2 of 16

Observe, understand, act

What F5 Insight is and which devices it manages today

F5's documentation describes Insight as a monitoring and analytics platform that shows how applications and systems are performing, finds problems early and helps fix them. It connects to the F5 systems, shows them on one dashboard, raises alerts with suggested fixes written by a large language model, and produces health report cards. F5 positions it as the operational platform for ADSP, the F5 Application Delivery and Security Platform.

The deck splits the work into three outcomes, starting with BIG-IP. Observe means all the data is accessible to Insight, through the AI Data Fabric (AIDF) where that applies. Understand means AI turns signals into insights, patterns and recommendations. The deck's Act column repeats the Understand wording, and its Act section is titled Fleet management and upgrades. In this lesson, Act means the console does something with the insights: fleet jobs, backups, licence management.

Version 2.0.0, released in October 2026, sets the current scope. Managed BIG-IP devices run TMOS 17.1.x, 17.5.x or 21.1.x. Local Traffic Manager (LTM) is required, and DNS, Advanced WAF, AFM, APM, Carrier-Grade NAT and SSL Orchestrator are supported. Each device needs iControl REST enabled and HTTPS reachable from the Insight host. The telemetry iRule additionally needs high-speed logging. Fleet management covers BIG-IP on TMOS 17.1.x, 17.5.x and 21.1.x, F5OS-A 1.8.x and 2.x.x on rSeries appliances, vCMP guests on VIPRION and F5OS tenants on VELOS. Licence management covers BIG-IP VE and F5OS instances.

The BNK data plane at Northlight's border is outside that list. BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes runs as Pods that Kubernetes manages, and Insight 2.0 does not name it among its managed platforms. Its telemetry is the subject of Seeing without slowing down. The BIG-IP in front of S3 on this scene is classic TMOS and is exactly the kind of device Insight manages. The releases came quickly: 1.0 at AppWorld in March 2026, 1.2 with fleet patching in July, 2.0 in October, with 1.1.0, 1.1.1 and the 1.2 point releases up to 1.2.3 in between, on what the deck calls an eight week release cadence.

Chapter 3 of 16

A console that is a small cluster

Where Insight runs and how large its host must be

Insight ships as a virtual machine image, OVA or QCOW, for x86_64. Inside it, Insight is a set of containerised services orchestrated by K3s, a lightweight Kubernetes distribution, on Ubuntu, with Helm pre-installed and every container image pre-loaded, so the first boot needs no registry. Data lives on persistent storage. F5 publishes installation guides for Proxmox VE, VMware ESXi and vSphere, OpenStack and AWS EC2. Only the KVM and ESXi patterns are documented and tested. F5 supports AWS where a deployment meets the documented requirements, and it has not formally qualified the AWS pattern. AWS has no published Insight image: the operator uploads the OVA to an S3 bucket, imports it with ec2:ImportSnapshot and registers the snapshot as an AMI with UEFI boot. The guide warns against ImportImage, because AWS modifies the file system and adds drivers when it processes a VM that way.

The minimum host is 12 vCPUs, 16 GB of RAM and 650 GB of disk. Of the 12 vCPUs, 8 do analytics and data processing and 4 do BIG-IP management work such as upgrades and patching. The release notes split the 650 GB into 100 GB for the operating system, 50 GB for backup and restore, and 500 GB for the default telemetry retention. That base serves about 50 BIG-IP instances. Each further 50 instances needs 6 more vCPUs, 4 more GB of RAM and 500 more GB of disk. Northlight's 8 instances fit the base with room to spare.

The network rules are short. Inbound to Insight: SSH on 22 and HTTPS on 443. Outbound: 443 to each BIG-IP's management port. Optional outbound: product.apis.f5.com:443 for connected licensing, us.edge.df.f5.com:443 and use1.connector.df.f5.com:443 for AIDF, and an OpenAI-compatible or Anthropic server for AI Chat. People sign in as Admin, Operator or Viewer, locally or through LDAP or SAML. A disaster recovery setup keeps a standby Insight that inherits the licence on promotion.

Chapter 4 of 16

Onboarding eight devices

Data centers, credentials and a poll every 60 seconds

Devices join under Manage, F5 Instances. The operator first makes data centers, which can be places, environments or any grouping. Northlight makes three: Frankfurt, Amsterdam and Madrid. The grouping matters beyond filtering, because the documentation says data center context makes the AI answers and MCP queries location-aware.

Each device needs an endpoint, an administrative username and password, a collection interval (60 seconds by default), a maximum number of concurrent connections (1 to 20, default 5), an optional CA certificate for TLS validation, a data center and the modules to monitor. No module is enabled by default. Insight tests the connection before it adds the device. BIG-IP's web server allows 10 concurrent connections by default, and F5 advises keeping 1 or 2 free for the ad hoc calls that features such as AI Chat make outside the scheduled collection.

For many devices there is YAML. A defaults.yaml holds values every device inherits, and a receivers.yaml lists the devices, each overriding what it needs. The keys show what runs underneath: an OpenTelemetry Collector. Each device becomes a receiver with data_types such as f5.dns, f5.gtm or f5.policy.asm switched on or off. The pipeline metrics/local passes them through batch/local to the exporter otlphttp/metrics-local, and the metrics end up in VictoriaMetrics, a time series database. A second pipeline, metrics/f5-datafabric, exists for export to AIDF.

The arithmetic is plain. One run every 60 seconds is 86,400 ÷ 60 = 1,440 runs per device per day. Eight devices make 11,520. With the default 5 connections each, Insight may hold up to 40 connections into the estate at once. Insight also refreshes each device's metadata (version, boot volumes, licence, disk, HA state) every hour.

Chapter 5 of 16

What every request leaves behind

The telemetry iRule and high-speed logging

Polling gives device statistics. Per request detail comes from an iRule that F5 publishes for Insight, irule_application_telemetry_using_publisher.tcl, attached to the virtual servers the operator picks. Because it runs where the traffic is handled, it needs no agent, no code change and no help from application developers.

It sends its records with high-speed logging (HSL). On the BIG-IP the operator creates a log destination named f5insight_destination of type Management Port, pointing at the Insight address on port 30514 over UDP, and a log publisher named f5insight_publisher that uses it. The iRule names that publisher in one line: set static::hsl_publisher /Common/f5insight_publisher. The management port has limits: it cannot load balance across pool members, cannot be used for request logging and has little bandwidth. F5 recommends sending HSL through TMM, the data plane, when the log traffic is expected to pass 1 Gbps.

Each record carries metadata and timings, and never a request or response body. It holds the device, virtual server, pool and node, the method, host, URI and a sanitised path, the status code and sizes, the request and response header names, the cookie names, the user agent, total time, time to first byte, server time, TLS handshake time, client and node round trip times, the client address and up to three X-Forwarded-For values, and the TLS version, cipher and key strength.

On Northlight's S3 path the numbers are friendly. As The road to the weights showed, Pod qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-x7k2p loads Qwen3-32B-FP8 as 34.3 GB of files in ranges of 64 MiB. 34,300,000,000 ÷ 67,108,864 ≈ 511.1, so a load is at least 512 ranged GETs, a few more because each of the seven files ends in a short range. That is about 512 records for a whole model load, far below anything that would trouble the management port. An inference API with thousands of small requests a second would be a different case.

Chapter 6 of 16

From traffic to an application inventory

What the AI Data Fabric adds

The F5 AI Data Fabric (AIDF) is an F5 cloud service that extends Insight. It does two things with the traffic data Insight collects. It classifies application workloads automatically from real traffic patterns, without manual tagging, so the inventory shows what is running now and where. It then scores each workload's security posture from real behaviour, using current CVE data, threat intelligence including the OWASP Top 10, and common weaknesses and misconfigurations. The deck lists application workload identification through AIDF among the 2.0 features, and F5 describes AIDF as the data foundation for later capabilities.

Enrolment goes through the F5 account team, which supplies three values: an Endpoint, a Sensor ID that identifies this Insight instance, and a Sensor Token. Under Manage, F5 AIDF, the operator switches it on, enters the three, tests the connection and saves. From then on a subset of the telemetry is batched and sent every 300 seconds to use1.connector.df.f5.com over gRPC inside TLS, authenticated with the sensor ID and token. Nothing goes to F5 unless that pipeline is configured and credentials are provisioned. At 300 seconds there are 86,400 ÷ 300 = 288 uploads a day. Results appear in the application inventory and security dashboards as the service processes them.

There is also a lighter way in. Insight Lite is a deployment mode that forwards telemetry straight to AIDF without keeping it locally. It is chosen at first boot with a cloud-init file that sets f5_insight.deployment_mode to lite, and can be upgraded to Full mode later with a licence. For Northlight the S3 virtual servers in three factories would show up as one workload with one score and a list of what lowers it. The licence manifest carries a flag for this, insight_aidf_allowance, so the entitlement says whether the instance may use AIDF at all.

Chapter 7 of 16

Asking the estate in plain language

AI Chat and the model that answers it

AI Chat lets an operator ask questions about BIG-IP configurations and logs in plain language. The documentation's examples are Why is pool member X marked down and Show me all virtual IPs with SSL offloading enabled. Behind the same feature, which F5 calls LLM Insights, Insight writes operational narratives and recommends changes for best practice, security hardening, performance and troubleshooting, drawing on the estate's own data.

Insight does not ship a model. An administrator opens System Management, LLM Settings, enables it and picks a provider. OpenAI uses https://api.openai.com/v1 with gpt-5.4 as the default. Anthropic uses https://api.anthropic.com with claude-sonnet-5-5 as the default. Local LLM accepts any OpenAI-compatible endpoint and a model name the server exposes. Test Connection checks the key and the model name before the settings are saved, and a model ID the provider does not accept fails with the provider's own error. F5 validates tool calling against gpt-5.4 and claude-sonnet-5-5 and recommends models of 120 billion parameters or more for reliable tool calling with self-hosted models.

Northlight runs its own model, so the obvious idea is to point Local LLM at https://api.northlight.example/v1 and Qwen3-32B. The request then travels the path every other caller uses: through the BNK data plane, past the key check, to a GPU worker. The documentation's threshold argues against it for now. Qwen3-32B has 32.8 billion weights, and 32.8 ÷ 120 ≈ 0.27, about a quarter of the recommended size. F5 says results from smaller local models may be inconsistent. A site that must keep everything inside could still do it, and should expect weaker tool use. A larger model served the same way would meet the advice. Insight also needs a network path to that endpoint, and 2.0 can send AI Chat through an outbound forward proxy where the site requires one.

Chapter 8 of 16

Tools the assistant may call

Telemetry query MCP tools, their limits and their audit trail

The model answers with the help of tools that Insight offers over the Model Context Protocol (MCP). An agent keeps its session explains how MCP sessions and tool calls travel. Here the interest is what the tools may touch. The assistant uses a dedicated tool when one fits the question. For anything else, 2.0 adds Telemetry Query MCP Tools, an early access feature that is on by default in new installations and is switched under Settings, About, Manage features.

The tools reach two stores. VictoriaMetrics holds time series for all collected metric modules. ClickHouse holds structured log records in the tables irule_logs, the records from the telemetry iRule, and sslo_logs. Each query starts with discovery. For metrics the assistant lists the modules and groups, looks up the metric names and labels, then builds and runs a PromQL query. For logs it lists the tables and their columns, then builds SQL from those names. It never guesses a name it has not discovered.

The limits are tight on purpose. A metrics query may cover up to 30 days and return at most 100 series of 100 samples each. That caps resolution: a 24 hour question can have at most one point every 1,440 ÷ 100 = 14.4 minutes, and a 30 day question one point every 720 ÷ 100 = 7.2 hours. A ClickHouse query must be one read-only SELECT or WITH statement with a LIMIT of no more than 100, and returns at most 100 rows. Writes, schema changes, several statements and other tables are refused. The tools cannot read PostgreSQL, Vault, passwords or licences. Every tool call is recorded by the audit system, and if the audit record cannot be written the call is blocked. One privacy point remains: client IP columns in ClickHouse are returned as they are.

Chapter 9 of 16

A report card for post-quantum key exchange

Crypto agility measured three separate ways

The road to the weights showed hybrid key exchange on the S3 virtual server: X25519MLKEM768 combines a classical exchange with ML-KEM, a post-quantum one, and needs BIG-IP 17.5.1 or later. Insight 2.0 turns that into a fleet view called Post-Quantum Readiness, the crypto agility item on the deck's roadmap. It reads Client SSL profile configuration and handshake statistics over iControl REST and tmsh, needs no iRule, and changes nothing on the devices.

It keeps three measurements apart. Device support asks whether the software version supports PQC. Configuration asks whether each Client SSL profile on an enabled virtual server has PQC configured and TLS 1.3 enabled. Observed usage asks whether PQC key exchange has actually been negotiated with clients. Each can be true while the next is false. A device may support PQC with nothing configured, and a configured profile may never negotiate PQC because its clients do not offer a hybrid group.

The Home page card is Critical when every device is below 17.5.1, Needs Attention when any assessed profile has TLS 1.3 disabled or PQC not configured, Good when every evaluated profile has both, and N/A when there is nothing to assess. Per virtual server the statuses are In use, Not in use, Needs upgrade, Needs configuration change, Not enough traffic and Data unavailable. The last two are limits of the assessment and say nothing about whether a server is classical only.

Timing follows the collection. Configuration is read at the device's collection interval, 60 seconds by default, and handshake statistics every five minutes. The assessment works on a five minute cycle, or on the device's interval when that is longer. It uses configuration from one cycle plus a one minute buffer, 5 + 1 = 6 minutes, and handshakes from two cycles plus the buffer, 2 × 5 + 1 = 11 minutes. With a 10 minute interval those become 10 + 1 = 11 and 2 × 10 + 1 = 21. For Northlight the card shows Needs Attention: Frankfurt is configured, and Amsterdam and Madrid need an upgrade first.

Chapter 10 of 16

Stage the image before the window

Software distribution jobs copy the ISO ahead of time

Act starts before the window opens. Upgrading means two jobs in Insight: a distribution job that copies the software image to the devices, and an installation job that installs it. Splitting them lets the slow copy happen on an ordinary afternoon, so the window holds only the install and reboots.

The operator first uploads the 17.5.1 ISO to Insight under software image management. Then, under Manage, Automation, Jobs, Add Job, Software Distribution, they name the job, pick the target instances and the image. Images only go to matching hardware: TMOS images to BIG-IP instances and F5OS-A images to rSeries appliances, and Insight rejects a mismatched pair when the job runs. If the image were an Engineering Hotfix, Insight would offer to add its Base ISO, which the device needs first. An option, Remove unused images, deletes images that are neither running nor part of this job, and the checks list them before anything is removed.

Run preview checks validates each target. Image Compatibility blocks. Disk Space warns, shows free space in MiB or GiB and greys out when the image is already present. Base Image Pre Distribution warns about a missing Base ISO for a hotfix. Cleanup Preview lists what will be deleted. The copy runs Serial, one device at a time, or Parallel with a batch size.

The limits for one distribution job are 100 instances, 20 in parallel, 2 ISO files and 10 GB in total. Two distribution jobs can run at once, and a third waits in a queue. Northlight's job has 4 instances and 1 ISO, so a Parallel batch of 4 sends all four copies together. Aborting a distribution abandons the copies in flight. Detailed logs are in /opt/f5insight/logs/fleet-manager.log on the Insight host.

Chapter 11 of 16

One HA pair, step by step

What an HA pair installation job does, in order

On this scene the BIG-IP box now stands for Amsterdam's S3 pair. The operator creates a Software Installation job with Installation Type HA Pair. Its execution is always Serial. Three failover options sit on the form. Disable Auto Config-Sync stops changes on the Active unit reaching the Standby during the job and turns sync back on afterwards. Automatic failback returns the original Active to its role once both are upgraded and healthy. Pause before failback holds that failback until a person has checked the new Active. It is a separate setting under Automatic failback, outside the four installation pauses.

Target Version is 17.5.1, the staged image. Set Target Volume picks where it lands: First Inactive (Numerically), Last Inactive (Numerically) or Next Sequential (New). With HD1.1 active, First Inactive installs to HD1.2. Re-running the same job next month with a new version and the same setting then lands on HD1.1, so the job can be reused with one field changed. The instance drawer shows Hostname, MGMT IP, Platform, Version, HA Role, HA Peer and Service Check Date, and both members of the pair are selected.

Run preview checks marks each instance Ready or Failed. Blocking checks are Image Compatibility, Software Image Presence, Upgrade Path (a supported hop, per K13845), License Compatibility and License Date, which compares the Service Check Date with the target's License Check Date (K7727). HA Sync Status and Install Config only warn. Disk Space blocks when a new volume cannot be created and warns otherwise. A minor upgrade such as 17.1.2 to 17.5.1 needs licence reactivation, and 2.0 does that on the instance's behalf. The pre and post flight snapshot records trunk status, interface status, virtual server state, pool health, pool member health and tmsh show sys performance all-stats.

Execute asks for an optional change request number and the words BEGIN INSTALLATION. Then the order is fixed. Insight finds which unit is Active, upgrades the Standby, reboots it into HD1.2 and checks it, fails over, upgrades the former Active, and runs final health checks on both. Traffic keeps flowing through whichever unit is Active. The one moment that touches the GPUs is the failover: a model load streaming 34.3 GB through the Active unit at that second may lose its connections, so the operator holds Deployment rollouts, as described in Replacing a model server, until the pair is done.

Chapter 12 of 16

Fitting the change into three hours

Serial, parallel, separate jobs and the pauses a person keeps

Two pairs must be upgraded inside a three hour window that also has to hold a rollback. The deck gives a per device time. Ten devices upgraded one after another took 7.5 hours, which is 450 ÷ 10 = 45 minutes each, and the same ten in parallel took 45 minutes. F5's target for the next step is 30 minutes for ten in parallel. The deck marks that figure with an asterisk and its footnote is not in the deck text, so treat 30 minutes as a qualified target.

An HA pair job upgrades its two members one after the other, so a pair takes about 2 × 45 = 90 minutes. Several pairs in one job run serially, one pair at a time. Amsterdam and Madrid in one job would take 2 × 90 = 180 minutes, the whole window, with nothing left for a rollback. The documentation gives the other way: create a separate job for each pair and run them at the same time. Two jobs take about 90 minutes together and leave 90 for a rollback.

For devices that are not pairs there is the Standalone/Bulk type. Serial installs one instance at a time. Parallel installs in batches, up to 50 at once. An installation job holds up to 100 instances, or up to 50 HA pairs run one after another. Since 1.2.2, Standalone/Bulk accepts Active and Standby units too, with no automatic failover, so a team can upgrade all Standbys in one job, fail over on its own schedule, and upgrade the former Actives in a second job.

Pauses are where a person decides. An HA pair job offers four installation pauses: after install on the Standby before reboot, after the Standby reboots before failover, after failover before installing on the new Standby, and after that install before reboot. The documentation's common choice is the second and third. Pause before failback is a separate setting that belongs to Automatic failback, so a job can also stop before the original Active takes its role back. Pausing never stops the step already running. An abort halts what it can, and a BIG-IP that has started rebooting finishes its reboot. F5OS-A appliances differ: install and reboot are one step, a firmware upgrade can add a second reboot, and every BIG-IP tenant on the rSeries goes down with it, so the appliance hosting the Standby tenants goes first.

Chapter 13 of 16

When the new version misbehaves

Rollback, and the backup taken before it

A plan that keeps 90 minutes for rollback needs a rollback that works. Insight 2.0 has one, built on how BIG-IP installs. The old software is still on the old volume, so for a BIG-IP a rollback is a switchboot back to the volume it ran before the install, followed by a reboot. For an F5OS-A appliance Insight calls the platform's own f5-system-rollback.

Eligibility follows the phases of the install. A device that reached the REBOOT phase or later switched to the new volume and can be rolled back, whether the install ended COMPLETED or FAILED after the reboot. A device that failed earlier, during INSTALL or VALIDATION, never left its old volume and needs nothing. A rollback always targets the latest terminal execution of the job, meaning COMPLETED, FAILED or CANCELLED. A rollback cannot be rolled back. To go forward again the job is simply run again.

In the console the operator opens the execution, ticks devices (pairs are selected as pairs), acknowledges that configuration changes made since the install will be lost, optionally gives a change request, and types BEGIN ROLLBACK. The rollback inherits the HA order, pauses and failover settings of the original run, so the Standby goes back first and the Active follows after a controlled failover. Only administrators may do this. Through the API it is POST /api/v1/fleet/upgrade/jobs/software-installation/{jobId}/rollback with an optional body, answered with 202 Accepted and a new execution of type ROLLBACK. A second request while one runs gets 409, and a device outside the eligible set gets 400.

A backup is the other safety net. Backup jobs save a UCS archive for BIG-IP or an F5OS backup for rSeries, on demand or on a schedule in the Insight host's time zone, serially or in batches of 2 to 20, to Insight itself or to an NFS or SMB share. Private keys are included only when chosen. An installation job can take its own pre-upgrade backup. A restore can keep the master key for encrypted objects and the management address.

Chapter 14 of 16

Getting the data out and letting scripts in

Export destinations, the metrics proxy and machine users

The deck says Insight works in any model: Click-Ops, automation or AI-enabled. The console and AI Chat are two of those. The third rests on export and APIs.

Under System Management, F5 Exporter, Insight sends telemetry from its OpenTelemetry pipeline to three kinds of destination. Splunk takes it through an HTTP Event Collector endpoint, usually on port 8088, with a HEC token. Insight checks TLS first and then sends a sample event. Datadog takes it on its OTLP/HTTP endpoint with an API key, and Insight validates the key against the Datadog API, for the US site https://api.datadoghq.com/api/v1/validate. An external ClickHouse takes SSLO logs, iRule HTTP metrics or both over the native protocol on tcp:// port 9000 or tcps:// port 9440. Only one external ClickHouse is allowed, its table must exist first from a DDL template, and validation writes and removes a test row. A destination is Pending, Active, Error or Inactive. Renaming it does not restart the collector, and other changes may.

For dashboards there is the metrics proxy, a Prometheus-compatible API at https://<host>/api/metrics_proxy/api/v1 with HTTP Basic authentication. Insight strips the prefix, drops the Authorization header and forwards the query to VictoriaMetrics, so Grafana can use it as a data source. Plain HTTP is redirected with 301.

For automation there are machine users, service identities that authenticate with a client_id and client_secret using the OAuth 2.0 client_credentials grant, next to people with the Admin, Operator or Viewer role. A script can list fleet devices with GET /api/v1/fleet/upgrade/devices and refresh one with POST /api/v1/fleet/upgrade/devices/{deviceId}/refresh. The ID is the device's UUID, never its IP address.

Chapter 15 of 16

Three ways to run it

Air-gapped, connected, and hosted by F5

F5's deck offers one product in three deployment models. On-prem air-gapped is fully self-contained, needs no outbound connectivity, keeps full fleet management and operations, and is meant for government, defence and critical infrastructure. On-prem and SaaS connected keeps local control and adds SaaS intelligence: AI-powered recommendations, fleet insights, security automation. It is meant for enterprise, finance, healthcare and retail. SaaS hosted centralises management across distributed environments, with multi-tenant operations and global policy and lifecycle management, for managed service providers, enterprises and distributed organisations. All three share the AI assistant, fleet operations, security, automation, MCP tools and APIs. The 2.0.0 release is on-premises and self-hosted. The SaaS form factor is on the roadmap's last phase.

The documentation shows what air-gapped means in practice. Licensing has two modes. Connected sends a licence report to product.apis.f5.com and receives a signed manifest automatically. Disconnected uses the same exchange by hand: Insight writes the report, a person carries it to a connected system and submits it, and carries the signed manifest back. The report is a JWT signed with HS256. The manifest is signed by F5 with RS512 and names its key set at product.apis.f5.com/ee/v1/keys/jwks. It lists entitled features such as insight_data_storage_gb and insight_aidf_allowance. When storage fills, Insight deletes the oldest records.

Other features follow the same rule. AIDF needs outbound access, so it is off. AI Chat needs a model inside the site, an OpenAI-compatible server reached internally, with the 120 billion parameter advice still in force. Splunk and ClickHouse exports work to internal endpoints. Datadog cannot be added, because its key check calls the Datadog API. A cloud with no way out describes the transfer station this exchange uses, and Who controls the cloud describes why some sites need it. Commercially, the deck lists Insight in blocks of 500 GB of data processed per month and a free 30 day trial. The documentation says Insight 1.x and 2.0.x do not enforce the 30 day trial limit, so the product keeps working after a trial expires. The 500 GB blocks count data processed, and the documentation does not say how they relate to the insight_data_storage_gb value in a manifest.

Chapter 16 of 16

From observability to a platform

Where F5 is taking Insight next

The deck's vision is one experience across the F5 estate: BIG-IP, NGINX and Distributed Cloud (XC), with room for future solutions. It has four parts. Derive insight from observability, such as recommendations and optimisations. Manage the fleet and its configuration intelligently. Operate in any model: Click-Ops, automation or AI-enabled. Be extensible, with reusable use cases and workflows that customers shape to their needs. F5's line for it is that Insight will be the operational experience for ADSP.

The roadmap has five phases. Observe, understand the environment, came at AppWorld 2026 with observability. Operate, act and manage with confidence, came in July 2026 with fleet management and the F5 assistant. Enhance, understand applications and shared services, is dated October 2026 with application identification, crypto agility and F5OS fleet management. Those three match what 2.0 documents today. Unify, across the F5 portfolio, is planned for the first half of calendar 2027 with configuration management, cross-platform security, observability and shared solution use cases. Extend, a continuously extensible platform, is planned for the second half of 2027 with the SaaS form factor, Insight Studio for extensibility, policy management, management and observability.

Fleet management has its own list. Ten devices in parallel go from 45 minutes now to a target of 30, a figure the deck marks with an asterisk. Scale goes from up to 150 instances to up to 250.
