Kubernetes underneath

Replacing a model server

A rolling update needs somewhere to put the new Pod, and every GPU is taken

The Deployment qwen3 usually runs two model servers. For this lesson it has been scaled to eight, one on each of the eight GPUs of gpu-node-1 and gpu-node-2, and a new version of vLLM is out. Kubernetes replaces the Pods a few at a time. This is called a rolling update. For a web server the usual plan is to start a new Pod first and remove an old one once the new one answers. That plan assumes there is room for one more Pod. On a GPU fleet there is none, because every card already has an owner. This lesson follows the update through its settings and through the arithmetic the Deployment controller does on every pass. It shows which old Pod is picked to go, how the update stalls with a Pod that can never be placed, what the status fields and commands say while it is stuck, and what the update costs when it is done the way GPU fleets do it. It ends with the other ways a rollout starts, stops and goes back, and with the upgrade of the gateway in front of the servers, which converts data on the way and cannot go back with Helm.

Chapter 1 of 14

One line changes, and a second ReplicaSet appears

A Deployment never edits a Pod. It starts a new set of them

A Deployment does not own Pods directly. It owns a ReplicaSet, an object whose only job is to keep a fixed number of identical Pods alive. You change the image in the Deployment's Pod template, the part under spec.template that describes one Pod. The Deployment controller notices that no ReplicaSet matches the new template. It creates a second one and gives it a label called pod-template-hash so the two sets can be told apart. The hash is computed from the template. In the rare case two templates hash to the same value, the controller raises status.collisionCount by one and hashes again.

The label is added to the new ReplicaSet's selector, to its Pod template, and so to every Pod it creates. The documentation says plainly not to change it. The Deployment's own spec.selector cannot be changed at all after creation in apps/v1. That is why the hash label is needed: both ReplicaSets carry app: qwen3, and only the hash keeps each one's selector from matching the other's Pods.

The controller also writes its bookkeeping on the ReplicaSet as annotations. deployment.kubernetes.io/revision is 2 on the new set and 1 on the old. desired-replicas and max-replicas record the Deployment's size and its size plus surge, which is how the controller later tells a scale change from a rollout. The controller sets the new set's size when it creates it. With the default settings and 8 replicas that is 2 Pods. It is 0 only when maxSurge is 0. Only a change inside spec.template starts a rollout. Changing the number of replicas does not, so scaling never creates a revision.

Chapter 2 of 14

Two numbers set the pace

One allows extra Pods, the other allows missing ones

Two fields under spec.strategy.rollingUpdate decide how the two ReplicaSets trade places. maxSurge is how many Pods may exist above the wanted number. maxUnavailable is how many of the wanted Pods may be out of service. Each can be a whole number or a percentage of replicas, and each defaults to 25%. A percentage is turned into a count, and the two round in opposite directions. maxSurge rounds up and maxUnavailable rounds down. For 8 replicas both come to exactly 2. For 6 replicas, 25% is 1.5, so the surge is 2 and the allowance is 1. For 2 replicas it is 0.5, giving 1 and 0.

They cannot both be zero, because then no Pod could ever be added or removed. The API rejects that with an error on the field maxUnavailable that reads may not be 0 when maxSurge is 0. It also rejects a maxUnavailable above 100%, since no more than all of the Pods can be missing. maxSurge has no such upper check: a surge of 200% is accepted and would ask for up to three times the Pods. A subtler case gets through validation. Take 3 replicas with maxSurge 0 and maxUnavailable 25%. 25% of 3 is 0.75, which rounds down to 0. Both now resolve to zero, so the controller quietly uses maxUnavailable 1 instead. Its source comment gives the reason: surge might not work because of quota.

The two results become a ceiling and a floor. The ceiling is replicas plus maxSurge, the most Pods that may be wanted across both ReplicaSets at once. The floor is replicas minus maxUnavailable, the fewest that must stay available. For eight replicas under the defaults that is at most 10 and at least 6.

Chapter 3 of 14

One pass of the controller does one thing

Scale the new set up if it may, else scale the old set down

The Deployment controller works in passes. Each pass looks at the Deployment and its ReplicaSets and makes at most one kind of change. First it tries to scale the new ReplicaSet up. If it did, the pass ends. Only if nothing was scaled up does it try to scale old ReplicaSets down. Only when neither happened and the rollout is complete does it clean up old history.

The scale up counts wanted Pods, the replicas field of every ReplicaSet added together, and compares that with the ceiling. Under the defaults the ceiling is 10. If the total is already 10 or more, the new set is not touched. Otherwise it grows by the room left, but never past 8. The count is of wanted replicas in the ReplicaSet specs, not of Pods that exist. A terminating old Pod has already left its ReplicaSet's count, so it never holds back the creation of a new Pod, even while it still holds a GPU.

The scale down uses a formula from the source: all wanted Pods, minus the floor, minus the new Pods that are not yet available. New Pods that are Pending or still loading are subtracted, so they cannot be used as an excuse to remove more old ones. Worked on the defaults: the new set starts at 2, so the allowance is 10 minus 6 minus 2, which is 2, and the old set goes from 8 to 6. On the next pass the total is 8, so the new set rises to 4. Then the allowance is 10 minus 6 minus 4, which is 0, and the rollout waits.

Chapter 4 of 14

Ready is the gate for every step

More old Pods may go only when new ones count as available

This chapter follows the defaults. Every GPU is taken, so the four new Pods start out Pending. Say an old Pod takes 30 seconds to stop. Then two cards are free, and two of the four new Pods are bound to them and start. The other two wait. The controller never looks inside a Pod. It counts. A new Pod counts once its Ready condition is true. That needs every container in the Pod to be ready, which for the vllm container means its readiness probe has passed, and every condition named in the Pod's readinessGates, if it lists any, to be True. This Pod has one container and no readiness gates, so the probe decides. For this model that takes about three minutes, because the server first loads 34.3 GB of weights, the size of the FP8 build Qwen3-32B-FP8, into GPU memory.

A further setting, minReadySeconds, makes a Pod stay Ready that many seconds before it is called available. The default is 0. If it is set, progressDeadlineSeconds must be larger, or the API refuses the Deployment with must be greater than minReadySeconds. Only once new Pods are available does the controller shrink the old ReplicaSet again.

The rounds then repeat. At 210 seconds two new Pods are available, eight are available in all, and the allowance is 10 minus 6 minus 2, which is 2. The old set goes from 6 to 4. That pass changed nothing on the new side, because the total was 6 plus 4, which is 10, already at the ceiling. On the next pass the total is 8, and the new set grows to 6. Each round frees two cards after 30 seconds and loads for 180, so each round is 210 seconds. After four rounds, at 840 seconds, all eight run the new image. Throughout, never fewer than six serve, and two Pending Pods wait in every round but the last. A rollout moves at the speed at which new servers finish loading, and both versions serve side by side the whole time.

Chapter 5 of 14

Which old Pod is removed

The Deployment sets a number. The ReplicaSet picks the Pod

When the controller lowers the old ReplicaSet from 6 to 4, it names no Pod. It only changes a number. The ReplicaSet controller then decides which two Pods to delete, by sorting its Pods and taking them from the front. The order, from the Kubernetes source, is this. A Pod not yet assigned to a node goes before one that is. A Pending Pod goes before an Unknown one, and an Unknown one before a Running one. A Pod that is not ready goes before one that is. A Pod with a lower deletion cost goes before one with a higher cost. A Pod on a node that holds more active Pods of the same Deployment goes first. A Pod that has been ready for less time goes first. A Pod whose containers restarted more goes first. A newer Pod goes before an older one.

The documentation adds that creation times are grouped on a logarithmic scale before they are compared, and that when everything matches the choice is random. On this fleet the old Pods are all bound, Running and Ready, with no deletion cost set. The first four rules tie. The fifth favours removing from whichever node holds more active Pods of the same Deployment, old and new counted together, which tends to spread the removals over both nodes. After that the choice falls to how long each has been Ready, its restarts and its age.

You can steer it. The annotation controller.kubernetes.io/pod-deletion-cost takes a whole number from minus 2147483648 to 2147483647, and a Pod without it counts as 0. Lower goes first. It is on by default since 1.22. A model server holding a warm prefix cache could carry a higher cost, so it is replaced last. The documentation warns that this is best effort and that updating the annotation often loads the API server.

Chapter 6 of 14

The extra Pod has nowhere to go

Surge first is the web habit. It needs a ninth GPU

A common setting for web services is maxSurge 1 and maxUnavailable 0. It means: never drop below full strength, and add one Pod at a time. Start the update again from eight old Pods with this setting. Each setting tried from here on is its own run of the same update, and each run starts from the same point: eight Pods of revision 1, the old ReplicaSet at 8 and no new one yet, as if the earlier run had never happened. The clock only keeps the runs in order. The ceiling is 8 plus 1, which is 9. The floor is 8 minus 0, which is 8. The controller creates the new ReplicaSet at 1, so nine Pods are now wanted.

The ninth asks for one nvidia.com/gpu like the others. The scheduler subtracts what is already requested on each node and finds four of four taken on both. It cannot place the Pod, and the Pod stays Pending with the condition PodScheduled False and the reason Unschedulable. The FailedScheduling event has two parts. The first counts the reasons per node: 0/2 nodes are available: 2 Insufficient nvidia.com/gpu. The second comes from preemption, the scheduler's attempt to make room by evicting Pods of lower priority. The old Pods have the same priority as the new one, so it finds nothing to evict and adds preemption: 0/2 nodes are available: 2 No preemption victims found for incoming pod. It retries whenever something in the cluster changes, and nothing relevant will.

Now run the scale down formula. All wanted Pods are 9. The floor is 8. The new Pods that are not available number 1. 9 minus 8 minus 1 is 0, so no old Pod may go. Read it the other way and the deadlock is plain. The old Pods hold the GPUs. The new Pod waits for a GPU. The controller may not remove an old Pod, because that would leave seven available and the floor is eight. Neither side will move.

The same trap catches kubectl rollout restart, which works by writing the annotation kubectl.kubernetes.io/restartedAt with the current time into the Pod template. That is a change under spec.template, so it is an ordinary rollout under the same two numbers, and on a full fleet with this setting it stalls the same way.

Chapter 7 of 14

Ten minutes later the rollout reports itself stuck

Kubernetes tells you. It does not fix it

The Deployment has a clock for this, progressDeadlineSeconds, with a default of 600. Progress has an exact meaning in the source. It is any of four changes: updatedReplicas went up, the number of old Pods went down, readyReplicas went up, or availableReplicas went up. A Pod that is Running and still loading its model changes none of them. Each time progress is seen, the controller refreshes the lastUpdateTime of the Progressing condition and keeps its lastTransitionTime. The deadline is measured from lastUpdateTime. So 600 seconds is the longest silence allowed between two pieces of progress. It is not a limit on the whole rollout. With maxSurge 0 and maxUnavailable 1, worked out in a later chapter, a rollout here takes 28 minutes and never trips it, because a new Pod becomes available every 210 seconds.

The stall here needs maxUnavailable 0. With any allowance the controller would remove an old Pod and free a card. When ten minutes pass with no progress, the controller sets the Progressing condition to False with the reason ProgressDeadlineExceeded and the message ReplicaSet "qwen3-58b4d7f9c6" has timed out progressing. That is all it does. The documentation says Kubernetes takes no other action, and leaves a rollback to higher level tools that read the condition. The controller keeps trying. It does not need to poll for this. It schedules its own check for lastUpdateTime plus the deadline, plus one second.

The documented causes of a stuck rollout are insufficient quota, readiness probe failures, image pull errors, insufficient permissions, limit ranges and application misconfiguration. A full GPU fleet is none of these by name. It shows as a Pending Pod with FailedScheduling.

Chapter 8 of 14

What an operator reads while it is stuck

Three commands, and the counters behind each column

kubectl get deployment qwen3 prints five columns: NAME, READY, UP-TO-DATE, AVAILABLE and AGE. In the stalled state they read qwen3, 8/8, 1, 8. READY is ready Pods over wanted replicas, and eight old Pods are ready. UP-TO-DATE is updatedReplicas, Pods from the new ReplicaSet, which is the one Pending Pod. AVAILABLE is 8. Nothing in that line looks wrong.

kubectl describe deployment qwen3 says more. Its Replicas line reads 8 desired, 1 updated, 9 total, 8 available, 1 unavailable. Total is the number of Pods the ReplicaSets report, eight old plus one new. Unavailable is the nine wanted across both sets minus the eight available. The describe output also shows StrategyType RollingUpdate, MinReadySeconds 0, RollingUpdateStrategy as 0 max unavailable, 1 max surge, and a Conditions table. The Available condition is True whenever available Pods are at or above the floor. Its reason is MinimumReplicasAvailable, its message Deployment has minimum availability. Progressing carries the timeout.

A third view matters for GPUs. Pods that are terminating are not counted in availableReplicas, and the documentation warns that a Deployment may then hold more Pods, and use more resources, than replicas plus maxSurge until those Pods finish. Newer clusters show them in status.terminatingReplicas, a field behind the feature gate DeploymentReplicaSetTerminatingReplicas, alpha and off by default in 1.33 and 1.34, beta and on by default from 1.35. For a model server that is exactly the count of GPUs held by Pods that serve nothing.

Chapter 9 of 14

Give up one GPU, then take it back

maxSurge 0 and maxUnavailable 1: remove first, then replace

GPU fleets usually turn the two numbers around. maxSurge 0 means no Pod above eight is ever wanted. maxUnavailable 1 means one of the eight may be missing. Start once more from the same point, eight old Pods of revision 1 and no new ReplicaSet. The new ReplicaSet is created at 0, because the total is already at the ceiling of 8. The first pass cannot scale up, so it scales down. The allowance is 8 minus 7 minus 0, which is 1, and the old ReplicaSet goes to 7. One old Pod is asked to stop.

On the next pass the total is 7, so the new set rises to 1 at once. That Pod is Pending until the old one is really gone, because a stopping Pod still holds its GPU. The allowance is now 8 minus 7 minus 1, which is 0, so nothing else moves. Say stopping takes 30 seconds. Then the new Pod is placed on the freed card and loads for 180 seconds. One replacement takes 210 seconds, and for all of it seven servers carry the traffic of eight. When the new Pod is available, the allowance becomes 1 again and the next old Pod goes. Eight replacements take 8 times 210, which is 1,680 seconds or 28 minutes.

The Available condition stays True all the way, since seven is the floor. Progress is seen at least every 210 seconds, well inside the 600 second deadline. The time to stop an old Pod is set by its shutdown, up to its terminationGracePeriodSeconds. A model server given 90 seconds of grace to finish long answers can make every step up to a minute longer.

Chapter 10 of 14

Shorter and deeper, or longer and shallower

The lost capacity is the same. You choose its shape

Raise maxUnavailable to 2 and two old Pods go at once. The update has four rounds of 210 seconds, so it ends in 14 minutes, and for those minutes six servers do the work of eight. Compare what is lost. One GPU missing for 28 minutes is 28 GPU-minutes. Two missing for 14 minutes is also 28. The total is fixed by how long one replacement takes, 8 Pods times 210 seconds, which is 1,680 GPU-seconds. The setting only chooses between a shallow dip that lasts and a deep one that is short. The defaults behave like the second case here, since 25% of 8 is 2 for both fields. Their surge of 2 just adds two Pending Pods that wait for cards.

The only way to lose nothing is a spare GPU for the surge Pod, idle the rest of the time. Work it through with a ninth card and maxSurge 1, maxUnavailable 0. The first new Pod lands on the spare and loads for 180 seconds while eight old ones serve. Then one old Pod goes, and the next new Pod is created at once but waits 30 seconds for that card. Every later round is 210 seconds again. The whole update takes 180 plus 7 times 210, which is 1,650 seconds or 27.5 minutes, with eight serving throughout. The price is a GPU that sits idle between updates.

On a shared pool the owner's notes add another option and a warning. Spare capacity can be lent to other workloads and reclaimed. A scheme that splits one workload across two Deployments to do that makes rolling updates harder, because each Deployment rolls on its own.

Chapter 11 of 14

Going back is another rollout

The old ReplicaSet was kept for this

When the update finishes, the old ReplicaSet is not deleted. It stays at zero Pods with its template intact, and it is the record of revision 1. A rollout is complete when four things hold: updatedReplicas, replicas and availableReplicas all equal 8, and the controller has observed the latest generation. The Progressing condition then turns True with reason NewReplicaSetAvailable, and from then on the deadline is no longer checked.

The field revisionHistoryLimit says how many old ReplicaSets to keep, and the default is 10. The documentation says history is cleaned up after a rollout completes. The controller's source does it at one other time too: on a pass over a paused Deployment with no rollback in flight. That has a side effect. A Deployment that never completes, because Pods crash or a rollout stalls, can collect more old ReplicaSets than the limit. Set the limit to 0 and there is nothing to go back to. Old ReplicaSets cost a little space in etcd and crowd kubectl get rs, and that is the whole cost of keeping them.

kubectl rollout history lists the revisions, with a CHANGE-CAUSE column copied from the annotation kubernetes.io/change-cause. kubectl rollout undo puts the previous template back into the Deployment, and --to-revision picks an older one. kubectl does this itself: it reads the template of the chosen revision from its ReplicaSet and sends it to the API server as a patch of spec.template. The controller only sees an ordinary template change. The Deployments page still shows a DeploymentRollback event in its example, but the controller emits that event only on an older path, driven by the deprecated annotation deprecated.deployment.rollback.to, which kubectl leaves out. After a plain undo the events are the usual ScalingReplicaSet lines. Only the Pod template is restored, never the replica count. And an undo is itself a rolling update, under the same two numbers. Here they are still maxSurge 0 and maxUnavailable 1, the setting the update just finished with. For a stalled rollout that is quick, since only one Pending Pod has to go. After a completed one it means another 28 minutes at seven of eight under that setting, or 14 minutes at six of eight under the defaults, because the old servers must load their weights again.

Chapter 12 of 14

Pause, restart, and a change in the middle

Other ways a rollout starts, waits, or throws work away

kubectl rollout pause deployment/qwen3 stops the controller from acting on template changes. It prints deployment.apps/qwen3 paused. While paused, edits to the template are stored and start nothing, the Progressing condition shows status Unknown with reason DeploymentPaused, and the deadline is not checked. A paused Deployment cannot be rolled back until it is resumed. Pause is how you batch several template changes, such as a new image and a new argument, into one rollout and one round of model loading. kubectl rollout resume starts it.

kubectl rollout restart writes the annotation kubectl.kubernetes.io/restartedAt with the current time into the Pod template. It is an ordinary rollout and creates a new revision, so it obeys the same two numbers and costs the same time: 28 minutes with maxSurge 0 and maxUnavailable 1, or 14 minutes with the defaults, which on this full fleet act like maxUnavailable 2.

A change in the middle of a rollout is called a rollover. The controller does not wait. It creates a ReplicaSet for the newest template, treats the one it was growing as old, and starts shrinking it. Unhealthy old Pods are removed first, and Pods still loading count as unavailable. For model servers that means Pods partway through a three minute load are thrown away, and the GPU time they spent is lost.

If an autoscaler changes replicas during a rollout, the controller spreads the change over the ReplicaSets that have Pods, in proportion to their size, with leftovers going to the largest. A PodDisruptionBudget does not slow a rolling update. The documentation says workload controllers are not limited by PDBs during rolling upgrades, although the Pods they remove count against the budget.

Chapter 13 of 14

Recreate: everything stops first

The same GPU-minutes, spent all at once

The other strategy type is Recreate. It has no surge and no allowance. The controller scales every old ReplicaSet to 0. Then it waits until no old Pod is still running. Pods in phase Failed or Succeeded are not counted. Only then does it create the new ReplicaSet at full size. The documentation states the guarantee: successful removal is awaited before any Pod of the new revision is created. That guarantee covers the upgrade only. If you delete a Pod by hand, its replacement is created straight away, terminating or not.

Work it out with the same numbers. All eight old Pods are told to stop at once. After 30 seconds they are gone and all eight cards are free. Eight new Pods are created, bound and start loading. After 180 seconds of loading they are all Ready. The update takes 210 seconds, three and a half minutes, and for all of it nothing serves. The lost capacity is 8 GPUs times 210 seconds, which is 1,680 GPU-seconds, the same 28 GPU-minutes as every rolling setting. Recreate is the deepest and shortest shape of the same loss.

It also loads every server at once. Eight copies of the 34.3 GB FP8 build are read from storage together, 274.4 GB in the same three minutes, which can make the load slower than one server's 180 seconds. Recreate suits a model that must never run two versions side by side, such as a server that writes to a shared cache whose format changed. For a public endpoint, three and a half minutes of refusals is usually the wrong trade.

Chapter 14 of 14

The gateway's upgrade only goes one way

Helm can put back a chart, not the data an upgrade converted

Every request to qwen3 enters the cluster at the gateway, 203.0.113.80, and in this cluster the gateway is F5 BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes, BNK for short. Its software is replaced in a different way from the model servers. BNK is installed and run by an operator, the F5 Lifecycle Operator, FLO. An operator is a controller that watches one kind of object and builds everything that object asks for. FLO watches one object of the kind CNEInstance. Its field manifestVersion names a release manifest, the list of every BNK component and version that belongs to one BNK release, and FLO deploys that list. FLO itself is installed with Helm. Helm is a package manager for Kubernetes: a chart is a set of templates, an installed copy of a chart is a release, and every install, upgrade or rollback of a release is stored as a numbered revision. The documented BNK upgrade has two steps in this order. First helm upgrade the Lifecycle Operator to the new chart. Then raise manifestVersion on the CNEInstance and let FLO move each component. For BNK 2.2.1 to 2.3.0 the chart goes from v2.9.27-0.3.4 to v2.21.13-0.0.28, and manifestVersion goes from 2.2.1-3.2226.0-0.0.511 to 2.3.0-3.2598.3-0.0.170.

Helm keeps its history in the cluster. Helm 3 stores each revision as a Secret in the release's namespace, named sh.helm.release.v1.<release>.v<revision>, with type helm.sh/release.v1 and the label owner=helm. The Secret holds the manifest Helm rendered for that revision. helm rollback f5-lifecycle-operator 1 does not render the chart again. It reads the manifest stored in revision 1, applies it with a three-way merge that compares the old manifest, the live objects and the target, and records the result as a new revision, 3, with the description Rollback to 1. Revision 2 becomes superseded. This is the same idea as kubectl rollout undo earlier in this lesson: going back is a new revision built from a stored copy. Helm keeps 10 revisions by default, set by --history-max. The stored copy has a limit. Helm knows only the objects listed in that manifest. It never installs or upgrades CRDs during an upgrade or a rollback. Anything a component changed in the cluster while it ran is outside every revision.

The upgrade from 2.2.1 to 2.3.0 changes such data. It converts two objects, cpcl-config-cm and cpcl-key-cm, from ConfigMaps to Secrets, because the new CWC requires Secrets. CWC is the Cluster Wide Controller, the BNK component that checks the licence. Its log line ResponseCM20LicenseVerified is how a lab confirms a licence was accepted. A ConfigMap and a Secret are two different kinds of object, and a program that asks for the ConfigMap cpcl-key-cm does not find a Secret of the same name. The conversion is one-way. No step turns the Secrets back into ConfigMaps, and no Helm revision contains the ConfigMaps as they were. So helm rollback brings back the 2.2.1 operator and its manifest, and the 2.2.1 components then look for a shape that no longer exists. Kubernetes shows the general case. A Pod that needs a Secret or ConfigMap that is not there, and not marked optional, does not start. The kubelet keeps retrying and reports an Event naming the missing object. The only way back across the conversion is to restore from a backup taken before the upgrade.

The owner's BNK installer turns this into rules it enforces before it touches anything. It refuses to upgrade a cluster whose CNEInstance condition Available is not True, because a fault that was already there would look like upgrade damage and would become the rollback target. It resolves the target manifestVersion against the registry first. FLO accepts a manifestVersion that does not exist without complaint: the value is recorded, Available stays True, nothing degrades, and the upgrade appears to succeed while nothing changes, because no component has a new chart to pull. It requires the Helm release to be in status deployed. It will not work on top of a release left in failed, and it names the command to investigate that release first. A release left in pending-upgrade makes Helm stop with another operation (install/upgrade/rollback) is in progress. Then it writes a snapshot of the cluster's state, kept as a build artifact for 90 days. The snapshot records whether the cpcl objects were ConfigMaps or Secrets. A rollback compares that record with the cluster and refuses when the shape has changed, and says to restore from backup. The health gate after an upgrade checks that the cluster is healthy, that the operator has observed the new spec and that the spec carries the target. It does not check that each component's image moved.

The second trap is a race between two writers of one Secret. The operator chart renders a Secret named external-otelsvr-secret, which holds a certificate for BNK's OpenTelemetry (OTEL) telemetry server. The OTEL certificates are also cert-manager Certificate objects, external-otelsvr and external-f5ingotelsvr, with RSA 4096 keys and rotationPolicy: Always. The owner's notes say the OTEL certificates create a Certificate with the same secretName, external-otelsvr-secret. They do not say which of the two it is. cert-manager is the controller that issues certificates and keeps them in Secrets. With rotationPolicy Always it generates a new private key at every issuance, and it waits until the new certificate is signed before it overwrites tls.key in the Secret. Always became the default in cert-manager v1.18.0. Before that the default was Never, which reuses the key, so on the v1.15 and v1.16 releases these labs pin, Always is set on purpose. By default a certificate is renewed two thirds of the way through its life, which for the default duration of 90 days is day 60. Helm and cert-manager both manage external-otelsvr-secret. In the lab a Helm render that looked the Secret up failed with no Secret with the name ... found, and the notes tie it to rotation. An overwrite of tls.key leaves the Secret in place, so a rotation on its own would not make the lookup miss. What briefly left no Secret to find is not recorded. Helm's lookup function returns an empty value when an object is not there, and a chart that needs the object has to stop at that point. The installer retries Helm operations on that exact error. In the lab a first attempt at the rollback procedure failed this way and succeeded on the retry. helm rollback itself does not render the chart, so the step that failed was another Helm operation in that procedure, and the notes do not say which.

The model servers are not touched by any of this. Their eight Pods keep their GPUs and their loaded weights, and nothing has to load 34.3 GB again. The risk sits in front of them. A Deployment can always go back to the template its old ReplicaSet kept, at a price of 28 GPU-minutes. A BNK upgrade past a one-way conversion cannot go back with Helm at all. If the restored gateway does not come up, all eight servers are healthy and unreachable. So the upgrade starts from a healthy cluster, with a backup, a snapshot and a target version that is known to exist.

What to keep

An update is a loan of GPUs

A rolling update is two ReplicaSets trading Pods under two limits. Each pass of the controller scales the new set up if it may, else scales the old set down by an allowance that subtracts new Pods not yet available, and each step waits for a new Pod to be Ready. On CPU that wait is seconds and an extra Pod fits somewhere. On GPUs the wait is the three minutes a model takes to load, and an extra Pod needs a whole free card. With eight Pods on eight GPUs a surge of one with no Pod allowed to be missing stalls, the one line view stays green, and ten minutes later the Deployment says ProgressDeadlineExceeded. Turning the numbers around works: remove one, wait for its GPU, load, repeat. The price is 28 GPU-minutes of serving, whether spent as 28 minutes at seven of eight, 14 at six, or three and a half at none with Recreate, and the same again to undo it. Only a spare GPU avoids it. Plan the update for an hour when seven are enough. The gateway in front of the servers is different again: its Helm history can bring back the operator, but not the ConfigMaps that BNK 2.3.0 turned into Secrets, so that upgrade needs a backup before it starts.
