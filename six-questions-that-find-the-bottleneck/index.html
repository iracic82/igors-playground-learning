Operations

Six questions that find the bottleneck

From what a platform team says, to what the workers measure, to what fixes it

A team running models on GPUs rarely reports a cause. It reports a feeling. The chat is slow. The agents feel sluggish at lunchtime. We bought more GPUs and the bill went up faster than the speed. Each of those sentences can come from at least three different faults, and each fault has a different fix. Adding GPUs fixes one of them and makes another more expensive.

F5 gives its engineers six questions to ask the architects and platform operators of an inference cluster. Each question comes with what to listen for in the answer and the measured result F5 points to. The questions cover how requests are routed, where latency appears, what the router counts during a burst, what a token costs, how teams and clusters share GPUs, and who runs the network on day two.

This lesson turns each question into a triage step. For each one it shows the mechanism behind the symptom, the counters on a vLLM worker that confirm it, a worked example on this site's cluster (Qwen3-32B from its FP8 build, 34.3 GB of weights, on Pods qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-x7k2p and qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-m9q4t), and the F5 capability that answers it. The technical depth for each answer lives in other lessons, which are named where they apply. The scene also shows a third worker, gpu-3, beside the two Pods, so that three workers can be compared side by side. Its readings are illustrative. Here the work is to get from a sentence a customer says to the number that proves it.

Chapter 1 of 15

A complaint is a symptom

The sentence a team says, the number behind it, and what fixes it

Triage starts by refusing to accept the first explanation. "The GPUs are overloaded" is a conclusion, and a GPU can be full in at least four ways: its queue is long, its KV cache is full, its cores are busy reading one huge prompt, or it is running slow because it is hot. Each of these shows on a different counter, and only some of them are fixed by sending traffic somewhere else.

The frame used in this lesson has four columns. The first is what the team says, in its own words. The second is the measurement that confirms or rejects it. On a vLLM worker those measurements are on the /metrics page on port 8000: gauges such as vllm:num_requests_waiting and vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc, counters such as vllm:prefix_cache_hits, and histograms such as vllm:time_to_first_token_seconds. The third column is the cause the number points to. The fourth is the capability that removes the cause, which may be F5's, may be a vLLM setting, or may be a purchase.

The order matters. A question that the team answers with "round robin" already tells you a lot before any metric is read, because round robin cannot see the cache or the size of a prompt. A question about the first word and the stream splits latency into two clocks that have different owners. The six questions are arranged so that each answer narrows the next. They are F5's, from its Distributed Inference session, and they are meant to be asked of the people who run the cluster, the AI infrastructure architects and the platform operators.

Chapter 2 of 15

Question one: how are requests spread over the Pods

Listen for kube-proxy, an Ingress, or round robin

F5's first question: as you scale across multiple GPU Pods, how are you load balancing requests today, and does your router know which GPU already has the prompt's context cached? The answers to listen for are a standard Kubernetes Service, an Ingress, or round robin. Each of them spreads requests without looking at the prompt.

A Service is the most common answer, and its behaviour is documented. In kube-proxy's iptables and nftables modes, the rules installed for each Service pick a backend Pod at random. The ipvs mode, deprecated since Kubernetes v1.35, offers round robin (rr) and other schedulers. The choice is made once per connection. A client that keeps one HTTP connection open sends every request on it to the same Pod, so the spread depends on how the client and any proxy in between reuse connections. The Service field spec.sessionAffinity can be set to ClientIP, with a default stickiness of 10,800 seconds, three hours. Behind a gateway every request arrives from the gateway's own address, so ClientIP affinity sends all of them to one Pod.

An Ingress or a gateway in front does the same job per request, with its own rule. Envoy Gateway, for example, uses Least Request when no policy says otherwise. None of these rules knows that turn ten of a conversation was prefilled on qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-x7k2p. With two Pods and a random pick, the next turn lands on the Pod that holds the conversation's cache half the time. With three workers it is a third, and with the eight of F5's benchmark it is one in eight. The deeper story of what is lost is in the lesson Turn ten at the price of turn two.

Chapter 3 of 15

What spraying a shared prompt costs

Recomputed context, duplicate copies, and the counter that shows it

A miss costs the whole prompt again. In the lesson Prefill and decode a worker reads prompts at about 0.21 milliseconds a token, an illustrative rate for this model on one H100. Turn ten of a conversation carries about 20,000 tokens of history. On the Pod that holds the first 19,200 of them, only the last 800 need prefill: 800 × 0.21 ms is about 0.17 seconds before the first word. On the other Pod all 20,000 are read again, about 4.2 seconds. The user sees a first word 25 times later, and the GPU spends about 4 seconds of compute on work it had already done.

The same spray also fills memory with copies. Each token of Qwen3-32B costs 64 layers × 2 (key and value) × 8 heads × 128 numbers × 2 bytes, 262,144 bytes. A shared system prompt of 2,000 tokens is 524 MB of KV cache. Sent at random to three workers, it ends up cached three times, 1.57 GB, of which 1.05 GB are duplicates. A router that sends matching prompts to the warm worker keeps one copy hot and leaves the other memory for other conversations.

The counter that proves it is the prefix cache hit rate, vllm:prefix_cache_hits divided by vllm:prefix_cache_queries, both counted in tokens. Read it per worker, and read it as a rate over a window, since both are counters. A chat or agent workload with a long shared system prompt and multi-turn history should find a large share of its tokens cached. A low rate there points at placement. F5's proof point for this question is that prefix-aware routing steers matching prompts to warm GPUs and reclaims 38 to 56% of GPU capacity. The slide does not say how that share was measured. On a fleet of eight GPUs it is the work of about 3 to 4.5 GPUs.

Chapter 4 of 15

Question two: the first word, or the stream

Two clocks, two phases, two different owners

F5's second question is the most useful one to ask early: when responses lag under load, are users waiting a long time for the first word to appear, or does the streaming text stutter once it starts? The two answers point at the two phases of every request. The first word is the end of prefill, when the whole prompt has been read. The stutter is in decode, where each new token is one pass over the weights.

vLLM measures both from four events inside its engine. QUEUED is when the request enters the scheduler's queue. SCHEDULED is when it is first given a place in a batch. NEW_TOKENS is each time an output is produced, and PREEMPTED is when a running request is pushed back to the queue to make room. From these the engine works out the queue interval, from QUEUED to the most recent SCHEDULED, the prefill interval, from that SCHEDULED to the first NEW_TOKENS, the decode interval, from the first to the last NEW_TOKENS, and the inter-token interval, between successive NEW_TOKENS.

They land in histograms. vllm:time_to_first_token_seconds is the wait for the first word. vllm:request_queue_time_seconds and vllm:request_prefill_time_seconds split that wait into its two main parts. vllm:inter_token_latency_seconds records every gap in the stream. vllm:request_time_per_output_token_seconds records one value per finished request, its end-to-end time minus its time to first token, divided by the number of output tokens minus one. In F5's words, listen for long waits to start, which are prefill and time to first token, often from re-reading retrieved context, or for slow streaming, which is decode and inter-token latency, stuck behind heavy jobs.

Chapter 5 of 15

A slow first word, taken apart

Queue time points at capacity, prefill time points at the prompt

Once the team says the first word is slow, the next step is to split that wait. Every histogram has a _sum and a _count series, so a mean over a window is the increase in the sum divided by the increase in the count. Do that for three histograms on the same worker over the same window and the parts of the wait add up.

Take one hour on gpu-1 with 600 finished requests. The time to first token summed to 2,460 seconds, a mean of 4.1 seconds. The queue time summed to 1,920 seconds, 3.2 seconds a request. The prefill time summed to 510 seconds, 0.85 seconds a request. 3.2 + 0.85 is 4.05, and the remaining 0.05 seconds is everything else between arrival and the first word. Here the GPU read the prompts quickly and the requests waited in line. The cause is capacity at that moment, or placement that piled work on this worker while another sat idle. Look at vllm:num_requests_waiting on all workers together. A queue on one and nothing on the others is a routing fault, which is question three.

Now take a different hour. Queue time is 0.1 seconds and prefill time is 4.0 seconds. The line is short and the reading is long. Divide the sum of vllm:request_prompt_tokens by its count: about 19,000 tokens a request. At 0.21 milliseconds a token that is 3.99 seconds, exactly the prefill time. Long prompts that are read in full every time are retrieved passages or long agent histories that miss the cache. That points back to question one, and to the hit rate. A long prompt that is genuinely new every time cannot be cached, and the fix there is faster prefill or dedicated prefill workers.

Chapter 6 of 15

When the stream stutters

Someone else's prefill in your passes, or a request pushed out of memory

The other answer to question two is that the first word comes quickly and then the text stops and starts. That is a decode problem, and its usual cause is a neighbour. A worker runs prefill and decode in the same passes on the same cores. In vLLM, chunked prefill is on by default: every pass has a token budget, max_num_batched_tokens, decodes get their one token first, and the rest of the budget goes to waiting prompts. The lesson Prefill and decode shows the cost. A 30,000-token document arriving on a worker that streams to three users is read in four passes of about 1.7 seconds each, so those users get a token every 1.7 seconds instead of every 12 milliseconds.

The second cause is memory. When the KV cache pool is full and a running request needs one more block, vLLM preempts the request it admitted last. Its blocks are freed, it goes back to the queue and later its prompt is computed again. The user sees the stream stop for as long as that takes. vllm:num_preemptions counts these events, and a rising rate is never normal.

The evidence is in the upper buckets of vllm:inter_token_latency_seconds. A worker that streams at 12 milliseconds a gap has almost all its samples in the low buckets. Stutter shows as a second hump at a second or more. F5's proof point for this question is the distinction itself: separating the reading of the prompt from the streaming of tokens is what lets an engineer place the fault. The fix follows from it. A router that knows a 20,000-token prompt from a 100-token lookup can avoid dropping heavy prefill on a worker that is streaming, and BNK's scheduler can place prefill and decode on separate worker pools, which F5's BNK and NVIDIA deck describes as decode freed from prefill interference.

Chapter 7 of 15

Question three: what the router counts in a burst

Listen for connection counts or static weights

F5's third question: how does your router decide which GPU is busy, does it treat every request equally, or does it account for prompt size and live GPU load? The answers to listen for are routing on connection counts, or static weights. F5 names Envoy AI Gateway as an example of the second. Both send a massive prompt to a GPU that is already overloaded, because neither looks at what the prompt costs.

Counting is the common default. Envoy Gateway uses Least Request when no policy is set. With equal weights, Envoy's least request balancer picks two hosts at random and sends the request to the one with fewer active requests, a method called power of two choices. A request is one request whether it carries 100 tokens or 20,000. Fixed weights are cruder still. Envoy AI Gateway's documentation shows a route rule with two backendRefs of weight 50 each, splitting traffic evenly whatever is happening on either side. The same gateway can also hand the choice to an endpoint picker through an InferencePool, so a team that runs it may or may not be weighing load. Ask which.

A burst shows the flaw. gpu-1 is running four short lookups that are already decoding. gpu-2 is running two agent requests with 20,000-token prompts that are still in prefill. By count gpu-2 is less busy, two against four, so a third 20,000-token prompt goes there. It must wait for about 40,000 tokens of prefill ahead of it and then read its own, so its first word comes after about 12.6 seconds. On gpu-1 the lookups are each about a token a pass, and the new prompt's prefill gets nearly the whole budget, so it would have started its first word after about 4.2 seconds.

Chapter 8 of 15

The same burst with the prompt in view

Count the tokens, estimate the wait, skip the full GPUs

The answer F5 gives to question three is a router that reads the prompt. BNK hands each request to the F5 endpoint picker over ext_proc, and the picker returns the address of one worker. The lesson Which GPU answers soonest takes its steps apart. For triage, three of its properties matter, and F5 names all three as the reasons its picker beat another router that also uses cache and load.

First, it has a tokenizer built in. It knows that a 20,000-token prompt is a different job from a 100-token lookup, so the queue in front of gpu-2 is 40,000 tokens long and not two requests. Second, it tracks each GPU's live rate of tokens per second and turns the queue into an estimated wait. Third, it sets saturated GPUs aside before it looks for a warm cache, with nothing to tune. In the burst from the last chapter, the picker sees 40,000 tokens queued on gpu-2 and none on gpu-1, estimates the waits and sends the new prompt to gpu-1, unless gpu-2 is still under the overload gate, holds most of that prompt in its cache and its queue will drain soon enough to be worth it.

F5's proof point is its heavy-load test against Envoy AI Gateway on the same eight GPUs: eight Qwen3-32B FP8 workers on vLLM, 200 concurrent requests with 20,000-token prompts and 128 tokens out, a working set that exceeds the fleet's KV cache by 24%, a window of 240 seconds. The F5 path completed 5,623 requests, the other 1,738, 3.2 times as many. Once warm, the first token came after 248 milliseconds against 3.56 seconds, 14 times sooner. The cheat sheet gives the first-token gain as a range of 7.8 to 14 times. The slide shows the 14 times. The source of the 7.8 is not given in the deck.

Chapter 9 of 15

Question four: what a token costs

The same GPUs, more finished work, a lower price per answer

F5's fourth question: are your GPUs used efficiently under load, and how are you reducing wasted capacity to improve your cost per token? A GPU is paid for by the hour whether it computes or waits. The cost of a token is the price of the GPU-hour divided by the tokens that hour produced. Anything that makes the same GPU finish more requests in the hour lowers the cost of each.

F5's benchmark gives the arithmetic real inputs. Eight GPUs for 240 seconds are 1,920 GPU-seconds, 0.533 GPU-hours. The F5 path completed 5,623 requests in that time, about 10,543 requests a GPU-hour. The other router completed 1,738, about 3,259 a GPU-hour. At an illustrative $3.00 a GPU-hour, a thousand of these requests cost about $0.28 on the first path and about $0.92 on the second. The ratio is the same 3.2 as the request counts, because the hardware was the same. Each request wrote 128 tokens, so output per GPU-hour was about 1.35 million tokens against about 0.42 million.

The same frame shows where waste hides. A prompt recomputed after a cache miss is GPU time bought and spent on work already done, the 4 seconds per miss of the third chapter. A request preempted and prefilled again pays twice. A GPU whose queue is empty while its neighbour's is full is a GPU-hour with too few tokens in it. F5's proof point for this question is that combining cache and live load in one routing decision removes redundant prefill and hot spots, and that this drove 3.2 times more output per GPU. The measurement to ask for is simple: completed requests and generated tokens per GPU-hour, before and after. vllm:generation_tokens and vllm:request_success, as rates, give both.

Chapter 10 of 15

Headroom bought for the burst

Over-provisioning, idle cycles and recomputed context

The second half of question four is what to listen for: over-provisioning expensive GPU nodes for burst headroom while the existing GPUs waste cycles recomputing context or sit idle. Both halves of that sentence are common, and they feed each other.

A team that cannot spread a burst well sizes the fleet for the burst. Suppose lunchtime traffic is three times the daily average, an illustrative ratio. If the fleet is sized so that the peak meets the latency target, then on average two thirds of its capacity waits. That is a business decision, and sometimes the right one. The trouble starts when the peak needs more GPUs than it should because the router is wasting what is there. If one in two turns misses its cache, each miss spends about 4 seconds of prefill on work already done. A hundred such misses a minute is 400 GPU-seconds a minute, close to seven GPUs doing nothing useful. Those seven are then bought again as headroom.

The counters separate the two cases. If every worker's vllm:num_requests_waiting is zero at peak and the time to first token is within target, the fleet is simply large, and the question is about scaling down outside the peak. If queues form on some workers while others are idle, or the hit rate is low for a workload that should share prefixes, the fleet is being wasted. GPU utilization from the NVIDIA DCGM exporter is a weaker signal here, because it reports the share of time a kernel was running, and a GPU recomputing a prompt it already had is busy by that measure. The lesson Keeping GPUs busy and healthy explains what that gauge does and does not show. F5's range of 38 to 56% of capacity reclaimed is the size of the prize when the waste is in placement.

Chapter 11 of 15

Question five: more teams, more clusters

Siloed pools, noisy neighbours, routing rules that drift

F5's fifth question: as you onboard more teams and tenants, how do you manage traffic across clusters and keep routing consistent across environments? The answers to listen for are siloed GPU pools per team, noisy-neighbour bursts that starve tenants who share, and routing policies that differ from cluster to cluster.

Silos are the first sign. Two teams each get four GPUs and their own route. At lunchtime team A's workers show KV use of 0.97 and 25 requests waiting, while team B's show 0.15 and none. The GPUs that could help exist, and the routing has no path to them. Teams then ask for more GPUs of their own, which is question four again.

Sharing has the opposite risk. When tenants share one execution path, one tenant's burst fills the queues and caches that the others need. F5's deck with NVIDIA shows it with tenant A's time to first token: 800 milliseconds, an SLA violation, while tenant B is noisy on a shared path, and 45 milliseconds when tenant traffic is isolated by policy. 800 ÷ 45 is about 18 times. The lesson Two tenants, one factory builds that isolation, from a VRF per tenant at the border to separate pools and queues, and budgets and quotas in front of them.

Fragmented routing is the third sign. When each cluster has its own proxy, its own rules and its own way of reading load, the same request is treated differently depending on where it lands, and nobody can say why one site is slow. The fix F5 points to is one routing hierarchy that makes the same decision at each level, from region to replica, which the next chapter takes apart.

Chapter 12 of 15

Two tiers, from region to replica

Which site first, then which GPU, each with its own inputs

F5's proof point for question five is two-tier routing from region to replica, which unifies multi-cluster traffic, isolates bursts and supports more tenants per GPU fleet. The two tiers answer the same question, where this request goes, at two scopes and with two different sets of inputs.

Tier one is global site selection by F5 CNF DNS with GSLB. It decides which region, cluster or cloud serves a request. Its inputs are policy, meaning data sovereignty, cost and tenancy, together with AI telemetry from each site, meaning GPU load, queue depth and time to first token, and the round-trip time and health of each site. Telemetry flows up from the sites, and ratios or weights flow down. The lesson One name, three factories follows a request through this tier, from the resolver to the answer that names Frankfurt.

Tier two is local inference routing by BNK, the AI load balancer inside a site. It decides which GPU, model or Pod handles the request. Its inputs are per-Pod: GPU utilization, queue depth, KV-cache locality, model affinity and priority class. That is the endpoint picker of question three.

F5's Distributed Inference session draws the same hierarchy in three levels. At the region or AI factory, F5 CNF GSLB answers which site, and whether the data has to stay in the country. At the tenant cluster, BNK at the region's border answers which cluster and model pool serve this business unit. The two decks place the choice of cluster differently. The two-tier slide gives it to tier one, and the three-level slide gives it to BNK at the region's border, a level between the site and the replica. At the replica, BNK with the F5 endpoint picker answers which replica answers this request soonest. The point for triage is that each level uses the same kind of signal, queue depth and time to first token, and that a fault at one level is invisible to the others. A site that is up but full is a tier-one question. One hot Pod in a healthy site is a tier-two question.

Chapter 13 of 15

Question six: who runs the network on day two

Listen for home-made proxies, unwatched egress, and networking nobody owns

F5's sixth question: how do you expose AI services reliably, secure ingress and egress, and make Kubernetes production-ready without adding operational complexity? The answers to listen for are stitched-together DIY proxies, unmonitored egress to external tools and models, and complex day-two Kubernetes networking.

A stitched path is easy to spot when you draw it. One proxy terminates TLS for the API, a second component balances across the model Pods, a script or a sidecar does the routing that needs the prompt, and outbound calls leave through whatever the node does by default. Each piece has its own configuration, its own logs and its own upgrade cycle. A fault between two of them shows in neither.

Egress is where the risk hides. Agents call tool servers and hosted model APIs. Model servers fetch weights from object storage. By default, with Calico's natOutgoing on the Pod pool, a packet from 10.244.1.21 leaves as the node, 10.0.1.11, so a firewall at the destination cannot tell which application or tenant called, and the source changes when the Pod moves to 10.0.1.12. The lesson What leaves the factory follows that problem to its fix. Day-two networking adds the details that break quietly, such as a VXLAN tunnel whose MTU is left too high, for example at 1,460, so that its packets come to 1,510 bytes with the 50 bytes of wrapping and do not fit a 1,500-byte network.

F5's proof point is that BIG-IP Next for Kubernetes unifies secure ingress, egress and Gateway API routing in one production-ready platform. Ingress is a Gateway and an HTTPRoute whose backend is an InferencePool with its picker. Egress is a declared object with a pool of source addresses per tenant. Both run in the same data plane, TMM, with one set of status conditions to check.

Chapter 14 of 15

When the answer is not routing

Some bottlenecks are in the hardware or the sizing

Good triage also finds the faults that no router can fix, and says so. Three are common enough to check before any routing claim is made.

The first is a model build that leaves no room. Qwen3-32B from its BF16 build is 65.5 GB of weights. On an 80 GB H100 that leaves a KV cache pool of about 4.5 GB, room for about 17,200 tokens at 262,144 bytes each, as the lesson Prefill and decode works out. A single agent prompt of 20,000 tokens needs 20,000 × 262,144 bytes, about 5.24 GB, which is more than the whole pool. The FP8 build, 34.3 GB of files, leaves room for about 136,000 tokens on the same GPU. A team that serves the BF16 build on one GPU and complains of queues has a sizing problem first.

The second is a GPU that runs slow without error. A card that is too hot or at its power limit lowers its clocks, and every pass takes longer. The time per output token rises on one worker only, with no change in its queue or its cache. The lesson Keeping GPUs busy and healthy shows the DCGM fields that reveal it, such as the clock and the throttle reasons. The third is a GPU that has failed. An Xid 79 means the GPU fell off the bus, and the device plugin marks it unhealthy. A pool that lost one of three workers has a third less capacity, and a router can only spread the rest.

The discipline is to look at one worker against its peers. A fault that shows on every worker under load is capacity or routing. A fault that shows on one worker, with normal traffic, is that worker.

Chapter 15 of 15

One call, six answers

A whole discovery, from sentences to findings

Put together, the six questions make a short conversation with a clear output. Take an illustrative platform team that runs Qwen3-32B from its FP8 build on eight GPUs for a set of internal agents.

To question one they answer that a Kubernetes Service balances across the Pods. That means a random pick per connection and no knowledge of the cache. To question two they answer that the first word is slow at lunchtime and the stream is fine. That puts the fault before decode, in the queue or in prefill. Their metrics split it: 3.2 seconds of queue in a 4.1-second first word on the two busiest workers, almost nothing queued on the others, and a hit rate of 30% for agents that share a long system prompt and long histories. To question three they answer that a gateway counts active requests. That explains the uneven queues during bursts of long prompts. To question four they answer that they asked for four more GPUs for the lunchtime peak. To question five they answer that a second team has its own four GPUs on another cluster with another proxy. To question six they answer that agents call two hosted model APIs and that nobody has a list of what leaves the cluster.

The findings follow from the answers, one per question. Placement is blind to the cache and to prompt size, which the endpoint picker behind BNK addresses, with F5's 38 to 56% capacity figure as the size of the opportunity. The slow first word is queueing caused by placement while GPUs sit idle, so the four extra GPUs may not be needed. The second team's silo is a tier-two and possibly tier-one routing question. Egress has no owner, which a declared egress per tenant in BNK addresses. Each finding is tied to a number the team can measure again after a change.

What to keep

Ask, then measure, then name the fix

Every complaint about a slow model service is a symptom, and the same symptom can come from different causes. The six questions take a team from what it feels to what its workers measure. Question one asks whether placement can see the cache. Question two splits the wait into the first word and the stream, and the histograms split the first word into queue and prefill. Question three asks whether the router counts requests or tokens. Question four turns the answers into requests and tokens per GPU-hour. Question five asks whether teams and clusters share safely or sit in silos. Question six asks who owns the way in and the way out. Each answer points to a counter, each counter to a cause, and only then to a capability. Some causes are hardware or sizing, and good triage says so.
