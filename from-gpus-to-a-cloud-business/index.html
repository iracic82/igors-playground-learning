Running a GPU cloud

From GPUs to a cloud business

Renting or owning, and what makes a GPU cloud profitable

Two people look at the same rack of GPUs with different questions. A buyer asks whether to rent GPU time from a cloud or to buy servers of their own. An owner who already has the servers asks how to turn them into a cloud that strangers can order from, pay for and leave. This lesson answers both, on Northlight Compute's three servers of four H100 GPUs each.

The buyer is tenant A. It runs its model server, the Deployment qwen3 with two Pods of one GPU each, serving Qwen3-32B from the 34.3 GB FP8 build, around the clock. Once a quarter it also fine-tunes a model for a week. The first half of the lesson works out, in hypothetical units, what renting that costs, what owning it would cost, where the break-even lies, why GPUs come in whole servers, and why the bill is always bigger than the GPU-hours. It then shows the difference between renting a bare GPU and renting a managed environment, and gives the checklist a buyer should use before signing.

The second half turns to the owner. A GPU server reachable from the internet is a hosted server. A cloud needs a catalog, ordering, identity, tenancy, provisioning, scheduling, metering, billing, service levels and a way to wipe and reuse what a customer leaves behind. The lesson lays out that whole operating model and shows which lesson on this site covers each part. It follows the three stages from infrastructure to product to on-demand service, the four kinds of owner who make that journey, the levers that decide whether the business makes money, and twelve checks an operator should pass before the first customer orders.

Chapter 1 of 16

Two ways to get a GPU

Buying a server and renting one differ on five axes

A GPU cloud gives a user GPU computing over a network, on request, without the user buying, installing or maintaining the servers. The same idea goes by several names. GPU as a service, often shortened to GPUaaS, names the way GPU capacity is delivered and consumed. GPU cloud usually names the wider environment that delivers it. A cloud GPU is one GPU, or one GPU instance, rented from a cloud. The words overlap and no standards body fixes the line between them, so a buyer should look at what is delivered rather than at the label.

The alternative is on-premises infrastructure: servers the buyer owns, in a data center the buyer runs or rents space in. The two models differ on five axes. The first is upfront deployment. A cloud user can start without buying anything. An owner first has to procure servers, prepare the data center, provide power and cooling, cable the network and install everything. The second is scalability. A cloud user can grow and shrink within what the provider has available. An owner who needs more than is installed has to buy and deploy more hardware.

The third axis is the cost model. Clouds charge on demand, for a reservation, by subscription or under a contract. An owner pays capital expense for the hardware and then operating expense for power, space, network and staff. The fourth is control. Ownership gives direct control over hardware, network, security and where the data sits. A cloud user depends on the provider's infrastructure and policies. The fifth is operational responsibility. In a cloud the provider handles much of the physical lifecycle. An owner is responsible for drivers, failures, capacity planning, platform operations and the end of each server's life.

Neither model wins on every axis, so the answer depends on the workload. Tenant A has two very different workloads, and the next chapters show that they lead to different answers.

Chapter 2 of 16

When renting fits, and what it gives

Swinging demand, short bursts and slow procurement

Renting has five benefits that follow from the axes. There is no upfront purchase. Capacity, where the provider has it, arrives much faster than a purchased server can be delivered and installed. Use can follow demand, small while experimenting and larger for training or production. The buyer can choose between GPU models, memory sizes, server shapes, storage and networking without owning each one. And the buyer spends less effort on physical infrastructure and more on models, data and applications.

Three situations make renting the natural choice. The first is demand that swings: a team that needs 2 GPUs one month and 30 the next cannot buy for the peak without paying for idle hardware the rest of the time. The second is a short burst of large compute, such as a fine-tuning run that needs a whole server for a week. The third is a procurement lead time that is too slow for the project. A model that must be in production this quarter cannot wait for servers that arrive next quarter.

NIST, the US standards institute, wrote the definition of cloud computing most providers still quote, Special Publication 800-145. One of its five essential characteristics is rapid elasticity: capabilities can be provisioned and released to scale outward and inward with demand, and to the consumer they often appear to be unlimited. The word appear matters. A GPU cloud has a fixed number of GPUs. Northlight has 12. A request for 8 H100s on one fabric can be refused when the pool is full, which is why the lesson Why a job waits while GPUs are free spends a chapter on reserved, on-demand and spot capacity.

Renting is also not automatically the cheapest option. NIST notes that measured service is typically charged per use. A workload that runs every hour of every day pays for every hour, and the next chapter shows where that tips the balance toward owning.

Chapter 3 of 16

Where owning wins: the break-even in hours

A GPU that is busy enough hours a month is cheaper to own

The comparison fits in one line of arithmetic per GPU. The numbers here are hypothetical and in made-up units, chosen to match the month in the lesson Metering is not billing. Northlight's list price is 5,000 units per GPU-hour. Tenant A's reservation discount brings its effective price to 4,000.

Owning has a fixed cost and a variable cost. The fixed cost is paid every month whether the GPUs work or not: the server's purchase spread over its life, the financing, the rack space, the power commitment, the network ports and a share of the staff who run it. Here it is 4,800,000 units a month for one server of four GPUs, which is 1,200,000 per GPU. The variable cost follows use, mostly the electricity of a busy GPU. Here it is 300 units per busy GPU-hour. An H100 SXM can draw up to 700 W, so a busy four-GPU server draws 2,800 W for its GPUs alone before the CPUs, fans and cooling.

Each hour a rented GPU works costs the price. Each hour an owned GPU works costs only the variable 300, but the 1,200,000 comes first. So owning saves 4,000 minus 300, which is 3,700 units for every hour of real use, and needs 1,200,000 / 3,700 = 324.3 hours a month to recover the fixed cost. A month has 720 hours, so the break-even is 45.0% of the month. Against the on-demand list price of 5,000, each hour saves 4,700 and the break-even falls to 1,200,000 / 4,700 = 255.3 hours, or 35.5%.

A GPU busy more than about 45% of the month is cheaper to own than to rent at 4,000. Below that, renting wins. The same arithmetic runs on the provider's side. The lesson Metering is not billing works it through for a hypothetical fleet of 32 GPUs, a separate example larger than the 12 GPUs on this lesson's board. That fleet costs 30,000,000 a month, or 937,500 per GPU, and breaks even at 937,500 / 3,700 = 253.4 hours, 35.2% of the month. That is the break-even the metering lesson reaches from the provider's side. The provider breaks even sooner than tenant A would because a large fleet buys, finances and staffs each GPU more cheaply. The provider's margin lives in that gap.

Chapter 4 of 16

GPUs come in whole servers

The break-even per GPU meets the shape of real hardware

The per-GPU break-even assumes a buyer can own exactly the GPUs it needs. A buyer cannot. GPUs come in servers of four or eight, and the fixed cost belongs to the whole server. Tenant A needs two GPUs. The cheapest owned answer is one four-GPU server: 4,800,000 fixed plus 1,440 busy hours × 300 = 432,000, a total of 5,232,000 a month. That is 528,000, or 9.2%, less than the 5,760,000 it pays to rent. Two of the four GPUs sit idle, and their share of the fixed cost is paid anyway.

The saving has a hidden price. With one server, both Pods of qwen3 share one machine, one power supply path and one network card. When that server fails or needs a firmware update, both replicas stop. Each replica is a full copy of the model in its own GPU's memory and takes about three minutes to load the 34.3 GB of FP8 weights, so a restart is not instant. On Northlight the two rented GPUs sit on gpu-srv-1 and gpu-srv-2, so one server can fail and the other Pod keeps answering. To match that, tenant A would have to own two servers: 9,600,000 fixed plus 432,000, which is 10,032,000 a month, 74.2% more than renting.

The picture changes when the servers fill up. Suppose tenant A's traffic grows until it runs eight replicas around the clock. Eight GPUs fill two servers exactly. Owning costs 9,600,000 plus 8 × 720 × 300 = 1,728,000, a total of 11,328,000. Renting costs 8 × 720 × 4,000 = 23,040,000. Owning now saves 11,712,000 a month, 50.8% of the rental bill, and keeps two failure domains.

The fine-tuning burst points the other way. Owning a server for it costs 4,800,000 a month, 14,400,000 a quarter plus 480 busy hours × 300 = 144,000. Renting the week costs 672 GPU-hours × 5,000 = 3,360,000 a quarter. The owned server would work 480 of its 8,640 GPU-hours, 5.6%. The common result for a workload like tenant A's is to own the steady base when it fills whole servers and rent the bursts.

Chapter 5 of 16

What owning adds to the job

The fixed cost buys hardware and a list of new responsibilities

The break-even treats owning as a number. In practice it is also a job. The operational-responsibility axis from the first chapter turns into specific work, and each item either adds cost or adds risk.

Before the first token is served, an owner procures servers, waits for delivery, prepares racks with enough power and cooling, cables the network and installs the operating system. Some choices are made at that moment and cannot be changed cheaply later. The owner's BNK notes give an example: a BlueField DPU, a network card with its own processor that can enforce policy the host cannot touch, sits in the bill of materials. It cannot be added to a deployed fleet without touching every node, which makes it a procurement decision rather than a tuning decision. A buyer who rents inherits the provider's choices and does not make these.

Once the servers run, the owner keeps the GPU driver, the CUDA runtime and the firmware current and compatible with the frameworks. The owner watches for GPU faults, the Xid errors the NVIDIA driver writes to the kernel log, and replaces failed parts. The owner plans capacity: how many GPUs to hold in reserve for failures and maintenance, and when to buy the next server, knowing that the order takes time to arrive. And the owner retires hardware at the end of its life and wipes its disks.

A renter still has work. On a raw bare-metal rental, the driver and the stack above it are the renter's too. What the renter is spared is the physical lifecycle: the room, the power, the spares, the delivery wait and the disposal. That difference is part of what the provider's price pays for, and part of why the provider's break-even is lower. A provider spreads one team of engineers and one set of spares across many tenants.

Chapter 6 of 16

The bill is more than the GPU-hour

Storage, transfer, idle commitments and support, in one worked month

A quick estimate multiplies the hours a workload needs by the hourly price. The real bill always comes out higher, because the GPU-hour is only one of the things a GPU cloud charges for. Storage, networking, data transfer, idle resources, commitments and support all add to the total.

Here is tenant A's month with the fine-tuning week in it, all in hypothetical units. Inference uses 2 reserved GPUs for 720 hours at 4,000: 5,760,000. Fine-tuning uses a 7-day block of 4 GPUs at the list price of 5,000, which is 672 GPU-hours and 3,360,000. The run finishes after 120 hours, so it used 480 GPU-hours and the block's other 192 were paid and idle: 960,000 units bought nothing. Storage holds the FP8 build of Qwen3-32B (34.3 GB), the BF16 build it fine-tunes from (65.5 GB), a 200 GB dataset and four merged BF16 snapshots of 65.5 GB, which is 561.8 GB. The run is a LoRA fine-tune, so each day it merges its small adapter into the base weights and saves a full 65.5 GB BF16 model that can be tested. The run spans five days and tenant A keeps the last four. At 100 units per GB-month that is 56,180. Copying the finished 65.5 GB model out to tenant A's own site costs 100 units per GB, 6,550. A support plan costs 300,000.

The total is 9,482,730. The quick estimate, 1,440 × 4,000 plus 480 × 5,000, gave 8,160,000. The real bill is 1,322,730 higher, 16.2% above the estimate, and 960,000 of that difference, about 73%, is the idle part of the commitment. Storage and transfer are real but small here. They grow large for buyers who move big datasets out of a cloud, which the lesson What leaves the factory follows from the network side.

Public clouds document each of these lines. On AWS, a Capacity Reservation is charged at the On-Demand rate whether instances run in it or not. EBS volumes and Elastic IP addresses are charged whatever state the instance is in. Every start of a stopped instance bills at least one minute. A Capacity Block for ML is priced by supply and demand and charged up front. AWS lists data transfer in and out separately, and gives 100 GB a month of transfer out to the internet free across all services and Regions.

Chapter 7 of 16

Raw GPU or a managed environment

The same H100 can be sold as a bare server or as an endpoint

Two offers can both say H100 and deliver very different things. Some GPU services deliver only a server or a virtual machine. Others add, layer by layer, the GPU driver and runtime, containers, Kubernetes, AI frameworks, model serving, monitoring, integration with storage, and operation of the whole cluster by the provider. A buyer has to know which one is on the order form, because every layer the provider does not run is a layer the buyer runs.

Tenant A's model server shows the difference. On a raw bare-metal server tenant A installs the NVIDIA driver and the container toolkit, builds or joins a Kubernetes cluster, installs the device plugin that advertises nvidia.com/gpu, deploys qwen3 with the image vllm/vllm-openai, and waits for 34.3 GB of FP8 weights to download and load. On a managed Kubernetes service the GPU nodes arrive ready, and tenant A starts at the Deployment. On a managed model-serving product tenant A deploys nothing and sends requests to an OpenAI-compatible endpoint such as https://api.northlight.example/v1/chat/completions, paying per token.

NIST's three service models give names to the first and last of these. In infrastructure as a service the consumer provisions processing, storage and networks and controls the operating system. In platform as a service the consumer deploys applications and does not control the operating system. A token endpoint is closer to software as a service, where the consumer only uses the application. The managed layers between them are where providers differ most, and names like GPU cloud say nothing about them.

The raw end has buyers who need it. The owner's GPU-from-scratch lab kit includes experiments that load a patched kernel module and trace every doorbell the driver rings, on a rented H100 PCIe. They need root on the host and an out-of-tree module, so only a bare-metal server, or a VM with a whole GPU passed through and a kernel the renter controls, will do. The lesson A tenant is a boundary shows what bare metal, passthrough and vGPU each hand over.

Chapter 8 of 16

What to check before signing

Two services with the same GPU can perform and cost differently

A GPU model on a product page is the start of an evaluation. Two services with the same GPU can run the same workload at very different speeds, because distributed work also depends on the links between GPUs, the network between servers, storage throughput, the CPUs and memory, the cluster topology and the software stack. A buyer should check seven groups of facts.

The GPU: the model, how many per server, the memory and the interconnect between them. An H100 SXM has 80 GB and links to its neighbours over NVLink at 900 GB/s. An H100 NVL has 94 GB and NVLink at 600 GB/s. Over PCIe Gen5 the figure is 128 GB/s. The server: the CPU, system memory, local storage and the PCIe topology, which decides whether a GPU and a network card share a switch. The network: bandwidth per server, Ethernet or InfiniBand, whether RDMA works between servers, and the topology across many nodes. Storage: local or shared, its throughput, and how datasets reach it.

Service operations: how long provisioning takes, whether there is an API as well as a console, how usage is metered and billed, how reservations work, and how much of the lifecycle is automated. Software: the driver, CUDA and runtime versions, container support, Kubernetes, and which cluster scheduler is offered. Then the terms around them: regions and where the data lives, security and compliance, support, and the service level agreement, the contract's promise of availability and response.

For tenant A the checks sort themselves by workload. Inference needs one GPU with enough memory for 34.3 GB of weights plus a KV cache, the memory that holds each conversation's work so far, and a network path to users. Fine-tuning on four GPUs depends on NVLink inside the server. A larger run across servers would depend on RDMA and the fabric, the subject of The network is part of the computer. The question that summarises the list is: what physical resource is being delivered, and through what service operating model?

Chapter 9 of 16

A server is not yet a cloud

Hosting provides infrastructure. A cloud repeats a lifecycle

Now turn the view around. Northlight's owners have servers in a data center, on fast networking and storage, reachable remotely. That alone is GPU hosting. Hosting is a valid business: a dedicated server, a long contract, few customers and few changes can be exactly what some buyers want. It is a different operating model from a cloud.

NIST's five essential characteristics describe the difference. On-demand self-service: a consumer can provision server time and storage as needed, automatically, without human interaction with each service provider. Broad network access: capabilities are reached over the network through standard mechanisms. Resource pooling: the provider's resources serve many consumers through a multi-tenant model and are assigned and reassigned according to demand. Rapid elasticity: capabilities are provisioned and released to follow demand. Measured service: resource use is metered, controlled and reported, with transparency for provider and consumer.

In a hosting operation, an engineer reads a ticket, picks a server, creates accounts, sets up the network, records usage in a spreadsheet and resets the server by hand after each customer. Every step can be done well. None of it repeats without a person. A cloud turns those steps into a lifecycle that runs many times a day: catalog, order, provision, isolate, schedule, meter, bill, keep the service level, recover, recycle.

This is the gap between hardware-ready and service-ready. A healthy GPU that passes its burn-in is hardware-ready. A service also needs its definition, ordering, capacity discovery and reservation, provisioning, network and storage configuration, tenant and access policy, scheduling, metering, billing, monitoring, service-level management, deprovisioning, and recovery. Bare metal fits this as well as virtual machines do. Bare metal and virtualization describe how a resource is delivered. Cloud describes how it is requested, provisioned, measured and released.

Chapter 10 of 16

The whole operating model on one page

Fourteen capabilities, and the lessons that open each one

No single part makes a GPU cloud. GPU partitioning alone does not make a multi-tenant service. Assigning a GPU alone does not make provisioning. A GPU-hour counter alone does not make billing. Each piece works only when the others around it do. Here is the whole set, as fourteen capabilities.

A catalog of what can be ordered. Ordering and fulfilment, which turn a request into work. Users and organizations, with roles and credentials. Tenancy, the boundary between customers across compute, network, storage, credentials, quotas and records. Provisioning, which turns a bare server into one a customer can log in to. Scheduling, which decides which GPUs a request gets. Network and storage set up per tenant. Quota and policy, which limit what each tenant may ask for. Metering, the record of who held what and for how long. Billing, which applies prices to that record. The service level agreement and the operations that keep it. Observability, the health of GPUs, servers, network and storage. APIs and a console, so people and programs can drive all of it. And lifecycle management, which ends in cleaning and reuse.

Most of these already have a lesson on this site. A tenant is a boundary covers tenancy, MIG, vGPU and what a namespace cannot isolate. From an order to a server and back covers the order's states, provisioning and the wipe. A server nobody has to trust covers ingestion and proving a server clean. Why a job waits while GPUs are free covers quota, reservations and scheduling. Metering is not billing covers telemetry, allocation, billable usage and rating. Who controls the cloud and A cloud with no way out cover sovereignty and air gaps. This lesson is the map that places them side by side.

On the board the map is physical. Identity, orders, scheduler, provisioner, metering and billing sit in the control plane. The portal and API face the customer. The fabric and storage are separated per tenant. Every customer action passes through several of these before it reaches a GPU.

Chapter 11 of 16

Infrastructure, product, service

Three stages, and a product can exist without the service behind it

Turning GPUs into a cloud business happens in three stages. Each answers a different question, and each can be finished while the next has not started.

The first stage is infrastructure: owning capacity. GPUs, servers, networking, storage and the data center are installed and healthy. The questions are technical. Which GPU models, how many, what network and storage, is it healthy. Northlight at this stage has 12 working H100s and nothing to sell.

The second stage is product: defining what a customer can buy. The operator decides GPU model and count, CPU and memory, storage and networking, bare metal or VM or cluster, contract duration, price, reservation policy, quota, support and service level. The question changes from what do we own to what can the customer buy. Northlight might define three products: a single H100 by the month, a whole four-GPU server by the week, and a four-GPU server on demand by the hour.

The third stage is on-demand cloud service: delivering the product again and again. When an order arrives, the platform checks capacity, reserves resources, allocates a server, provisions it, configures network and storage, applies access policy, meters use, and at the end terminates and recovers the resources. That repeatable delivery is what turns a product into a running service.

The second and third stages separate easily. A product can be on a price list, with a sales team selling it, while every order is fulfilled by engineers working tickets. That is a product without an operating service, and it stops scaling as orders grow. Commercialization is therefore broader than renting GPUs. It covers the product definition and the whole repeating operation behind it. The customer sees select, order, use, monitor, terminate. Behind each of those the operator runs catalog, ordering, provisioning and scheduling, metering, billing and SLA, recovery and reuse.

Chapter 12 of 16

A catalog item and the order behind it

Inventory says what exists. The catalog says what can be ordered

Northlight's operations team knows each server's GPU type, memory, network connection, rack position and status. That is inventory. A customer does not want to order asset gpu-srv-3. It wants a dedicated four-GPU server for a week. The service catalog is the layer that turns inventory into products a customer can choose.

Three records are easy to confuse. Inventory describes what physically or logically exists. A price list describes rates. The catalog defines what a customer can order and on which conditions, and refers to both. A catalog item can carry a product name or SKU, the GPU type and count, bare metal or VM, the compute shape, network and storage options, reservation or duration rules, the region or site, eligibility and quota, the price or contract mapping, a provisioning template, a termination and recovery policy, and the support or SLA scope.

The item has to stay tied to three things. To real capacity: a product can be listed while no capacity exists for it, so the operator needs live availability, reservations, waiting or an alternative offer. To provisioning: the item has to say how the service is built, from server selection and image to network, storage, credentials and what counts as done. To billing: the item defines what was sold. If the product says seven-day dedicated reservation while metering counts GPU utilization and billing charges VM runtime, three systems hold three definitions of the same sale.

One name clash catches people. OpenStack's identity service, Keystone, also has a service catalog, a list of OpenStack services and their API endpoints for clients to discover. It solves a different problem. A good test question for the whole chain comes from a first external customer: we need eight GPU servers for one week, when can we have them, what will it cost, and what happens when the week is over. Northlight has three servers, so the honest answer starts with capacity, and the operation has to be able to give it.

Chapter 13 of 16

Your own brand

Running a cloud means owning the decisions a reseller does not make

An infrastructure owner has two broad ways to sell GPU capacity to many customers. It can resell it through someone else's cloud, where another company's portal, catalog, prices and policies face the customer. Or it can run its own cloud. In the second case the owner decides six things. The brand the customer sees. The catalog, meaning which products exist. The pricing policy. The operating policy: quotas, eligibility, maintenance windows, what happens at the end of a term. The infrastructure configuration: which GPUs, which network, which storage, which sites. And the customer experience from the first order to the last invoice.

Those six decisions are the difference between running a cloud and reselling one. The software underneath can be built in house, assembled from open projects such as OpenStack, or bought. Some vendors sell the operating layer as a product that sits between the owner's infrastructure and the owner's branded service. Buying it does not hand the brand or the catalog to the vendor. The owner stays the operator and sets every one of the six.

The owner's BNK notes show the same split for an inference product. A provider who sells model endpoints to tenants defines the endpoint in its own console and API. An adapter turns that definition into Kubernetes objects: a Gateway, an HTTPRoute, an InferencePool with its endpoint picker, a policy rule and a firewall policy, all labelled for that one endpoint. The BNK controller programs the data plane from them. Deleting the endpoint deletes exactly its objects. The provider's console sits on the control path only. Requests from the tenant go from the gateway straight to the GPU the picker chose and never pass through the console.

That split is the practical meaning of own brand at the network layer. The provider's product decisions live in its own control plane. The parts that carry traffic are configured from it and carry no other company's policy.

Chapter 14 of 16

Four owners, four paths

Telecoms, data centers, AI factories and GPU providers start from different places

Four kinds of organization already own GPU infrastructure and face the same missing layer. Each starts from different assets.

A telecom operator may already run data centers, a wide network, regional and edge sites, enterprise customer relationships and, increasingly, GPUs. Its path runs from owned GPUs and data centers to a branded AI cloud. F5's AI Grid deck, which summarises NVIDIA's AI Grid, puts numbers on the starting point: telcos and CDNs operate about 100,000 distributed network data centers. The same F5 slide names, under the heading commercialization plumbing, what a telco still lacks to sell inference rather than only run it: hard multi-tenancy, role-based access, token metering and a marketplace. The opportunity lies in running central, regional and edge sites under one service and policy model, with workloads placed by latency, data location, capacity and customer policy. Telco clouds and sovereign AI overlap, and the lesson Who controls the cloud shows why local hosting alone does not make a service sovereign.

A data center operator often already does GPU hosting. Its path runs to an on-demand GPU cloud, usually without replacing servers. Take a site with twenty eight-GPU servers. In hosting, a customer negotiates with sales and an engineer delivers a server. In a cloud, the same servers are products, and an order flows through entitlement, capacity, reservation, provisioning, access, metering and termination. The transition goes in six stages: capacity and inventory, catalog and SKUs, customers and tenancy, repeatable provisioning, metering and billing, and lifecycle recovery. Hosting and cloud products can run side by side.

An AI factory operator runs a site built for its own training and inference. Its path runs to a multi-customer service, which adds customer accounts, ordering, tenant isolation, metering, billing and service levels. Not every factory should take that path. Internal research and restricted workloads are good reasons to stay internal.

A GPU infrastructure provider already sells capacity, often by contract. Its path runs to an API or self-service product, where programs and pipelines order and release GPUs without a salesperson in between.

Chapter 15 of 16

What makes it profitable

The levers move saleable hours, billable hours and price

The lesson Metering is not billing works through a provider's month in full for a hypothetical fleet of 32 GPUs, a separate example larger than the 12 GPUs on this lesson's board: 23,040 theoretical GPU-hours, 11,520 billable, an effective price of 4,000, a fixed cost of 30,000,000, 300 variable per billable hour, a result of +12,624,000, and break-even when about 35.2% of the theoretical hours are billable. That chain, theoretical capacity to saleable capacity to billable time to effective price to revenue to cost, is the economic model. This chapter is about the levers an operator can move inside it.

Product and demand fit comes first. Products that match what customers want to hold raise billable hours. A catalog full of eight-GPU servers sells poorly to customers who each want one GPU for inference, and the reverse. The mix of dedicated and flexible products is the second lever. Monthly reservations make revenue predictable and lower the effective price. Weekly blocks and on-demand hours sell at list price and leave gaps. Reservation policy is the third: how much of the fleet to commit, and how much to keep for on-demand customers who pay more.

Provisioning and recycle time is the fourth. A server in cleaning, failed or in an unknown state exists and cannot be sold. In the metering lesson's month, eight servers handed over 12 times each with one hour of cleaning took 384 GPU-hours from saleable capacity. At 4,000 that is up to 1,536,000 units a month of hours that could have been sold. Correct metering and billing is the fifth: an allocation that opens late or closes late is lost money or a refund. Full cost accounting is the sixth. Hardware is one cost among power, cooling, facility, network, storage, software, engineering, support, financing and the cost of winning customers.

An operations platform makes these levers measurable. It can standardize products, automate workflows, track service state, produce consistent usage records and manage the lifecycle. It cannot create demand, so it guarantees neither utilization, revenue nor margin. The question it helps answer every day is whether the operator knows what is saleable, what is allocated, what is billable, whether billing matches service state, and when capacity returns to the pool.

Chapter 16 of 16

Ready to sell: twelve checks

Infrastructure-ready, workload-ready and service-ready are three states

An operator can be ready in three different senses. Infrastructure-ready means the GPUs, servers, network and storage are deployed, healthy and monitored. Workload-ready means AI work runs: drivers, runtimes, containers, scheduling and perhaps a serving platform are in place, and a training job or a qwen3 Deployment succeeds. Service-ready means a customer can select, order, receive, use, measure and end a service through a repeatable operation. Each state can be reached without the next.

Twelve checks show where an operation stands, in four groups. Service productization: a specification a customer can understand, with GPU type, count, form, network, storage and duration. Price, contract and reservation rules that sales and operations apply the same way. An order that can be tracked through ordered, reserved, provisioning, ready, active, terminating, failed, cancelled and completed. Customers and resources: organizations, users, roles, credentials and quotas managed per customer. Tenant isolation that has been tested for identity, network, storage and access. Provisioning state that shows whether compute, GPU, network, storage, image and access are really ready.

Commercial and operations: a billable usage record tied to the contract, kept apart from raw telemetry. Rating, invoicing, cancellations and adjustments that agree with real service state. Incident, capacity and SLA operations that handle failures, maintenance and reservations. Lifecycle and growth: ordering, provisioning, retry, rollback, support and termination as repeatable procedures rather than one engineer's knowledge. Termination, reset and reallocation that clean access, data, network, storage and server state before capacity returns. And new GPU models, servers, sites and products added without rebuilding the operation.

The checks are a diagnostic. Adding them up into a score hides what matters. Passing ten of twelve can still hide a serious risk when a missing item is tenant isolation, customer data handling, billing correctness, access revocation or failure recovery. Those five come first.

What to keep

Own the steady hours, operate what you sell

For a buyer, renting and owning are two cost lines that cross. On this lesson's hypothetical numbers an owned GPU pays back its 1,200,000 monthly fixed cost after 324.3 busy hours, 45.0% of the month, and a provider buying and staffing at scale, in the metering lesson's hypothetical 32-GPU fleet, breaks even sooner at 35.2%. GPUs come in whole servers, so tenant A's two steady replicas only pay to own once there is enough steady work to fill servers and keep two failure domains. Its fine-tuning week, once a quarter, stays rented. The real bill, 9,482,730 against an estimate of 8,160,000, shows that idle commitments cost more than storage or transfer. For an owner, servers on a network are hosting. A cloud adds fourteen capabilities that repeat without a person, moves through infrastructure, product and on-demand service, keeps its own brand, catalog and prices, and makes money by turning theoretical hours into saleable and billable ones.
