Kubernetes underneath

A volume finds its Pod

How 34.3 GB of weights stay on a disk that outlives the model server

A container's own filesystem is deleted with the container. A model server that keeps its weights there downloads 34.3 GB on every start, the size of the FP8 build of Qwen3-32B. A PersistentVolume is storage that outlives the Pod, and getting one into a container takes a chain of small steps, each owned by a different component. A claim asks for storage. A storage driver creates a disk, and Kubernetes binds the two. The disk is attached to the node, mounted once for the node, mounted again for the Pod, and finally mounted into the container. In the site's other lessons the Deployment qwen3 runs two replicas, qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-x7k2p on gpu-node-1 and qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-m9q4t on gpu-node-2. Here it is scaled down to one replica while its weights move onto a disk of their own, because the kind of disk used here can be attached to only one node at a time. This lesson follows that one replica through every step, with the events and log lines each step leaves behind and what happens when a step fails. Then it looks at who may share the weights, what protects them when the claim is deleted, and what happens when the Pod moves to the other node, first by a drain and then after a crash. Adding the volume changes the Pod template, so the Deployment starts a new revision with a new pod-template-hash, 58b4d7f9c6. A Deployment names each Pod from its own name, the pod-template-hash of its template and five random characters, so the one Pod of the new revision is qwen3-58b4d7f9c6-w4j7n, on gpu-node-1. A replacement in the same revision keeps the hash and gets new random characters. Numbered names like db-0 are how a StatefulSet names its Pods.

Chapter 1 of 13

A claim asks, a class says how

The claim is stored and nothing is created yet

A Pod does not name a disk. It names a PersistentVolumeClaim, or PVC, which is a request: this much storage, this access mode, from this class. The class is a StorageClass object that the cluster's owners wrote. It names the provisioner, which is the storage driver that creates volumes, and it carries two settings that matter later. reclaimPolicy says what happens to the disk when the claim is deleted. volumeBindingMode says when the disk is created. The default is Immediate: create it as soon as the claim appears. This class says WaitForFirstConsumer, which delays creation until a Pod that uses the claim is being scheduled. So when the claim for the weights is applied, no disk is made. The claim stays Pending, and an event on it says why.

The class can be left out of a claim. Then the cluster's default class is used, the one marked with the annotation storageclass.kubernetes.io/is-default-class: "true", which kubectl get storageclass shows with (default) after its name. An empty string is different. storageClassName: "" asks for a volume with no class at all, and no default is filled in. A claim that names a label selector cannot have a volume created for it. The provisioner rejects it with claim Selector is not supported.

The size is a minimum. Binding gives at least what was asked and possibly more. For a Filesystem volume the request is the outer size of the disk. The filesystem built on it takes some of that, so the usable space for the weights is a little under 50Gi. 50Gi is 53,687,091,200 bytes, about 53.7 GB, and the 34.3 GB of the FP8 build leave about 53.7 − 34.3 = 19.4 GB spare. The full BF16 build of Qwen3-32B is about 65.5 GB and would not fit in 50Gi at all. volumeMode Block would hand the Pod a raw device with no filesystem at all.

Chapter 2 of 13

The scheduler picks the node before the disk exists

Then the driver creates the disk where that node can reach it

Many disks can only be reached from one zone or one node. A disk made first, in a zone chosen without knowing the Pod, can end up where no node has a free GPU. The Pod would then stay Pending because no node matches the volume. WaitForFirstConsumer turns the order around. The model server Pod is created with the claim in its volumes. The scheduler picks a node as usual, by GPUs, memory and taints, and gets gpu-node-1. It writes that choice onto the claim as an annotation. The driver's provisioner watches claims. It sees the annotation and calls CreateVolume on the driver, passing the node's zone as a requirement. The driver creates a 50Gi disk there. The provisioner then writes a PersistentVolume, or PV, the object that stands for the real disk.

This only works if the scheduler is the one choosing. A Pod that sets nodeName itself skips the scheduler, and its WaitForFirstConsumer claim then stays Pending, because nobody writes the selected node. The documentation says to use a node selector on kubernetes.io/hostname instead. A class can still list allowedTopologies to limit the zones it creates disks in, though with WaitForFirstConsumer that is rarely needed.

When no node fits for storage reasons, the scheduler says which reason in the Pod's FailedScheduling event: node(s) didn't find available persistent volumes to bind, node(s) didn't match PersistentVolume's node affinity, or node(s) did not have enough free storage. A claim in Immediate mode that is not yet bound stops the Pod with pod has unbound immediate PersistentVolumeClaims.

Chapter 3 of 13

Waiting on the driver, and when it fails

Events on the claim, retries with back-off, and a way back to the scheduler

While the disk is being made, two parties wait. The Pod is held by the scheduler in a step called PreBind, which checks every second whether the claim is bound and gives up after 600 seconds, the default bind timeout. The claim collects events. The persistent volume controller hands the claim to the driver with a Normal event, reason ExternalProvisioning, whose message begins Waiting for a volume to be created either by the external provisioner and names it. The provisioner then adds a Normal event, reason Provisioning, with the text External provisioner is provisioning volume for claim "inference/qwen3-weights".

The external-provisioner gives every call to the driver 15 seconds by default, its --timeout. A call that times out may still finish inside the storage system, so the provisioner treats the volume as possibly created and asks again. That is safe because the CSI specification requires CreateVolume to be idempotent: asked twice with the same name, the driver returns the same volume. After a failure the provisioner waits 1 second, then doubles the wait up to 5 minutes, and keeps retrying at that ceiling. It runs up to 100 creations at once.

One failure sends the Pod back to the scheduler. If the driver answers CreateVolume with RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED, the provisioner records ProvisioningFailed and removes volume.kubernetes.io/selected-node from the claim, so the scheduler can choose again. A driver can prevent that by publishing capacity. With storageCapacity: true in its CSIDriver object and --enable-capacity on the provisioner, which polls every minute by default, CSIStorageCapacity objects tell the scheduler how much space each zone has before it picks a node.

Chapter 4 of 13

Claim and volume are bound to each other

One claim, one volume, and a rule for how it ends

The PersistentVolume records what the driver made: the driver's name, the volume's ID, the size, the access modes and the zone it can be reached from. It also records which claim it is for. The persistent volume controller completes the pair. It writes the volume's name into the claim, and both objects show the phase Bound. The binding is one to one and lasts until the claim is deleted. What happens then is the reclaim policy, copied from the class when the volume was made. Delete removes the PersistentVolume and the real disk with it, weights included. Retain keeps both. The volume then shows the phase Released, and nobody else can claim it until an administrator cleans it up. With the claim bound, the scheduler binds the Pod to gpu-node-1.

The controller marks its work with annotations. pv.kubernetes.io/bind-completed on the claim says the binding is finished. pv.kubernetes.io/bound-by-controller says the controller made it, as opposed to a claim that named its volume in advance. pv.kubernetes.io/provisioned-by on the volume names the driver. Besides watching for changes, the controller goes over every claim and volume again every 15 seconds by default, so a missed update is picked up.

A volume has five phases. Pending is the brief state of a volume just created. Available means free. Bound means bound to a claim. Released means its claim is gone and it has not been reclaimed. Failed means automatic reclamation went wrong. kubectl get pv shows the phase under STATUS, next to CAPACITY, ACCESS MODES, RECLAIM POLICY and CLAIM.

Chapter 5 of 13

The disk is attached to the node

A VolumeAttachment object asks, and the driver answers

The disk exists in the storage system, and gpu-node-1 cannot see it yet. Attaching is the step that connects a disk to one machine, the way a cloud attaches a block device to a virtual machine. It is done from the control plane. No program on the node takes part. A controller inside kube-controller-manager, the attach and detach controller, sees a Pod on gpu-node-1 that uses the volume. It creates a VolumeAttachment object that names the driver, the node and the volume. A second helper beside the driver, external-attacher, watches for these objects. It calls ControllerPublishVolume on the driver. When the storage system reports success, the helper sets status.attached to true. A new block device appears on the node. It has no mount point yet.

The VolumeAttachment's name is csi- followed by the SHA-256 of the volume handle, the driver's name and the node's name, so the same three always give the same object. The attacher gives each call 15 seconds, runs 10 workers and backs off from 1 second to 5 minutes on failure. Only one copy of it is active at a time. If it dies, another takes over in about 15 seconds. When the driver can list its attachments, the attacher compares them once a minute with what Kubernetes expects, and attaches again a volume that someone detached by hand.

The driver can refuse. FAILED_PRECONDITION means the volume is published to another node and has no multi-node capability. RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED means the node has reached its maximum number of attached volumes. A driver reports that maximum as max_volumes_per_node, and the scheduler counts against it. When the attach succeeds, the controller records a Normal event starting AttachVolume.Attach succeeded for volume.

Chapter 6 of 13

The driver's half on the node

How the kubelet finds the plugin and learns what it can do

Everything from here runs on gpu-node-1, and it needs the driver's node plugin. That plugin runs as a Pod on every node, usually from a DaemonSet, next to a small helper called node-driver-registrar. The helper puts a registration socket at /var/lib/kubelet/plugins_registry/block.csi.northlight.example-reg.sock. The kubelet watches that directory, finds the socket, and learns from it the path of the driver's own socket, typically /var/lib/kubelet/plugins/block.csi.northlight.example/csi.sock. From then on the kubelet speaks gRPC to the driver over that Unix socket.

At registration the kubelet calls NodeGetInfo. The driver answers with its node_id for this machine, here node-1-id, the most volumes it can attach, max_volumes_per_node, and the zone the node is in. The kubelet writes these into a CSINode object, where the attach and detach controller and the scheduler read them.

The cluster also holds a CSIDriver object for each driver. It tells the kubelet how to treat the driver's volumes. attachRequired defaults to true, which is why the attach step ran. podInfoOnMount: true makes the kubelet pass the Pod's name, namespace, UID and service account in the publish call. fsGroupPolicy defaults to ReadWriteOnceWithFSType, which decides whether the kubelet changes file ownership on the volume.

Before any mount the kubelet asks the plugin which node calls it supports. If STAGE_UNSTAGE_VOLUME is among them, it will stage and then publish. Without it the kubelet skips the stage step and only publishes. The kubelet's volume manager runs its loop every 100 ms and waits up to 10 minutes for an attach before it gives up.

Chapter 7 of 13

Staged once for the node, published once for the Pod

Two mounts, so that a second Pod on the node needs only one

From here everything happens on gpu-node-1. The kubelet talks to the driver's node plugin, a Pod of the driver that runs on every node, through a Unix socket. It makes two calls. NodeStageVolume is called once for a volume on a node, before any Pod uses it. For a block device the plugin creates a filesystem on it if it has none, which is true only the first time, and mounts it at a staging path under the kubelet's plugins directory. NodePublishVolume is called once for each Pod. The plugin bind-mounts the staged filesystem into that Pod's own directory under /var/lib/kubelet/pods. A bind mount makes a directory that is already mounted appear at a second path. If another Pod on this node uses the claim, it needs only the second call.

The CSI specification fixes the order. NodeStageVolume comes only after ControllerPublishVolume has succeeded, and it must succeed before any NodePublishVolume. Every one of these calls must be idempotent. A stage call for a volume that is already staged at the same path with the same capability returns success. That is what lets the kubelet simply retry after a timeout or a restart without asking what state the node is in.

The kubelet's log records each step. After the stage it writes MountVolume.MountDevice succeeded with the device mount path, the globalmount directory. After the publish it writes MountVolume.SetUp succeeded. Whether the plugin formats the disk, and with which filesystem, is the driver's choice. The class or the volume's fs_type ask for one, here ext4, and the kubelet does not format anything itself.

Chapter 8 of 13

Ownership, read-only, and a mount that never finishes

What else the kubelet does to a volume before the container starts

Between the publish and the container, the kubelet may change who owns the files. If the Pod sets securityContext.fsGroup, the kubelet by default walks the whole volume and changes the group and permissions of every file to match. On a volume holding 34.3 GB of weights in many files, that walk can take a long time on every start. The setting fsGroupChangePolicy: OnRootMismatch makes the kubelet look at the top directory first and skip the walk when it already matches. For a CSI volume the kubelet applies fsGroup only when the driver's fsGroupPolicy allows it, the mount is writable, and, for the default policy, the volume has an fsType and a ReadWriteOnce access mode. Otherwise it logs that it is skipping fsGroup.

A mount can also be made read-only for the container, with readOnly: true on the volumeMount. That makes the container's view read-only. It does not make the volume itself read-only, and on Linux mounts below that path are not read-only unless recursiveReadOnly is also set. For a claim that several replicas only read, persistentVolumeClaim.readOnly: true in the Pod's volumes is the setting that asks the driver for a read-only publish.

When a volume does not arrive, the kubelet waits 2 minutes and 3 seconds for the Pod's volumes. The odd 3 seconds is there so the timeout can be told apart from others. Then it records a Warning, reason FailedMount, with a message starting Unable to attach or mount volumes and listing the unmounted and unattached volumes, and it tries again. Once the volume is mounted, the kubelet reports its use in metrics such as kubelet_volume_stats_used_bytes and kubelet_volume_stats_capacity_bytes, labelled by namespace and claim.

Chapter 9 of 13

The mount enters the container

One more bind mount, made inside the container's mount namespace

The volume is now a directory on the node, and the container does not exist yet. The kubelet sends CreateContainer to containerd with a list of mounts. The entry for the weights pairs the Pod's directory on the node with the path /models in the container. containerd copies it into the container's config.json as a bind mount. When runc builds the container, it creates a new mount namespace, which is a private table of mounts. In it, runc binds the node's directory onto /models under the container's future root, and then switches to that root. After the switch the node's paths are gone from the container's view and /models remains. vLLM opens files there like any local files. The first Pod downloads the 34.3 GB into it once. Every later start reads them from the disk.

Three paths now show the same ext4 filesystem: the staging mount for the node, the bind mount in the Pod's directory, and /models inside the container. Unmounting has to undo them in the reverse order. The container's mount disappears with its mount namespace when the container ends. The kubelet then asks the plugin for NodeUnpublishVolume to remove the Pod's bind mount. When the last Pod on the node using the volume is gone, it asks for NodeUnstageVolume to remove the staging mount.

The mount's propagation is private. Mounts made later under /models on the node do not appear in the container, and the container cannot make mounts that appear on the node. That is the default, and other settings exist for special cases such as a driver's own Pod.

Chapter 10 of 13

Who may share the weights

Four access modes, counted in nodes and in Pods

The Deployment has to go back to its usual two replicas, one on each GPU node, and both want the same weights. Whether they can share one volume depends on its access mode. ReadWriteOnce allows the volume to be mounted read-write by a single node. Several Pods on that node can all use it. ReadOnlyMany allows many nodes to mount it read-only. ReadWriteMany allows many nodes to mount it read-write. ReadWriteOncePod allows a single Pod in the whole cluster. The claim here is ReadWriteOnce on a block disk. Four replicas on gpu-node-1 could share it, one for each GPU. A replica on gpu-node-2 could not. For replicas on both nodes the weights belong on storage that many nodes can mount, such as a file share with a ReadWriteMany class, mounted read-only by each Pod.

A volume is mounted with one access mode at a time, even if it supports several. The kubelet turns the mode into the CSI term the driver understands: ReadWriteOnce becomes SINGLE_NODE_WRITER, ReadOnlyMany becomes MULTI_NODE_READER_ONLY, ReadWriteMany becomes MULTI_NODE_MULTI_WRITER. For a driver that supports the finer modes, ReadWriteOnce becomes SINGLE_NODE_MULTI_WRITER and ReadWriteOncePod becomes SINGLE_NODE_SINGLE_WRITER. ReadWriteOncePod is stable since Kubernetes 1.29 and needs recent CSI helpers.

There are other ways to give every replica the weights. A StatefulSet can make one claim per replica. A new claim can clone an existing one by naming it as its dataSource, in the same namespace and at least as large, if the driver supports cloning. An image volume mounts an OCI image or artifact read-only into the Pod, so the weights can be shipped like a container image. A failed pull of it blocks the containers from starting.

Chapter 11 of 13

What protects the weights, and how the disk grows

Finalizers hold the claim while it is used, and expansion keeps the same disk

Deleting a claim that a Pod is using does not pull the disk out from under it. Kubernetes puts the finalizer kubernetes.io/pvc-protection on every claim. A delete then only marks the claim Terminating, and the claim stays until no Pod uses it. A bound volume carries kubernetes.io/pv-protection in the same way. A CSI volume with the policy Delete also carries external-provisioner.volume.kubernetes.io/finalizer, so the PersistentVolume object goes away only after the provisioner has called DeleteVolume and the real disk is gone, in whichever order the claim and volume are deleted.

With the policy Delete the end is final. Once the last Pod lets go, the claim goes, the disk is deleted and the 34.3 GB must be downloaded again. With Retain, the volume stays Released with the data on it. It is not bound again by itself. An administrator deletes the PersistentVolume and either deletes the disk by hand or makes a new volume object for the same disk. The older Recycle policy, which wiped the files, is deprecated and works only for nfs and hostPath.

A larger model needs a larger disk. If the class has allowVolumeExpansion: true, editing the claim's request from 50Gi to 100Gi asks the driver to grow the same disk. Nothing is replaced. The filesystem is then grown on the node when a Pod uses the claim, online if the driver supports it, for ext3, ext4 and XFS. A claim cannot shrink below its current size. 100Gi is 107,374,182,400 bytes, twice 53,687,091,200.

Chapter 12 of 13

The Pod moves and the disk has to follow

A ReadWriteOnce volume is detached from one node before another gets it

gpu-node-1 is drained for maintenance. Pod qwen3-58b4d7f9c6-w4j7n is asked to stop, and the Deployment creates a replacement at once, which lands on gpu-node-2. The Deployment still runs one replica here, so no other qwen3 Pod is on gpu-node-2. The replacement belongs to the same revision, so it keeps the hash and gets new random characters: qwen3-58b4d7f9c6-c2x5r. The volume is still attached to gpu-node-1, because the old Pod has not finished stopping. The attach and detach controller refuses to attach a ReadWriteOnce volume to a second node. It writes a warning on the new Pod and waits. Up to Kubernetes 1.34 the message begins Multi-Attach error. From 1.35 it begins Waiting for detach. The new Pod sits in ContainerCreating. When the old container has exited, the kubelet on gpu-node-1 unmounts the volume from the Pod's directory and from the staging path. The controller then deletes the old VolumeAttachment, the driver detaches the disk, and the whole chain runs again on gpu-node-2. The weights arrive with the disk.

The warning names the Pods holding the volume only when they are in the new Pod's own namespace. For Pods elsewhere it gives a count, such as and 1 pod(s) in different namespaces. With no Pod known at all, the 1.35 message reads Volume is already exclusively attached to one node, waiting on detach before it can be attached to another node. The controller's source names the usual cause of a lasting warning: a workload with more than one replica sharing a ReadWriteOnce claim across nodes.

The CSI specification sets the order of the move. ControllerUnpublishVolume must come only after every NodeUnpublishVolume and NodeUnstageVolume for the volume has succeeded. The kubelet reports the end of its part by removing the volume from status.volumesInUse on its Node. The controller checks its state every 100 ms.

Chapter 13 of 13

When the old node is dead

Six minutes of waiting, then a forced detach, or a taint to skip the wait

A drain lets the old kubelet unmount the volume. A crash does not. By now gpu-node-1's maintenance is done, and kubectl uncordon gpu-node-1 has made it schedulable again. Suppose gpu-node-2 then loses power while qwen3-58b4d7f9c6-c2x5r runs on it, at 1000 s on this clock. Nothing on gpu-node-2 can confirm that its container stopped or that the mounts are gone. The node controller marks the Node Ready Unknown once it has heard nothing for node-monitor-grace-period, 50 seconds by default, and adds the taint node.kubernetes.io/unreachable with the effect NoExecute. Every Pod tolerates that taint for 300 seconds, through a toleration that admission adds by default. Then the Pod is evicted: it gets a deletionTimestamp and shows as Terminating, at about 1000 + 50 + 300 = 1350 s. The ReplicaSet does not wait for a Terminating Pod. It creates a replacement at once, qwen3-58b4d7f9c6-f8m3v, the scheduler puts it on gpu-node-1, and the replacement waits with the same FailedAttachVolume warning as before.

After that nothing moves on its own. The attach and detach controller stops wanting a Pod's volume attached only when the Pod is gone from the API or counts as terminated. Terminated means the phase is Failed or Succeeded, or the Pod is being deleted and every one of its container statuses is terminated or waiting. The statuses of qwen3-58b4d7f9c6-c2x5r still say running, because the only component that could change them is the kubelet on the dead node. So the volume stays wanted on gpu-node-2, no detach is ever requested, and the replacement can wait indefinitely. The documentation on node shutdown describes Pods in this state as stuck in terminating status forever.

The controller does have a timer, but it starts only once a detach has been requested and the node has not confirmed the unmount. It is ReconcilerMaxWaitForUnmountDuration, an internal value of the controller fixed at 6 minutes, with no command-line flag. When it has run out and the node is unhealthy, the controller force detaches and logs attacherDetacher.DetachVolume started: this volume is not safe to detach, but maxWaitForUnmountDuration expired, force detaching. The word unsafe is meant. A forced detach breaks the CSI rule that the node must unmount first, and if the node was only cut off and is still writing, the data can be corrupted. The kube-controller-manager flag --disable-force-detach-on-timeout turns this off for storage where that risk is not acceptable, and then only the out-of-service taint below can free the volume.

An operator who knows the node is really off has two ways to start the move. Adding the taint node.kubernetes.io/out-of-service with the effect NoExecute or NoSchedule tells Kubernetes the node is out of service. The Pod garbage collector then force deletes the terminating Pods on that not ready node, and the controller detaches their volumes at once, without the 6 minutes. The taint must be removed by hand once the node is back. The other way is kubectl delete pod with --grace-period=0 --force, which removes the Pod from the API. That requests the detach, but the unmount on gpu-node-2 is never confirmed, so the 6 minute timer runs first. On this clock the operator adds the taint at 1500 s and the disk moves to gpu-node-1 within seconds. A forced delete of the Pod at 1500 s would instead have led to a forced detach at 1500 + 360 = 1860 s.

What to keep

A claim, a disk, and three mounts

The weights reached the container through a chain in which every link has one owner. The claim asked and the class said how. The scheduler chose the node first, so the driver could create the disk in a place that node can reach, with retries and events along the way. The persistent volume controller bound claim and volume. The attach and detach controller asked for the disk to be connected to the node. On the node the kubelet found the driver through its socket, had the volume staged once, published into the Pod's directory, and listed as a mount for the container, where runc bound it at /models. The access mode set who could share it, finalizers kept it while it was used, and the reclaim policy decided what happens to the weights at the end. When the Pod moved, the disk was detached and followed, and when a node died, the disk stayed with the dead node until an operator declared the node out of service and the controller took it back by force.
