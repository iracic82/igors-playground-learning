Scheduling

One GPU pool, many owners

Quota first, priority only for what is left over

GPUs are too expensive to leave idle, so one pool of them usually serves several owners. Here there are three. A customer who pays for a dedicated model endpoint. A public endpoint anyone can call. And batch jobs that can wait. All of them want more at peak time, and there are only eight GPUs. Kubernetes has a tool for this, Pod priority, and it gives the wrong answer: the most important workload can take everything. What you actually want has two parts. Each owner has a number of GPUs that are theirs no matter what. Anything beyond that is spare, and spare capacity goes to whoever matters most right now and can be taken back. This lesson runs both designs on the same pool. The first chapter says why such pools exist at all. Large operators plan about $700 billion of capital spending for 2026, most of it for AI data centres, and two in three organisations that host generative AI run some or all of their inference on Kubernetes.

Chapter 1 of 13

Why GPUs are pooled, and why on Kubernetes

Capital this expensive cannot sit idle, and most of it runs on Kubernetes

Every GPU in this pool was bought by someone, and the largest buyers of them keep raising what they spend. The four largest cloud operators, Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta, tell investors each quarter how much they plan to spend that year on land, buildings, servers and chips. This is called capital expenditure, or capex. After their first quarter results at the end of April 2026, the plans for 2026 were about $200 billion at Amazon, $180 to $190 billion at Alphabet, $190 billion at Microsoft and $125 to $145 billion at Meta. Added up, that is $695 to $725 billion, so a little over $700 billion at the top of the ranges. In 2025 the same four spent a record of about $410 billion. $725 billion is 77% more than $410 billion, so the plan for one year grew by nearly four fifths. Most of it is for AI data centres. Some of the rise is higher prices for the same hardware. Microsoft's finance chief put $25 billion of its $190 billion down to what she called higher component pricing. Press reports on the call tied that mostly to the rising cost of memory chips.

Forecasts for the rest of the decade are larger again. In April 2025 McKinsey estimated that data centres worldwide will need about $6.7 trillion of capital by 2030 to keep up with demand for compute. Of that, $5.2 trillion is for data centres that run AI and $1.5 trillion is for those that run traditional IT. The $5.2 trillion is McKinsey's middle case. In its fastest case, 205 gigawatts of new AI capacity is added between 2025 and 2030 and needs $7.9 trillion. In its most constrained case, 78 gigawatts are added and need $3.7 trillion. A gigawatt here is electrical power for the data centre, which is how the industry counts capacity. These are forecasts with a wide range, and McKinsey says its AI figure counts only part of the chain of suppliers, so it is likely to be low.

Money on that scale explains the shape of this lesson. A GPU that sits idle is capital that earns nothing. A GPU held by one team that uses it two hours a day is idle for the other twenty two. So companies put GPUs in a shared pool and let several owners draw from it, which is cheaper than giving each owner enough GPUs for its own peak. Sharing brings the question the rest of the lesson answers: when everyone wants more at once, who gets the GPUs.

Very often the pool is run by Kubernetes. The Cloud Native Computing Foundation, or CNCF, is the foundation that hosts Kubernetes. Its 2025 Annual Cloud Native Survey, published on 20 January 2026, found that 66% of organisations that host generative AI models use Kubernetes to manage some or all of their inference workloads. Inference means running a trained model to answer requests, which is what vLLM does with Qwen3-32B here. About two in three, in other words. When the figure is quoted second hand, check it against the survey itself, which says 66%. The same survey has a second number that is easy to mix up with it: 82% of container users run Kubernetes in production, up from 66% in 2023. It also shows that AI on Kubernetes is young. Only 7% of organisations deploy models every day and 47% deploy them occasionally. In a separate finding, 44% do not yet run AI or machine learning workloads on Kubernetes at all. The survey reports these as separate findings, so they are not one split and do not add up to 100%. Treat these as one survey of the cloud native community. They show a direction among the people who answer a CNCF survey.

Kubernetes gives the pool its basic tools. A GPU node advertises its GPUs as the resource nvidia.com/gpu, a Pod asks for some, and the scheduler binds the Pod to a node with enough free. What the default scheduler does not know about is owners. The scheduler on this board is the KAI Scheduler, which NVIDIA released as open source and which the CNCF accepted as a Sandbox project on 21 December 2025. Sandbox is the CNCF's entry level for new projects, below Incubating and Graduated. The CNCF blog post that announced it lists what KAI adds for large AI clusters: gang scheduling, which starts all the Pods of a job together or none of them, hierarchical queues shared by Dominant Resource Fairness for clusters with many teams, and asynchronous binding, which binds Pods to nodes in the background, run against clusters of more than 10,000 GPUs. Dominant Resource Fairness compares owners by the resource each uses the largest share of. In this pool every Pod asks for one GPU, so the GPU is that resource, and a fair split simply compares GPU counts. The next chapters build the pool, show what goes wrong with Pod priority alone, and then fix it with KAI's queues.

Chapter 2 of 13

Eight GPUs, three owners

The pool is full, and everyone is content

Look at the two nodes. Each slot is one GPU with one Pod on it. On the board the dedicated endpoint's GPUs, the public endpoint's GPUs and the batch jobs' GPUs each have their own colour, and a GPU whose Pod is starting or terminating shows that state. The dedicated endpoint runs three replicas, the public endpoint runs three, and two batch jobs use what is left. Nothing is idle. The default Kubernetes scheduler placed them. It was told one thing about importance, through a PriorityClass on each Pod: dedicated first, public second, batch last. While demand is steady this looks like a perfectly good design.

A PriorityClass is a small cluster object with a name and an integer value. A Pod names one in spec.priorityClassName, and the API server copies the value into the Pod's spec.priority when the Pod is created. A class made by a user can have a value up to 1,000,000,000. This pool has three classes with the values 300, 200 and 100. The numbers themselves are illustrative. Only their order matters, because the scheduler sorts waiting Pods by this value and treats a higher value as more important.

Sharing is the point of the pool. Every Pod here asks for nvidia.com/gpu: 1, so a GPU is either held by one Pod or free. A model server such as vLLM with Qwen3-32B reads about 34.3 GB of weights from the official FP8 build Qwen3-32B-FP8, 32.8 billion parameters stored mostly at one byte each with some layers kept at higher precision, and takes minutes before it answers its first request. This lesson uses about three minutes. A replica that is removed is therefore gone for minutes, and a GPU that sits idle earns nothing in that time. The rest of the lesson is about who may take a GPU from whom.

Chapter 3 of 13

Priority alone takes everything

The most important workload starves the next one

The customer behind the dedicated endpoint gets a traffic spike and its autoscaler asks for eight replicas. With Pod priority as the only rule, the scheduler does what it was told. The new Pods outrank everything else, so it evicts batch, and then it evicts the public endpoint, all of it. One customer now holds the whole pool and a service that other people depend on is gone. Nobody decided that. Being less important than a customer when capacity is spare was read as being less important than that customer always.

The mechanism is called preemption. When a Pod fits on no node, the scheduler looks for a node where removing Pods of lower priority would make room. It evicts those Pods and writes the node's name into status.nominatedNodeName of the waiting Pod. The victims get their graceful termination period, and the waiting Pod is placed once they are gone. This runs once for each of the five waiting Pods, and each one needs one GPU, so five Pods are evicted.

Two standard tools do not help. A PodDisruptionBudget on the public endpoint is honoured only as best effort. The scheduler prefers victims whose budget stays intact and preempts anyway when it finds none. A ResourceQuota can cap how much a namespace may request at one priority class. A cap of three on the customer would protect the public endpoint, and it would also stop the customer from using GPUs that are idle. A cap is a ceiling. What the pool needs is a floor for each owner.

Chapter 4 of 13

Three ways to fix it

A spreadsheet, two Deployments, or a scheduler that knows quotas

The talk this lesson follows was given by a team that runs a managed inference platform on one GPU node pool per region. Their first fix was manual. A spreadsheet held who was owed how many GPUs, a chat channel carried the requests, and an on-call engineer deleted Pods of workloads that held too much. Comparing the current state with the wanted one by hand took up to several hours for one change, and it got worse with every new workload.

The second option needs no new component. Each workload becomes two Deployments. The first holds the guaranteed replicas at a very high priority. The second holds the extra replicas at the priority of the workload's tier. The spike can then evict only extra replicas. The cost is everywhere else. A rolling update has to be done twice, the autoscaler may only scale the second Deployment, a quota change means moving replicas between two objects, and nothing shows the workload as one thing.

The third option is a scheduler built for quotas. The team compared KAI Scheduler, Kueue, Volcano and YuniKorn. Their first test was preemption by priority above quota. A queue that wants its quota back must be able to evict Pods running above quota, and Pods above quota must also be ranked against each other. By their account only KAI and Kueue did both without extra work. They chose KAI for three more reasons. It places GPUs and CPUs with NUMA in mind, which keeps a Pod's GPUs and CPUs close together and cuts traffic between NUMA nodes, the separate memory regions inside one server. It can delay an eviction. And it works with NVIDIA Grove, which they may adopt.

Chapter 5 of 13

Give every owner a queue

A quota is what you are owed. Priority is for the rest

Start again with a scheduler built for this. KAI Scheduler adds an object called a Queue, one per owner. A queue has a quota: the number of GPUs that owner is guaranteed. Add the quotas up and they should fit in the pool. Here that is three, three and none, six of eight. A queue also has a priority, and it means something narrower than before. It only decides who gets capacity beyond quota. The values 300, 200 and 100 now belong to the three queues, and the Pods no longer carry them.

A Queue is cluster-scoped, with apiVersion scheduling.run.ai/v2. Under spec.resources it has one block each for gpu, cpu and memory, and every block has three fields. quota is the guarantee. A value of 0 or no value means nothing is guaranteed, and -1 means unlimited. limit is a hard cap. overQuotaWeight splits spare capacity between queues of the same priority. GPU quota counts whole GPUs, CPU is in millicores and memory in megabytes.

The limit is where a first setup goes wrong. A limit of 0, or no limit at all, means the queue may hold none of that resource, its own quota included. Each resource is checked on its own. A queue that sets only the gpu block leaves cpu and memory at a limit of 0, and every Pod that requests CPU stays Pending with the reason OverLimit. Set limit: -1 on all three unless you mean to cap. A queue can also name a parentQueue to form a tree. Only queues without children take workloads. The three here are flat.

Chapter 6 of 13

How a Pod joins a queue

One label, one scheduler name and a priority below 100

A Pod joins a queue with one label, kai.scheduler/queue, and asks for this scheduler in spec.schedulerName. The default scheduler leaves alone any Pod that names another scheduler, so both can run in one cluster. KAI does not schedule single Pods. A component called the pod grouper follows a Pod's owner references to the top, from ReplicaSet to Deployment, and writes a PodGroup for the workload. The PodGroup carries the queue, the priority class and minMember, the number of Pods that must run together. For a Deployment it makes one PodGroup with minMember 1.

The priority class on a Pod now has a second job. Under KAI a Pod whose PriorityClass is 100 or more can't be preempted and can't run above quota. KAI installs four classes: train at 50, build-preemptible at 75, build at 100 and inference at 125. A Pod with no class gets a default by workload type, and for a Deployment that default is inference. A model server left at its default is therefore protected and also locked inside its quota.

So every Pod here uses a PriorityClass below 100, the one named train with the value 50. That lets it borrow spare capacity and give it back. Inside one queue this value still orders work, and a higher Pod can evict a lower one of the same queue. It never reaches across queues. From now on every decision starts with the same question: is this owner within what it is owed.

Chapter 7 of 13

Spare GPUs are lent out

Batch runs on capacity nobody is owed

The same pool, read the new way. The dedicated endpoint holds three GPUs and is owed three. The public endpoint holds three and is owed three. Batch is owed nothing and holds two. Those two are the spare capacity, lent to the only queue that asked. This is why a pool with quotas runs fuller than separate clusters would: capacity an owner is not using this minute does useful work for someone else. The loan has one condition, and batch accepted it by having no quota. It can be called in.

KAI works this out again in every scheduling cycle, as a number per queue called the fair share. It has two phases. In the first, every queue gets the smaller of its quota and what it asks for, and priority plays no part. That gives 3, 3 and 0, which uses six GPUs. In the second, what remains goes to the queues in order of priority. All of it is offered to the highest priority before any reaches the next. The queue at 300 wants nothing more, the queue at 200 wants nothing more, and batch at 100 wants two and gets both.

overQuotaWeight matters only between queues of equal priority. Suppose two batch queues, both at priority 100, with weights 2 and 1 and three spare GPUs. They would get two and one. Priority always comes first. A queue with priority 2 and weight 1 is served before a queue with priority 1 and weight 100.

Chapter 8 of 13

The spike, again

The customer takes the spare GPUs and stops there

The dedicated endpoint spikes again and asks for eight replicas. It already holds its three, so the five new Pods are all above quota. Above quota, queues compete by priority, and this queue has the highest. So the scheduler looks for capacity it is allowed to take: GPUs held above quota by a queue of lower priority. There are exactly two, the batch jobs. They are evicted and two of the new Pods start. The other three stay pending, because every remaining GPU is held by a queue within its quota, and quota is not for sale at any priority. The public endpoint did not lose a single replica.

Each cycle runs its actions in a fixed order: Allocate, Consolidate, Reclaim, Preempt and StaleGangEviction. Allocate places Pods on free capacity and evicts nobody. It finds no free GPU. Reclaim is the only action that moves capacity between queues. The fair share has changed with the request. Phase one still gives 3, 3 and 0. In phase two the two spare GPUs now go to dedicated-a, which is first in line and wants five more. Its fair share is 5, and batch's is 0.

Reclaim needs four things to be true. The two workloads are in different queues. The victim can be preempted. The queue that asks is below its fair share, here 3 of 5. The victim's queue is above its own, here 2 of 0. KAI keeps the word preempt for a higher Pod evicting a lower one inside the same queue.

Chapter 9 of 13

Quota beats priority

A lower queue takes its GPUs back from a higher one

Now run it the other way round. It is night, the public endpoint has scaled down to one replica, and the dedicated endpoint has grown into everything that was free. It holds seven GPUs, four of them above its quota. In the morning the public endpoint scales back to three. Its queue has a lower priority than the one holding those GPUs. It does not matter. The public queue is below its quota, and a queue below quota that asks for capacity gets it, from whoever is above theirs. Two of the customer's replicas are evicted. The talk points to one setting that softens this: a delay before the eviction, long enough for the cluster autoscaler to add a node, so that often nothing has to be evicted at all.

The numbers show why. At night the public queue asked for one GPU, so phase one gave 3, 1 and 0 and left four spare, all of which went to dedicated-a. Its fair share was 7. In the morning public asks for three. Phase one gives 3, 3 and 0, two are spare, and the fair share of dedicated-a drops to 5 while it still holds 7. KAI compares queues by allocated over fair share. That is 7 / 5 = 1.4 for dedicated-a and 1 / 3 for public. After two evictions both stand at 1.

The guarantee has one switch. queuePriorityInQuotaReclaim, off by default and marked alpha, lets a queue of strictly higher priority take GPUs that a lower queue holds within its quota, as long as the taker stays within its own quota. Leave it off if a quota is a promise.

Chapter 10 of 13

Waiting before evicting

A delay gives the autoscaler time to add a node

The delay the talk mentions is an annotation on the workload's Pods, kai.scheduler/preemption-delay, with a duration such as "5m". While the window is open the workload does not cause any eviction, through reclaim, preempt or consolidation. It is still placed at once if capacity is free. It stays a Pending Pod that fits nowhere, and that is exactly what a cluster autoscaler reacts to. If a node arrives in time, the Pods start there and nobody is evicted. If none does, evictions go ahead when the window closes.

The window is counted from the creation of the workload, or from its last eviction if that is later. With the default layout from the chapter How a Pod joins a queue, the whole public Deployment is one PodGroup, created with the Deployment. Its five minutes ran out long before this morning, so a scale-up of that Deployment gets no delay at all. The replay below therefore runs the pod grouper with --deployment-gang-schedule=false, which gives each Pod its own PodGroup, so every new replica starts its own window.

Replay the morning with a five-minute delay on the public endpoint. At 08:00:00 its two new Pods are Pending. The autoscaler orders a node, and a third node with four GPUs joins at 08:03:40, which is 220 seconds later. Allocate places both Pods on it. The customer keeps all seven replicas. The other two of node-3's four GPUs stay free. Allocate gives them to the next Pending Pod of any queue, and in this replay none is waiting. The public endpoint waited 220 seconds for GPUs it was owed, and that is the price. Every time in this replay is illustrative.

A second setting protects the other side. A Queue can carry reclaimMinRuntime and preemptMinRuntime, and a workload in that queue is not reclaimed or preempted until it has run that long. Both default to 0s. A model server here needs about three minutes to load the 34.3 GB of weights of the FP8 build. The minimum runtime is counted from the PodGroup's last start time, LastStartTimestamp, not from each Pod's start. With the default layout of one PodGroup for the whole Deployment, that is when the Deployment first ran. A new replica of a Deployment that has run for hours therefore gets no protection from it. With one PodGroup for each Pod, as in this replay, reclaimMinRuntime: "10m" on dedicated-a keeps a replica that has just finished loading from being taken back until about seven more minutes have passed, 10 − 3 = 7.

Chapter 11 of 13

One shard for each GPU type

A quota counts GPUs and does not say which kind

A GPU quota is a count. Each whole GPU a Pod requests is one unit. A MIG device, a fixed slice of a GPU, counts by the number in its profile name, so a mig-3g device is three units. A fractional request counts by its fraction. Nothing in the quota names a GPU model. In a pool with two kinds of GPU, a queue owed three could be handed three of the older kind and the quota would be met. The talk notes an open issue on this in the KAI repository.

The team's answer is one shard per GPU type. A SchedulingShard is an object that starts a separate copy of the scheduler. That copy sees only the nodes, queues and PodGroups labelled kai.scheduler/node-pool with the shard's partitionLabelValue. Shards share nothing. Each runs its own cycle with its own quotas, and no reclaim or preemption crosses from one to another. Inside each shard the team makes one queue per workload, exactly as in this lesson.

Their worked example is a dedicated endpoint with a quota of four GPUs whose Pods each need two. Two replicas fit in the guarantee, since 2 × 2 = 4. A third replica runs above quota on spare capacity and can be evicted when another queue wants its guaranteed GPUs back. The third node in the last chapter was a what-if replay. Back in the pool this lesson builds, Northlight's eight GPUs on node-1 and node-2 are all of one type, so it has one shard. A node the autoscaler adds needs the same node-pool label, or the default shard takes it instead. A second GPU type would get its own label value, its own shard and its own three queues.

Chapter 12 of 13

What the operator sees

Three gauges per queue, a condition on the Pod and a message on the PodGroup

The state of every queue is exported as Prometheus metrics. The queue controller publishes kai_queue_deserved_gpus, which is the quota, and kai_queue_allocated_gpus, which is what the queue holds now. The scheduler publishes kai_queue_fair_share_gpu, updated in every cycle. After the spike, dedicated-a reads 3, 5 and 5. Allocated minus deserved is how much a queue has borrowed, here two. Allocated above fair share means the queue is about to give something back. All three carry the label queue_name. The kai_ prefix is the default of the --metrics-namespace flag, and an install that changes it changes every name.

An eviction leaves two traces. Before KAI deletes the Pod it sets the condition DisruptionTarget on it, with the reason PreemptionByScheduler and a message naming the workload that took its place. For a reclaim the message reads Pod, the victim's namespace and name, was preempted by workload, then the namespace and name of the PodGroup that asked. The counter kai_pod_group_evicted_pods_total rises as well, with labels for the PodGroup, the node pool and the action.

A Pod that waits explains itself on its PodGroup, under status.schedulingConditions. Three reasons cover this lesson. OverLimit means the queue's limit for some resource is too low, often 0 because it was never set. NonPreemptibleOverQuota means a Pod of class 100 or more needs GPUs beyond quota. PreemptionDelayNotElapsed means the workload is still inside its delay window. The scheduler's own log is in the kai-scheduler namespace, one Deployment per shard.

Chapter 13 of 13

What a GPU scheduler can't see

It moves Pods. The work is in requests

This design gives each owner its GPUs. It knows nothing about what runs on them, and two gaps follow. First, eviction is about Pods, and a model server is in the middle of answers. Once a Pod is marked for removal no new request is sent to it, and it gets its grace period, 30 seconds unless changed. A long answer from an agent can outlast that. Second, a quota guarantees GPUs, not what you get out of them. How many requests those GPUs serve depends on where each request is sent, which is the endpoint picker's job. The better that works, the less often an owner has to go above quota at all. Capacity is decided here. Requests are decided in front of it.

Put numbers on the first gap. KAI evicts by deleting the Pod with no options of its own, so the Pod's terminationGracePeriodSeconds applies. The kubelet sends the TERM signal, waits, and sends KILL when the period ends. An answer of 4,000 tokens at 40 tokens a second takes 100 seconds. With the default, it is cut after 30 seconds and 1,200 tokens. Raising the period to 120 seconds saves that answer and makes the owner of the GPU wait up to 120 seconds in all, 120 − 30 = 90 seconds longer than with the default.

The GPU is not useful the moment it is free either. The replica that takes it over loads about 34.3 GB of weights from the FP8 build, which this lesson puts at about three minutes. From the reclaim to the first answer is roughly 30 + 180 = 210 seconds with the default grace period. The team in the talk still reports what they wanted: higher use of the pool, and provisioning in minutes where it took hours.

What to keep

Quota first, priority for the rest

One GPU pool can serve several owners if two questions are kept apart. What is each owner guaranteed, and who gets what is left. Pod priority answers both with one number, so the most important workload can take everything. A queue per owner separates them. Its quota is a guarantee that priority cannot touch while the alpha setting queuePriorityInQuotaReclaim stays off, as it is by default, and capacity beyond quota is lent by priority and taken back when its owner asks. That keeps the pool full and keeps promises. It decides which Pods hold GPUs and nothing about the requests on them, so draining long answers and placing each request well are left to the layer in front.
