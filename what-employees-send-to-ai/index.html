BNK and gateways

What employees send to AI

Steering a company's AI traffic through one point that can read it, decide and keep a record

The other lessons on this site follow requests coming into a GPU cluster. This one follows a request going out of the company that runs the cluster. People at work paste code, contracts and customer records into AI services on the internet, often ones they signed up for themselves. The company cannot see what was sent, and blocking one service moves people to the next. The lesson follows a single paste from an engineer's laptop. It shows how the laptop is told to send only AI traffic to a proxy, the other roads to the same proxy, how the proxy knows which company and which person the traffic belongs to, why it has to be one end of the encryption, the questions it asks about each request and the actions it can take. Then it looks at what streaming does to the delay, what the record and its dashboards show, the traffic that never arrives, how an agent's tool calls are inspected, what happens when the proxy does not answer, and how a company starts by only watching. F5 calls this product Workforce AI Security. It is a forward proxy, the opposite end of the path from F5 AI Gateway, which is a reverse proxy in front of models a company runs or buys. Two early chapters place it: the five stages of AI use a company passes through, and how its job differs from the AI Gateway's. Four late chapters follow how a company decides to adopt it: the seven questions a trial must pass, what each team has to see during the trial and the objections it raises, and the loop that keeps governance going after the trial ends.

Chapter 1 of 19

Code and a key leave in one paste

The company sees a hostname and a byte count

Mara is a platform engineer at Northlight. A script that warms up the qwen3 model servers keeps getting a 401 from the gateway, so she pastes all 48 lines into a consumer chat service and asks what is wrong. Line 7 holds a live API key for api.northlight.example. She signed up for that service herself, with a personal address. Northlight has no contract with it, so nothing says how long the paste is kept or whether a model is trained on it.

The company's firewall saw very little. A TLS session starts with a ClientHello, and that message carries the name of the server in its server_name extension before any encryption is in place. That is where the hostname chat.public-ai.example comes from. In TLS 1.3 every handshake message after the ServerHello is encrypted, so even the certificate the service sends back is hidden. What is left for a device on the path is the laptop's address, the destination and port 443, that one name, and the sizes and timing of encrypted records. About 2 kB went out. Nothing in that says it was source code, or that line 7 held a key.

A rule at that layer can allow a destination or block it. F5's material names what follows from that. Security becomes the team that says no and is seen as slowing AI adoption down. People who are blocked turn to unsanctioned AI anyway, on another service or on their phone, which is called shadow AI. And traffic to services that are allowed stays a blind spot, because a destination rule never sees the prompt or the data in it. The pressure to allow is real. F5's slide gives a 15 percent productivity gain for employees who use AI assistants for routine work such as drafting, searching and summarising internal documents. It cites the study 'Generative AI at Work', published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics. The slide gives only that figure and the citation. The study itself followed more than 5,000 customer support agents and measured how many issues each resolved per hour with an AI assistant and without one. Later versions of the paper report about 15 percent more. Its first NBER working paper gave about 14 percent.

Chapter 2 of 19

Five stages of AI use, and where sight runs out

Risk climbs with each stage while what a company can see falls

Mara's paste is the simplest kind of AI use there is. F5's material arranges the kinds a company meets into five stages, in the order most companies reach them. Individual use comes first: a person and an assistant, with typed prompts and pasted text, as in the last chapter. Integrated use is second. AI is embedded in tools people already have, and assistants reach company data through MCP and other tool connections. Specialized use is third: developer tools and coding agents, such as the assistant in Mara's editor. Agentic AI is fourth: cloud agents and agentic processes that carry out whole tasks of several steps on their own. External use cases are fifth. These are AI inside the company's own applications, the AI it builds and serves to customers, such as the qwen3 model servers on Northlight's two GPU nodes.

F5 draws two curves across those stages. One is risk, made of access and autonomy. Each stage reaches more data and acts with less review by a person, so it rises. The other is visibility, made of security and control, and it falls. Each stage moves AI traffic further from anything a web filter or a firewall can read. The place where the two curves part is labelled the tipping point, where existing tools stop keeping up. The widening space between them is the visibility gap. The tipping point sits at the very start of the first stage on F5's slide. The firewall in the last chapter had already lost sight of Mara's paste there.

The second stage is the easiest to miss. An office suite or a note-taking app that the company approved years ago gains an AI feature in an update. The vendor is the same, the contract is the same, and data now goes to a model. F5's customers describe it in one line: AI is turning up inside apps we already approved. F5 lists embedded AI detection and embedded AI monitoring under the heading Gain Visibility for exactly this case. Its session does not describe how the proxy tells an app's AI requests apart from the rest of that app's traffic. What the rest of this lesson makes clear is the condition. The proxy can only see a request that is steered to it, so the app's AI domain has to be one the proxy receives.

F5 also explains why it starts with these first stages. In the ranking of market priorities it quotes, AI user control and AI governance come first. An AI security platform and agentic security come second. AI application security, multimodal AI, AI security posture management (AISPM) and AI code harnesses come third. Its slide sums the ranking up in one sentence: AI usage control is a now problem. The proxy in this lesson works on the first three stages wherever their traffic leaves a managed device for a public AI service, and the agent chapter shows how far it reaches into tool use. AI the company builds and serves itself, the fifth stage, is the job of a different proxy, which the next chapter compares.

Chapter 3 of 19

Two proxies that do not overlap

The AI Gateway is an endpoint an app calls, this proxy is the path people's traffic takes

F5 sells two products that both sit between a caller and a model, and its material is firm that they do not overlap. F5 AI Gateway is a reverse proxy. It stands in front of models and is an endpoint that applications call on purpose: one hostname, an API key or a token, and a REST or gRPC API, with MCP traffic to tools as well. Workforce AI Security is a forward proxy. It sits at the network or endpoint egress, where people's traffic leaves the company, and the browsers and apps it governs never address it at all. F5 puts it in one line: the AI Gateway is an endpoint for the application, and Workforce AI Security is the network path.

The two differ in where traffic comes from. The AI Gateway takes integrated API traffic from internal apps, microservices, coding agents and CI/CD pipelines. Workforce AI Security takes intercepted traffic from people: web browser sessions and user-based agents such as Claude Code, Cursor and assistants in an editor. A coding agent can therefore end up on either side. Claude Code run by Mara on her laptop, signed in to a public service, is user-based and goes through the forward proxy. The same tool in a build pipeline, configured with an API key to call Northlight's gateway endpoint, is an API client of the AI Gateway. The use and the path decide which control point applies, and the product name does not.

They also differ in where traffic may go. The AI Gateway sends requests to sanctioned destinations only: models the company hosts itself, such as qwen3 behind api.northlight.example, and the company's own subscriptions to public providers, with OpenAI, Amazon Bedrock and Azure as F5's examples. Workforce AI Security sees every AI destination people reach, sanctioned enterprise SaaS tools and unsanctioned shadow AI alike. F5's shorter summary is that the AI Gateway governs AI infrastructure a company hosts or buys, and Workforce AI Security governs the use of public AI services.

What each one is for follows from that. The AI Gateway offers token quotas and budgets, dynamic routing between models, prompt injection defence and credential hiding, which keeps provider keys on the gateway and out of applications. Workforce AI Security offers shadow AI discovery, an audit trail, real-time redaction of personal data and intellectual property (data loss prevention, or DLP), policy guardrails and safe AI enablement. The people who buy them differ as well. For the AI Gateway they are application developers, platform engineers and DevSecOps teams, along with the head of enterprise AI, the VP of application development, platform architects and the chief data officer. For Workforce AI Security they are the CISO and security architecture, the SecOps team, corporate IT, HR, and governance, risk and compliance (GRC), with AI enablement leaders. F5's own summary: the AI Gateway governs what we build, and Workforce AI Security governs what we use.

Chapter 4 of 19

A PAC file sorts the traffic on the laptop

AI hosts to the proxy, everything else direct

Northlight wants to see AI traffic without hauling the whole web through a proxy. A proxy auto-configuration (PAC) file does the sorting on the laptop. It is a JavaScript file with one function, FindProxyForURL(url, host). The browser calls it for every URL it is about to open, and the string it returns says where to connect: DIRECT for the site itself, or PROXY with a host and port. A SOCKS entry is also allowed. The file is served with the MIME type application/x-ns-proxy-autoconfig.

The browser gives the function a few helpers. dnsDomainIs(host, ".public-ai.example") is true when the host ends in that domain. shExpMatch matches a shell-style pattern with * and ?. isPlainHostName is true for a name with no dots. isInNet and dnsResolve work on the address a name resolves to, so a rule that uses them makes the browser look the name up before it can decide. For an https URL the browser strips the path and the query before it calls the function, and from Chrome 75 that cannot be turned off. A PAC file can therefore steer by host and by nothing finer. That is why the list of AI services is a list of domains.

F5's PAC setup has five steps. F5 creates a PAC file URL for the company's connector, once. Device management pushes two things to each managed laptop, also once: the PAC URL and F5's root certificate, which a later chapter needs. The browser or app then fetches the list of AI domains on its own. Those domains come from F5's Public Services Catalog, and requests for them go to the proxy and are inspected. Every other URL returns DIRECT, takes its usual route and is never decrypted. F5 gives two reasons. The cost of inspection stays limited to AI traffic, and the company needs no proxy capacity for all of its web traffic. A domain that the catalog does not list yet is not steered, and F5's material says the PAC integration can add one, which it calls addService.

Those two pushed items are everything that changes on the laptop. No program is installed on it. F5 calls this agent-free deployment: no endpoint client is required, and traffic arrives through a SASE platform or a PAC file. Both pieces are settings the operating system already understands. A proxy auto-configuration URL is a standard network setting, and a root certificate is one more entry in the system trust store. Device management tools can push both to a fleet of laptops in one policy. Security products that work through an agent on each endpoint, such as CrowdStrike's AI detection and response product, which F5 compares itself with, need a client installed, updated and supported on every machine. F5 lists not needing a client as its advantage over such products. It lists rich telemetry from the endpoint itself as their advantage. What an agent-free design gives up is shown later in this lesson: a model that runs on the laptop, and traffic that never leaves it, cannot be steered to a proxy.

Chapter 5 of 19

CONNECT, a chained gateway, or a DNS answer

Three other roads that end at the same proxy

A PAC file is one way to steer, and the others end at the same proxy. F5's table of ingress forms says to pick one. With a forward proxy, the browser opens a TCP connection to the proxy and sends the method CONNECT. Its target is only a host and a port, separated by a colon, and the port is never left out because CONNECT has no default port. A proxy must reject an empty or invalid port, usually with 400 Bad Request. It should also refuse ports that have nothing to do with the web. The specification's example is a CONNECT to port 25, which would let anyone relay mail through the proxy. The PAC file's PROXY answer leads to exactly this exchange.

Any answer in the 200 range turns the connection into a tunnel at the end of the answer's headers. Every byte after that comes from the server named in the request, and such an answer carries no Content-Length. Any other answer means there is no tunnel. A proxy that wants the client to log in first answers 407 Proxy Authentication Required with a Proxy-Authenticate challenge, and the client repeats the CONNECT with a Proxy-Authorization header. The tunnel ends when either side closes its connection.

Many companies already send all web traffic through a secure access service edge (SASE) platform. That platform can keep its job and pass only AI traffic on, which is called proxy chaining. It imports F5's list of AI domains as a URL category and applies a Forward-to-Proxy action to it. Other traffic keeps its usual route through the platform. The platform has usually logged the user in already, which the next chapter uses. The third road uses DNS. The company's resolver answers an AI service's name with the proxy's address, the proxy acts as a reverse proxy for that name, and it reads the wanted name from the server_name in the TLS handshake. F5's table also lists DNS over HTTPS as an ingress form, without a method.

F5's table names an example platform for each road. Proxy chaining with a forward proxy and CONNECT is shown with Zscaler and Netskope, two SASE platforms. DNS redirection, or reflection, is shown with Cloudflare, whose DNS filtering policies have an override action that answers a name with an address the company chooses. The PAC road needs no outside platform, since F5 hosts the PAC URL itself. The SASE road has five steps of its own. Users' traffic already flows through the SASE platform from any device. The platform matches the AI domains on F5's list. Its Forward-to-Proxy action sends that traffic to F5. F5 decrypts it, applies policy and logs the session. All other traffic keeps its usual route through the SASE platform and never reaches F5.

The native connectors cover those named platforms. A company that runs some other proxy at its edge uses the Manual Ingress Connector, which F5 describes as a way to integrate more providers. Any proxy that meets the ingress requirements can be connected, and F5 names its own BIG-IP SWG and BIG-IP SSL Orchestrator (SSLO) as examples. SWG is F5's secure web gateway, and SSL Orchestrator decrypts traffic and steers it through a chain of security services. A manual connector uses the same standard forms that native connectors use: a forward proxy that sends CONNECT, or a reverse proxy. It is attributed the same way too, by source address or the X-Sp-Connector-Id header for the tenant and X-Authenticated-User for the person. So a company whose web traffic already leaves through BIG-IP can keep that path and hand only its AI category on to the proxy, exactly as a SASE platform would.

Chapter 6 of 19

Which company, and which person

Attribution comes before any decision

The proxy is a shared service. It takes traffic from many companies, so before it can apply anyone's rules it has to know whose traffic this is. F5's material calls this attribution and gives it two levels: the tenant, meaning the company and its connector, and the user.

The tenant is found in one of two ways. The first is the source address. The company registers dedicated egress addresses or subnets, and traffic from those addresses belongs to it. Northlight's office traffic leaves through 203.0.113.192/28 in this example, so a connection from 203.0.113.197 is Northlight's. The second is a header, X-Sp-Connector-Id, which carries a pre-shared key. A platform in the middle that sends many companies' traffic from the same addresses uses the header, since the address alone cannot tell them apart.

The user comes from a header too. A SASE platform that has already logged the person in passes the name on in X-Authenticated-User, or in a similar identity header. The proxy then knows that this request is from mara@northlight.example and which groups she is in, and later steps can treat engineering and finance differently. F5's session does not show how the person is named when a laptop reaches the proxy straight from a PAC file, so in this lesson Mara's name on that path is an illustrative assumption.

A header is only as trustworthy as the hop that set it. Any program on a laptop can write X-Authenticated-User. The proxy can believe it because it comes from a connector the company configured and that is known by its address or its key. The same reasoning ties this chapter to every record later in the lesson: the audit log can only name people as precisely as attribution did.

Chapter 7 of 19

The proxy becomes one end of the encryption

A certificate made on the spot, signed by a root the laptop trusts

A tunnel only moves bytes. The prompt is inside TLS, encrypted between the browser and the service, and a proxy that relays it learns a hostname and nothing else. To read the prompt the proxy has to be one end of the encryption. So it answers the browser's TLS handshake itself. This is what security teams call TLS inspection, or a man in the middle that the endpoint has agreed to.

The browser's ClientHello names the server it wants in the server_name extension. That field holds a DNS hostname and never a literal address. The proxy reads chat.public-ai.example from it, makes a certificate for that name on the spot and signs it with F5's root, the one device management installed on the laptop. The browser checks the name, the dates and the signature chain, finds the root in its trust store and carries on. The session looks normal to Mara, and the chat page works as it always did.

F5 draws the result as three sessions. TLS 1 is the normal case, one session end to end, with the real certificate from a public certificate authority, trusted through the browser's built-in roots. With inspection there are two. TLS 2 runs from the browser to the proxy with the generated certificate. TLS 3 runs from the proxy to the real service with the real certificate, and the proxy checks that one against its own public root store. Between them the proxy holds the request in plain text. It applies policy there, opens a new outbound request to the service and passes the response back the same way.

The root is the price of all this. Whoever holds its private key can read every session it signs for. That is a strong reason to steer only AI hosts to the proxy and leave banking, health and everything else alone.

Chapter 8 of 19

Who is asking, and may they use this service

The first policy question can end the request

With TLS 2 open, the proxy holds a plain HTTP request. It takes every request through the same six steps in the same order: terminate TLS, identify the user, check access, assess the content, enforce policy and forward. The first two were the last two chapters. The third asks about the service and has nothing to do with the content yet.

Step 3 can answer allow, block or redirect, by service, and it can also look at the license type. That is the kind of account in use: a personal sign-up, or the company's paid plan on the same service. F5 lists this as service and license enforcement, and also as identity-aware access, since the answer can differ by person and group. If the answer is no, nothing else runs. Steps 4 and 5 are skipped, the content is never examined, and the person is blocked or redirected.

A colleague of Mara's in finance gets that outcome. The consumer service is closed to that team, and the browser is sent to the service Northlight pays for. Engineering is allowed, with inspection, so Mara's request goes on to step 4. Redirect is the answer to the problem a block rule creates. The person still gets an AI service, and it is the one whose terms cover the company's data.

Rules can be stacked, and two of them may match the same request. In the current policy version the looser rule always wins. F5's next version, which it calls Policy v2, lets the company choose whether the stricter or the looser rule wins, adds company-wide Override and Default policies, targets groups, services or the whole company, lets any policy be switched on or off, and sets an action per service, tool, assistant, model, data source and kind of sensitive data.

Chapter 9 of 19

What is in the request

Step 4 classifies the data, the intent and the risk

Now the proxy reads. F5 lists five features of intercept for a service in its Public Services Catalog: the request, the response, the license type, file upload, and MCP payloads, which are the tool definitions, tool calls and tool results an agent exchanges with the AI service. The agent chapter later in this lesson shows how those payloads are read. Not every service supports every feature, because each service shapes its traffic differently and the proxy has to understand that shape before it can find the prompt in it. F5's slide shows three catalog entries as an example. Claude, rated medium risk, supports all five. ChatGPT, also medium, and Microsoft Copilot, rated low, support the first four and not MCP. F5's next feature list brings request, response, license, file upload and MCP support to as many catalog services as possible, which it calls getting closer to feature parity.

File upload is the feature with the narrowest reach. For an upload the proxy can allow or block, and that is all. F5's slide says plainly that there is no inspection of the file's content today. A rule can therefore stop every spreadsheet sent to a service, or a group's uploads to a service, but it cannot open a file, find the customer records inside it and mask them the way it masked Mara's key. Text typed or pasted into the prompt is read. The same text attached as a file is only counted as an upload. For data loss prevention that is a real gap, and a company that cares about files decides service by service whether uploads are allowed at all.

Step 4 asks what is in this request, in three parts. Detect intent classifies what the prompt is for, such as debugging, drafting or summarising. Detect sensitive data runs classifiers for personal data and the company's own patterns written as regular expressions. Northlight has one for its API keys: the prefix nlk_live_ followed by 16 hexadecimal characters. F5 also lists custom semantic classifiers, which match on meaning. They find things a fixed pattern cannot describe, such as a draft contract. Content controls look for harmful content, for prompt injection, which is text written to take over a model's instructions, and for source code.

The third part is the risk. The request gets a score of low, medium or high from what was found. Mara's paste scores high. It holds Python source code, which this team is allowed to send, and one string that matches the key pattern on line 7. The intent reads as debugging, which is what she is doing. None of this decides anything yet. Step 4 only describes the request, and step 5 decides.

F5 lists file upload control as its own capability, under Mitigate Risk. Given what the intercept slide says, that control works on the upload as a whole. A rule can stop a spreadsheet of customer records from leaving by blocking uploads to a service, even while typed prompts to the same service are allowed and inspected. It cannot let the spreadsheet through with the personal data taken out.

F5's capability list holds one more detector under Gain Visibility: malicious use detection. Going by its name, it concerns people using an AI service to do harm, which is a different question from the one data rules ask, whether something sensitive is leaving. Prompt injection defence is listed separately, under Mitigate Risk, and so are the content controls for harmful content. F5's session gives the name and its heading and does not describe what the detector looks for or what it does when it fires. Like intent, its findings belong to step 4, which describes a request, while the actions belong to step 5.

Chapter 10 of 19

What policy does with it

The key is masked and the question goes through

Step 5 asks what policy allows for what step 4 found. Each kind of finding has its own action. For sensitive data F5 lists five: monitor, warn, mask, redact and block. Monitor lets the request through and records it. Warn tells the person what was found and lets them decide. Mask replaces the sensitive part with a placeholder and sends the rest. Redact also takes the data out, and F5 lists it as its own action, with transparent data redaction among its capabilities. Block sends nothing and tells the person why. Content controls and tool calls have their own actions, with allow and block lists for tools.

Northlight's rule for engineering lets source code through to this service and masks credentials. The proxy replaces the key on line 7 with a placeholder and leaves the other 47 lines alone. Then step 6 forwards the request. It opens TLS 3 to the real service, checks the real certificate against public roots and sends the edited request inside it. Mara gets her answer about the 401. The key stays inside the company.

The order matters. The mask is applied before the request leaves the proxy, so the service never receives the key at all. It does not depend on the service deleting anything later. The same rule written as block would have stopped all 48 lines and left Mara without an answer. Written as monitor, it would have sent the key and recorded that it did.

The response passes back through the proxy on the same two sessions, and it can be inspected on its way to the browser too. A rule can look at what a service returns as well as at what was sent.

A block leaves a person stuck, and F5 has a capability meant to avoid that, which it lists under Increase Safe Adoption as inline user fallback. Inline means it happens in the flow of the request, at the moment of the block. The person is offered the approved alternative there and then, instead of a dead end, and can carry on with the work in the service whose terms cover the company's data. F5's advice on rollout says the same thing in other words: redirect instead of dead-ending. Its pitch to the teams that run AI programmes says it again: make the approved path the easy path, and redirect risky tools to the ones the company already pays for. The colleague whose customer table was blocked is the case it is for. The table did not go to the consumer service, and the colleague still needs an answer. F5's session does not show the screen the person sees or the exact way the alternative is offered, so this lesson does not draw one.

Chapter 11 of 19

Why the answer still feels fast

The proxy's cost is paid once, before the first word

Mara's worry is speed. The proxy adds work to every request: one more network leg and the time spent reading. Streaming hides most of it. A chat service sends its answer as a series of small events with a few tokens each, and the page shows them as they arrive. The format is called server-sent events and travels as text/event-stream. Each event is one or more lines that start with data:, and a blank line ends the event and hands it to the page.

What a person feels is the wait before the first words, and the proxy's cost lands there once. In this example the direct path shows a first token after 480 ms. Through the proxy it takes 590 ms, which is 70 ms inside the proxy and 40 ms of extra distance. The proxy in this example passes each later event along as it arrives, so tokens come at the service's own pace, 40 a second here, one every 25 ms. An answer of 300 tokens has 299 gaps between its first and last token, 299 × 25 = 7,475 ms on either path. It finishes at 480 + 7,475 = 7,955 ms direct and 590 + 7,475 = 8,065 ms through the proxy, 110 ms later on a total of about 8 seconds.

F5 draws the same picture for distance. A user near the AI service waits and then streams. A user far from it waits longer, and once streaming starts the tokens arrive at the same pace. Distance adds a one-time wait before the first token. That matters because of where the service runs today: in AWS US-East-2, across three availability zones, scaling with load. It is used worldwide from there, and F5 plans regional deployments in Europe and Asia.

Chapter 12 of 19

The record, and what it is used for

One line per request, six dashboards, and a choice about storage

Every request leaves a record: who, which service, which kind of account, what was found and what was done. Mara's line names her, the service, her personal account, source code and one credential, a high risk score and the action mask. The key itself is not in it.

Collected, the records answer questions nobody could answer before. F5's dashboard has six tabs. Public Services shows usage, blocks and sensitive data by service. MCP shows the tools discovered, the most used tools and tool violations. Intent shows what people use AI for, by group and by service. Risk shows the risk level over time and the services and users with the most high-risk requests. Adoption shows the share of the company's directory users who were active in the last 30 days. Private Model shows requests and token use by model, user and group. Any point on a chart opens the transactions behind it. Every tab filters by date range and group, with 30 days as the default range, and a view can be downloaded or emailed on a schedule.

The records can also go to the company's own systems. F5 lists analytics and SIEM telemetry among its capabilities, meaning the security information and event management system where a security team already keeps its logs. Storage is a choice. With zero data retention, a company can decide that prompts, metadata or both are never stored on F5's platform and are sent directly and at once to its SIEM, its business intelligence tools or its own storage.

The same question comes up about where the proxy itself runs. It is a SaaS service today. A fully air-gapped version cannot exist, because the services it governs are on the public internet and it has to reach them. F5 plans a hybrid model sooner, in which the data plane and the control plane run apart, and later a fully on-premises one. A data plane is the part that carries and inspects the traffic, and a control plane is the part that holds the policy and the configuration.

F5 gives that rollout by quarter of its fiscal year 2027 (FY27). Today Workforce AI Security runs as a standalone product, active and deployed for customers in North America, with immediate discovery of employees' AI use, inline outbound DLP for public AI tools and its own standalone architecture. In FY27 Q1 it joins F5's AI Security Platform (AISP), the product that brings AI Gateway, AI Guardrails, AI Red Team and Workforce AI Security under one control plane, as Early Access on North American SaaS. In FY27 Q2 the SaaS service reaches general availability (GA) in all three of F5's regions: North America, EMEA (Europe, the Middle East and Africa) and APCJ (Asia Pacific, China and Japan). That is the regional deployment the streaming chapter mentions. In FY27 Q3 the hybrid model arrives: the data plane runs on the company's premises while the control and management planes stay in F5's SaaS. In FY27 Q4 the fully on-premises version reaches GA, with the data, control and management planes all installed at the company. F5 names regulated sites as the customers it is for, places where the inspection itself has to run on the company's own equipment. A company that adopts standalone Workforce AI Security now moves into AISP, with its unified control plane, when the platform launches.

For Northlight the order matters in a concrete way. Its proxy today runs in US-East-2 and its office is in Europe. The streaming example counted 40 ms of extra distance, and distance of that kind is what a European deployment shortens. From Q2 it can be served from EMEA. From Q3 the inspecting data plane can run in Northlight's own data centre, next to the GPU nodes, with policy still written in the SaaS console.

Chapter 13 of 19

What never reaches the record

A log of AI use that ends at the edge of the steered path

The record has edges, and F5 lists them plainly. The first is the path itself. A personal phone on mobile data has no PAC file and no company root, so its traffic never arrives. The same goes for any device or network that is off the SASE or PAC path, such as a laptop that is not managed. A domain that is not in the catalog yet is not steered either, and its traffic goes out unread until someone adds it. F5 plans automatic service discovery, which watches live traffic for AI services that are not yet in the catalog and creates intercepts for them.

The second edge is certificate pinning. An app that pins certificates trusts only its own short list of roots and ignores the operating system's trust store. When it is steered to the proxy it sees a certificate signed by F5's root, refuses it and fails to connect. The company then has two choices. It can take the host out of the PAC file, and the app works without being inspected. Or it can leave it steered, and the app does not work on managed laptops. Some apps have a setting that lets an administrator change which roots they trust, which ends the dilemma for those apps.

The third edge is traffic with nothing to steer. A model running on the laptop, for example with Ollama, listens on localhost and has no public hostname that a PAC file or a DNS answer could send to the proxy. And when an agent talks straight to a tool server, that leg is outside the path too. Tool servers can run on the laptop or anywhere with hosted compute, and the proxy does not inspect that traffic. A later chapter shows where the line falls for an agent.

The fourth edge lies inside traffic that does arrive. When a file is uploaded to a service, the record can say that an upload happened, to which service, by whom, and whether it was allowed or blocked. It cannot say what was in the file, because the proxy does not inspect upload content today. A spreadsheet of 200 customer names sent as an attachment leaves an allowed upload in the log and no finding of personal data. The same rows pasted into the prompt would have been found and blocked. A company that reads its log for leaks of personal data has to read the allowed uploads as unknowns.

Seeing a request in this log proves it happened. Finding nothing proves much less.

Chapter 14 of 19

An agent's tool calls

Three of the five legs pass the proxy, two never do

Mara's editor runs a coding agent that talks to an AI service in the cloud and uses tools through the Model Context Protocol (MCP). A tool is a function the agent can run, such as reading a file or querying a ticket system, and a tool server is the program that offers it. F5 shows the agent's traffic as five legs, and only some of them go past the proxy.

First the client asks its MCP servers for their tool lists. That leg runs between the laptop and the tool servers and is not inspected. Second, the client sends the prompt to the AI service together with the tool definitions, the names and descriptions of every tool it may call. That leg goes to the cloud and is inspected. Third, the AI service replies with a tool call, a request to run one named tool with given arguments. That is inspected on its way back. Fourth, the client runs the tool on the MCP server, which again is local or remote and not inspected. Fifth, the client sends the tool's result to the AI service, and that is inspected.

F5's diagram labels these legs with numbers from the six-step list, so tool traffic passes through the same pipeline as a prompt: identify, check access and enforce among them. Policy here works with allow and block lists of tools. A tool call to a tool on the block list is blocked. F5's session does not say what the client receives in its place. The MCP tab of the dashboard counts tool violations, and this lesson takes a blocked call to be one, which F5 does not spell out. F5 lists MCP tool discovery among its capabilities too, so the tools people's agents use show up even when nobody has written a rule for them.

The tool result is where data most often leaves. An agent that reads a file full of customer records sends the content to the AI service in leg five. That leg is inspected like a prompt, so a rule for personal data applies to it as it applied to Mara's paste.

Chapter 15 of 19

When the proxy does not answer

The PAC file's last word decides between failing open and failing closed

Every steered request depends on the proxy being reachable. A PAC function can return more than one entry, separated by semicolons and tried left to right. MDN gives an example with two proxies and then DIRECT. The browser uses the first entry until it fails to connect to it, then moves on to the next.

The last entry decides what an outage does. If it is DIRECT, AI traffic goes straight to the services while the proxy is down. Work goes on, and nothing is inspected or recorded for that time. This is failing open. If there is no DIRECT, the AI services stop working until the proxy is back, which is failing closed. MDN describes how Firefox handles that second case: when every proxy is down and the list has no DIRECT, the browser asks the user whether to ignore the proxies for now and connect directly. After 20 minutes it asks whether to try the proxies again, then again after 40 more, each gap 20 minutes longer than the one before.

A proxy that did not answer is not tried again at once. MDN gives the times for Firefox: the first retry is after 30 minutes, the next after an hour, and each later gap is 30 minutes longer. Those times are long. A short outage can therefore leave a laptop sending AI traffic directly for half an hour after the proxy has recovered, and none of that traffic is in the record.

F5 makes an outage less likely on its side, with the service spread over three availability zones and scaling with load. The choice of what to write at the end of the PAC string is still the company's. It is a choice between keeping people working and keeping every request inspected.

Chapter 16 of 19

Seven gates before a trial

One failed gate is a conversation, two or more end it

Everything so far rests on conditions that a company either meets or does not. F5's specialists check them before a trial starts, and call the result a qualified proof of concept (POC), a trial that has a real chance of ending in a purchase. Their summary is one sentence: we win where AI traffic is reachable and attributable. The check has seven gates, each a question with a yes or no answer.

The first three gates are the mechanism of this lesson in question form. Interception path asks whether the proxy can get into the traffic path at all, with a PAC file pushed by device management (MDM, mobile device management) or by chaining from a SASE platform. TLS trust asks whether the company can distribute F5's root certificate to its devices. Without it every inspected session fails with a certificate error, as the TLS chapter showed. Identity asks whether there is single sign-on (SSO), ideally with SCIM, so requests can be attributed to users and groups. SCIM, the System for Cross-domain Identity Management, is the protocol in RFC 7644 by which a directory pushes its users and groups to a service as /Users and /Groups resources. SSO names the person who signs in. SCIM tells the service which groups that person is in, and who the directory's users are at all, before anyone has sent a request. Policy by group, such as the engineering and finance rules in this lesson, needs that.

The next three gates ask whether the product fits what the company has. Coverage asks whether the proxy intercepts the tools the company's people actually use, or only controls access to them. A service the proxy intercepts gets its content inspected. A service it can only control access to can be allowed or blocked by name, and what people send to it is not read. Enforcement depth asks whether what the company expects matches what ships, such as file content inspection, which does not ship today. Deployment asks whether the company can use a multi-tenant SaaS service, which is how the proxy runs until the hybrid and on-premises versions arrive. The last gate is about people. Stakeholders asks whether there is a budget owner, an IT or network owner who can push the PAC file and the root, and a compelling event, a reason to act now.

The scoring rule is short. One failed gate is a conversation: the gap is discussed and may be worked around. Two or more failed gates disqualify the trial. Northlight passes six. Its laptops are managed, MDM can push the PAC URL and the root, SSO with SCIM is in place, its people use catalog services, the SaaS model is acceptable, and its security lead owns the budget and its network team owns the PAC file. It fails one: its compliance team expects uploaded files to be scanned for customer data. That is a conversation about file upload being allow or block only today, so the rule for uploads to unapproved services becomes block. A second company whose staff work from personal laptops with no MDM and no SASE platform fails both the interception path and TLS trust, which is two failures, and the trial does not start. When a trial does go ahead, the win F5 describes is plain: the company sees its AI usage where it could not before.

Chapter 17 of 19

Start by watching

Monitor first, then turn findings into rules

A company that turns on blocking on the first day learns what it broke from the help desk. F5 recommends the opposite order, and its material on running a trial lays it out in six steps. Two are about the business: finding the pain, such as shadow AI, data leaking out, compliance pressure or AI adoption that security has stalled, and in the end tying the results to agreed criteria. The middle four are the technical part.

Scoping, the second step, comes first among them: who takes part, what the goals are, which documents are needed and how success is measured. Then comes the third step, monitor only. Traffic is steered to the proxy and nothing is enforced. Every rule records and none of them acts, so users see no change at all. F5 lists monitor-first rollout among its capabilities for this reason.

The fourth step shows the usage. After a few weeks of monitoring, the dashboards answer which AI services are in use, by whom, and what for. That usually includes services nobody approved and intents nobody expected. The fifth step builds policy from those findings and enforces it across the company in stages, team by team, so that a mistake in one rule reaches a few people first.

F5 gives 30 to 60 days as the typical cycle from the start of the trial to the purchase, so the monitoring and the staged enforcement both fit inside that. This chapter looks back at how Northlight reached the rules the earlier chapters showed working. Its first month was records with the action monitor, showing that credentials left in pastes several times a week. Only then did the credential rule move from monitor to mask, first for engineering. That is the rule that masked the key in Mara's paste. Warn is a useful stage in between: the person sees what was found and decides, which teaches people about the rule before it starts to change what they send.

Chapter 18 of 19

What each team must see, and what each one asks

Four roles, four proofs and four objections

A trial is judged by several teams at once, and each one looks for something different. F5's material names four roles. Security is the CISO and the security team. GRC is compliance, privacy and risk. IT/Ops is IT, network and infrastructure. AI enablement is the CIO, the chief AI officer (CAIO), the AI centre of excellence (AI CoE) and the executive leadership team (ELT). For each, F5 gives the question that role asks, what to lead with, and what the role needs to see in its own environment before the trial counts as a success.

Security asks what data is going into ChatGPT, F5's example of a public chat service, and is shown how risk is mitigated. It needs to see one enforcement working on its own traffic: a redaction, or an agent's write tools stripped. At Northlight the redaction is Mara's key masked on line 7. The agent case is the post_comment call from the agent chapter. A write tool is one that changes something, such as posting a comment or deleting a file, while read_file and search_tickets only read. Allowing the read tools and blocking the write tools lets an agent investigate and stops it from acting. F5's word is stripped. Its session does not say whether the write tools are removed from the definitions in leg two or their calls blocked in leg three, and this lesson showed the call blocked. GRC asks how to prove the policy is followed, and is shown risk mitigation and then the audit trail. It needs one user's AI activity from start to finish that it could hand to an auditor. IT/Ops asks what breaks if something is blocked, and is shown visibility first. It needs to see that nothing breaks: no login prompts and no broken apps. AI enablement asks whether people use the AI the company pays for, and is shown safe adoption. It needs its own adoption numbers: which AI tools and which licences are actually used.

The same teams raise objections, and F5 gives a short answer to each. The first objection is friction, the fear that users' work will break or slow down. F5's answer is that, deployed right, users never notice. F5 says there are no login prompts. Its session does not say how that is achieved on every road. On a chained road the platform in front has already named the person in a header, and how the person is named on the PAC road is not described. F5 also says that if the proxy goes down, traffic flows as normal. It does not say how. In this lesson's PAC setup that comes from the PAC list ending in DIRECT, as the outage chapter showed, and that chapter also showed the catch: traffic that goes DIRECT is not inspected, and Firefox does not try the proxy again until 30 minutes after it failed, so it keeps going DIRECT that long even when the proxy is back sooner. The rollout is in steps: monitor mode first, enforcement one team at a time, and redirect instead of dead-ending. The second objection is about the F5 acquisition and whether the product will still be delivered. F5's material phrases the question that way and does not name the company it came from. F5's answer is that the product and its roadmap continue, that it is now part of F5's AI Security Platform, and that F5's support stands behind it. The third objection is coverage, whether the proxy will see a particular tool. Here F5's coaching note to its own staff is to state the limits upfront: local models, tool servers, pinned apps, devices off the path and file contents. The fourth is fit with what the company already runs, and why its existing vendor cannot do this. The answer is that the proxy layers on. It is chained from the existing SASE platform or steered with a PAC file, and replaces neither.

Chapter 19 of 19

A loop, not a project

Six steps with an owner each, started again at every new stage of AI use

The trial ends, and the work does not. F5's material draws governance of AI use as a loop of six steps and says so in its title: closing the gap is a loop, not a project. Discover is the first step: what AI is in use, by whom, and with what data. Decide is the second: the policy, the approved tools and the risk tiers, all of which F5 calls the customer's call. Approved path is the third: sanctioned tools are put in place before any broad blocking, so that people have somewhere to go. Enforce in steps is the fourth, in the order warn, then redirect, then redact, then block. Prove is the fifth: audit, investigations and reporting. Optimize is the sixth: adoption, licences and usage. Then it starts again at Discover.

Each step has an owner, and F5 adds that every owner is a buyer. Discover belongs to Security and IT/Ops. Decide belongs to AI enablement and GRC. Approved path belongs to IT/Ops and AI enablement. Enforce in steps belongs to Security and IT/Ops. Prove belongs to Security and GRC. Optimize belongs to AI enablement. The roles are the four from the last chapter, so each team's proof during the trial is also its step in the loop afterwards.

The loop maps onto this lesson's mechanisms. Discover is the dashboards and shadow AI discovery. Decide is writing the rules for step 3 and step 5 of the pipeline. Approved path is the sanctioned service that redirect and inline fallback send people to, which has to exist before a block makes sense. Enforce in steps is the order of actions, and it fits with the trial's monitor, warn and mask: each rule climbs from watching to acting one stage at a time. Prove is the audit trail and the SIEM feed. Optimize is the Adoption tab and the licence counts.

The loop restarts for each new stage of AI use, the stages from the second chapter. Northlight governs individual use first. When its people take up integrated use, with AI inside approved apps and assistants connected to tools, Discover runs again for those, and so on for specialized use and agentic AI. Each stage brings new services, new tools and new data paths, and the earlier rules do not cover them until the loop has passed over them.

What to keep

One point in the path, and an honest idea of its reach

The whole design is one decision made twice. On the laptop, a small function, or a SASE platform, or a DNS answer decides which connections go to the proxy. At the proxy, policy decides what happens to each request: first whose traffic it is, then whether this person may use this service, then what to do about what is in it. The last of those rests on the root certificate, since the proxy can only decide about what it can read. Mara's paste went from invisible to a request with a masked key and a line in a log, at a cost of 110 ms on an answer of about 8 seconds. Her agent's tool results are checked the same way. The same engineer on a personal phone, an app that pins its certificates, a model on the laptop and a tool server it talks to are as invisible as before. A proxy that does not answer can make everything invisible while it is down, and in Firefox for up to half an hour after it failed, even if it recovers sooner. The gaps are closed with a sanctioned service good enough that people have no reason to go around it, and a rollout that watches before it blocks. Whether any of this can start is decided by seven gates, of which the first three are the mechanism itself: a path to the proxy, a trusted root and named users in groups. After the trial the same steps run as a loop, discover, decide, approved path, enforce in steps, prove and optimize, once for each new stage of AI use the company reaches.
