Running a GPU cloud

Start from the workload, not the GPU

Twelve questions in a fixed order, and the GPU is answered by the other eleven

Most enterprise AI infrastructure projects open with one question: which GPU should we buy. It sounds like the most concrete place to start, and it is the one most likely to produce an expensive mistake. Training a model, fine-tuning it, answering employees in real time, searching company documents and running agents all load a server in different ways. They need different amounts of GPU memory, different networks, different storage and different controls. A card chosen before those needs are written down is a guess. This lesson walks a planning sequence that puts the GPU near the end. Four requirements come first: the workload, the data, the control the organization needs, and the service model it will offer. Then the architecture follows in order: compute, network, storage, platform, operations, governance and economics. The twelve checklist items that carry the sequence are taken one at a time, each turned into real numbers for Northlight's Qwen3-32B model running on vLLM. By the end the GPU choice falls out of the arithmetic, and so does the server that was missing.

Chapter 1 of 16

The first question is usually the wrong one

Twelve items in four groups, asked in an order that ends with the hardware

A GPU is one part of a system that also moves data, stores it, schedules work, watches itself, enforces rules and costs money every hour it runs. Choosing the card first fixes one corner of that system and forces every other part to fit around it. If the card has too little memory for the conversations the business needs, the answer is more cards, and more cards need more power, more network ports and more operations. If it has more than the workload needs, the money is spent on capacity that sits idle.

The safer order is short. Write down the workload, the data and the control requirements, then design the architecture, then pick the GPU and the compute around it. The requirements half has four parts: workload, data, control and service model. The architecture half has seven, in this order: compute, network, storage, platform, operations, governance and economics. The GPU choice sits inside compute, after the requirements have set its memory size, its count and the way the cards must talk to each other.

The planning checklist that carries this order has twelve items in four groups. Group A, requirements: 1 workload, 2 data and models, 3 deployment and service model. Group B, infrastructure: 4 GPU and compute, 5 network, 6 storage. Group C, platform and operations: 7 AI platform and runtime, 8 resource operations, 9 observability, reliability and lifecycle. Group D, control and business: 10 security, access and governance, 11 capacity and scalability, 12 economics and operating responsibility.

Northlight is the example throughout. It runs Qwen3-32B with vLLM on two GPU nodes, gpu-node-1 and gpu-node-2, four H100 cards each. On this scene they are gpu-srv-1 and gpu-srv-2. The third server, gpu-srv-3, is empty: whether it should be bought is one of the things this checklist will decide. The lesson Private, public, on-prem or hybrid: where each workload runs uses the same order to decide where a workload goes. This lesson stays inside one environment and works each item down to numbers.

Chapter 2 of 16

Item 1, workload: what runs, and how many at once

Training, fine-tuning, inference, RAG, agents and HPC press on different parts of a server

The first item asks for the workload types and the concurrency each one needs. The types listed are training, fine-tuning, inference, RAG, agents and HPC, and each presses on a different part of the system. Pre-training and full fine-tuning hold weights, gradients and optimizer state on many GPUs for days and exchange gradients between them on every step, so they need GPU memory and a fast network between GPUs. Real-time inference holds one copy of the weights per replica and spends the rest of the card on the working memory of the conversations it serves. RAG, retrieval-augmented generation, adds a search step that pastes retrieved passages into the prompt, so prompts grow long and the reading phase, prefill, gets heavy. Agents call the model again and again with a growing context and call tools in between. The owner's distributed inference deck states it directly: multi-agent workloads are prefill-heavy. HPC, scientific simulation on the same GPUs, often needs double precision arithmetic and batch scheduling that language models rarely use.

Concurrency is the number that makes a workload concrete. It is how many requests the system must hold in flight at the same moment, and for language models it decides GPU memory more than anything else does. A model server keeps a KV cache for every conversation in its batch, and that cache grows with every token of context.

Northlight writes down three workloads. The employee assistant serves 2,000 staff, and at the busiest minute 6% of them, 120 people, have a conversation in progress, averaging 4,000 tokens of context each. The contract search is a RAG service: 16 questions in flight at peak, each carrying about 12,000 tokens of retrieved passages plus a 500-token answer. The quarterly fine-tune adapts the model to Northlight's documents. The first two are inference and run all day. The third is a training job that runs for days and then stops. The next chapter turns the first two into gigabytes. The lessons Prefill and decode and Three numbers on a GPU card explain the two phases of inference and the card's three numbers.

Chapter 3 of 16

Concurrency turns into gigabytes

262,144 bytes for every token of context, held for as long as the request runs

Qwen3-32B's configuration file gives the four numbers that size its KV cache: 64 layers, 8 key-value heads, 128 values per head, and bfloat16 as its data type, 2 bytes per value. For each token of context the model keeps a key and a value in every layer: 2 × 64 × 8 × 128 × 2 bytes = 262,144 bytes. The model asks for 64 query heads, but grouped-query attention shares each key-value head among eight of them, so the cache is eight times smaller than it would be with 64 KV heads. vLLM stores the cache in the model's own data type unless --kv-cache-dtype says otherwise. The FP8 build keeps bfloat16 as its working type, so its cache is also 262,144 bytes a token.

One H100 has 80 GB. vLLM takes the share set by --gpu-memory-utilization. Northlight sets it to 0.9, which was the default up to the vLLM v0.10 releases. Current documentation gives 0.92, or 1.0 with the extensible KV cache, so the value is written down instead of left to the version. At 0.9 the share is 72 GB. The Qwen3-32B-FP8 weights take 34.3 GB of it, leaving 37.7 GB for the cache and the working values of each pass. At 262,144 bytes a token that is at most 143,814 tokens before the working values are counted.

Now the workloads. A 4,000-token conversation holds 1.05 GB. The assistant's 120 conversations hold 125.8 GB, which is 3.34 cards' worth of free memory, so four replicas. A contract question of 12,500 tokens holds 3.28 GB, and 16 of them hold 52.4 GB, so two replicas. Six H100 cards serve the two inference workloads at peak, each holding one copy of the weights.

The model's own limit matters too. Its max_position_embeddings is 40,960 tokens. One conversation at that length holds 10.74 GB, so a single card can hold only three of them at full length. Setting --kv-cache-dtype fp8 halves the cost to 131,072 bytes a token and doubles the room to about 287,600 tokens, at some cost in accuracy that has to be measured. The owner's deck shows what happens when concurrency is under-sized: eight Qwen3-32B FP8 Pods on vLLM, 200 concurrent prompts of 20,000 tokens, a load that exceeds the fleet's KV cache by 24%. Requests queue or caches get thrown out, and time to first token climbs.

Chapter 4 of 16

Item 2, data and models: size, sensitivity, retention and growth

Weights, checkpoints, embeddings and documents, and how long each must be kept

The second item asks what the data and models are: dataset size, sensitivity, model artifacts, checkpoints, embeddings, retention and growth. Each is a different kind of object with its own size and its own rules.

Model artifacts first. The Qwen3-32B-FP8 build is 34.3 GB of files and the full-precision BF16 build is 65.5 GB. Keeping both, so the team can compare quality, takes 99.8 GB. Every fine-tuned version adds another copy. A quarterly fine-tune that produces a BF16 model and an FP8 build adds 99.8 GB a quarter, 399.2 GB a year if all are kept.

Checkpoints are larger. Full fine-tuning with mixed precision and the AdamW optimizer keeps 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for the bfloat16 weights, 2 for the gradients, 4 for an fp32 master copy and 8 for the optimizer's two running averages. For 32,762,123,264 parameters that is 524.2 GB. A checkpoint that can resume training saves the bfloat16 weights, the fp32 master copy and the optimizer state, 14 bytes per parameter. It leaves out the gradients, because the next step computes them again. That is 458.7 GB each time it is written, or 393.1 GB for a framework that rebuilds the bfloat16 weights from the master copy. Keeping the last three 458.7 GB checkpoints during a run takes about 1.38 TB.

Embeddings are the vectors that make the contract search work. Northlight splits its contracts into 10 million chunks and embeds each one as 1,024 numbers. Stored as 4-byte floats in pgvector, which adds 8 bytes per vector, each takes 4,104 bytes, so the table is 41.04 GB before any index. The source documents themselves sit in a document store of 200 TB, the same store the lesson Private, public, on-prem or hybrid: where each workload runs uses to argue for bringing the model to the data.

Sensitivity and retention decide where each object may live and for how long. Contracts are confidential. Their chunks and embeddings are derived from them, so they get the same handling. Training checkpoints can be deleted when the run is accepted. The final models are kept for as long as anything depends on them. Growth is the last line: the contract store adds documents every week, and the embedding table grows with it.

Chapter 5 of 16

Item 3, deployment and service model

Eight possible answers, and each one adds parts to the architecture

The third item asks what kind of environment this is. The checklist names eight answers: public cloud, private, on-premises, hosted, hybrid, air-gapped, internal shared infrastructure, or customer-facing cloud. They are not exclusive. Northlight's environment is private, on premises and internal shared infrastructure at once. The lesson Private, public, on-prem or hybrid: where each workload runs separates the words. Here the point is what each answer adds to the design downstream.

An air-gapped answer removes every automated connection to the outside. The model servers can no longer download Qwen3-32B-FP8 from a public hub when they start. The site needs internal registries for container images, packages and model weights, and a transfer station where new files are scanned, checked against their signatures and carried in by hand. On this scene those are the internal registries and the transfer station. The Hugging Face client has a switch for this, HF_HUB_OFFLINE: with it set, no HTTP calls are made and only cached files are read, so a missing file fails loudly instead of reaching out. The lesson A cloud with no way out follows such a site in full.

A customer-facing answer adds a whole layer. Internal infrastructure serves the organization's own users under its own governance. A customer-facing cloud service also needs a product catalogue, separate tenants, self-service, metering, billing and a commercial lifecycle with contracts and support. Good infrastructure does not turn into a cloud business by itself. The lessons What turns GPU servers into a GPU cloud and From GPUs to a cloud business cover that layer.

A hybrid answer adds the joint between environments: a gateway that sends each request to the right place, and identities and logs that work across both. A hosted answer moves the building and the power to someone else while the hardware stays dedicated. Northlight's answer for its two inference workloads is internal, private and on premises, connected to the internet for downloads. For the fine-tune the answer is still open, and capacity will decide it.

Chapter 6 of 16

Item 4, GPU and compute: memory decides the card

The same model fits one card in FP8, barely fits in BF16, and needs eight to fine-tune

With the first three items answered, compute can be chosen from requirements instead of brand popularity. The checklist names four things to choose: accelerator memory, node shape, CPU and RAM balance, and interconnect. Memory comes first, because it says yes or no.

NVIDIA lists the H100 SXM with 80 GB at 3.35 TB/s and the H200 with 141 GB at 4.8 TB/s. Both are rated up to 700 W. With Qwen3-32B-FP8, one H100 keeps 37.7 GB for conversations, as the last chapter worked out. The BF16 build is a different story. Its 65.5 GB leave 6.5 GB of the 72 GB budget, room for about 24,796 tokens, less than one conversation at the model's 40,960-token limit. vLLM refuses to start until --max-model-len is lowered or a second card shares the model. So the build decides the card count before any traffic arrives: one H100 per replica in FP8. In BF16 it is two per replica with --tensor-parallel-size 2 to keep the full 40,960-token context, or one per replica with --max-model-len cut below 24,796 tokens, which leaves room for only a single conversation of that length.

The H200 changes the arithmetic. At 0.9 its budget is 126.9 GB, and after the FP8 weights 92.6 GB are left, about 353,241 tokens, 2.46 times the room of an H100. The assistant's 125.8 GB of cache fits on two H200 instead of four H100, and the contract search's 52.4 GB fits on one. Three H200 do the work of six H100 for these two workloads, with faster memory for decode as well. Whether that is cheaper depends on the prices, which is item 12's job.

The fine-tune needs a different shape altogether. Its 524.2 GB of training state is 6.55 cards of H100 memory before any activations, so at least seven cards, and in practice all eight of Northlight's, with the state sharded across them. That is the first collision the checklist finds: the two inference workloads need six cards all day, and the fine-tune needs eight for days. Item 4 has done its job by sizing each workload and exposing the collision. The shortage itself is a capacity gap, and item 11 records it. The lessons Every weight moves a little and A training run at scale take the training memory apart in detail.

Chapter 7 of 16

Node shape, CPU and RAM, and the links between cards

Four or eight GPUs a box, and why tensor parallel stays inside one

Node shape is how many GPUs share one server, and what surrounds them. NVIDIA sells HGX H100 boards with 4 or 8 GPUs. Northlight's two nodes have four each. That number limits every job that must run inside one box. A Kubernetes Pod lives on one node, so a model server that needs four cards must find four free on the same server.

Inside a box, the cards talk over NVLink. NVIDIA lists 900 GB/s of NVLink per H100 SXM, counted across both directions, against 128 GB/s for PCIe Gen5. That gap is why tensor parallelism, which splits each layer's matrices across cards and exchanges partial results inside every layer, stays inside one NVLink domain. Pipeline parallelism splits the model by ranges of layers and sends only the activations between stages, which a slower network between boxes can carry. The lesson A model bigger than one GPU shows both.

CPU and RAM have jobs of their own. The CPU tokenizes text, runs the HTTP server, schedules each batch and, for training, loads and shuffles data. The host RAM holds the weights while they are read from disk and copied to the cards, and it is where vLLM puts anything moved off the GPU. Its --cpu-offload-gb option treats host memory as extra GPU memory, at the price of copying those weights across PCIe on every pass. vLLM's container also needs shared memory: its Docker instructions run it with --ipc=host, or a larger --shm-size, because PyTorch uses shared memory between processes for tensor parallel inference.

For scale, the public clouds publish their shapes. Amazon's p5.48xlarge has 8 H100, 192 vCPUs and 2 TiB of memory. Oracle's BM.GPU.H100.8 has 8 H100, 112 OCPUs, 2,048 GB of memory and 16 local NVMe drives of 3.84 TB. Both give each GPU roughly 256 GB of host memory. Northlight's choice of 4-GPU nodes suits single-card replicas well. It is the worst fit for the fine-tune, which must span both boxes and so leave NVLink for the network on every step.

Chapter 8 of 16

Item 5, network: three kinds of traffic

Small requests, large weights from storage, and gradients between GPUs

The fifth item asks for bandwidth, topology, storage traffic and service connectivity designed for the actual workload. A GPU environment carries three kinds of traffic, and they differ by orders of magnitude.

Requests and answers are the smallest. A 4,000-token conversation is a few kilobytes of text going in, and an answer streams back a few bytes per token. Service connectivity matters more than bandwidth here: a stable address such as api.northlight.example at 203.0.113.80, TLS, identity checks, and a gateway that picks the right model server. The lessons on the factory scene follow that path.

Storage traffic is large and bursty. When the assistant scales out, four new replicas each read 34.3 GB of weights at once, 137.2 GB in total. Over one 25 Gb/s storage link that is 43.9 seconds of transfer before any replica can begin loading. Over 100 Gb/s it is 11 seconds. Checkpoints in a training run are larger still, as the next chapter shows. Storage traffic should be planned for its peaks, because that is when a replica is late or a training step waits.

GPU-to-GPU traffic is the largest, and only training has much of it. NCCL's performance notes give the best-case time to all-reduce S bytes across n ranks, each with bandwidth B: t = (S / B) × 2(n - 1) / n. Qwen3-32B's gradients in bfloat16 are 65.5 GB. Across eight GPUs at 400 Gb/s each, 50 GB/s, one all-reduce takes at least 2.29 seconds. At 100 Gb/s per GPU it takes 9.17 seconds. Inside one 4-GPU box over NVLink, at 450 GB/s in each direction, it would take 0.22 seconds. This is why training clusters give every GPU its own network port, as Oracle's shape does with 2 × 200 Gb/s of RDMA per GPU, and why the lesson The network is part of the computer treats the fabric as part of the machine.

Northlight's nodes have one 25 Gb/s port each. That is plenty for the two inference workloads and slow for loading weights in a hurry. For the fine-tune across two boxes it is the bottleneck.

Chapter 9 of 16

Item 6, storage: capacity, throughput, IOPS and checkpoints

How much, how fast, how many small reads, and what must survive a lost disk

The sixth item asks for capacity, throughput, IOPS, model storage, datasets, checkpoints and backup. These are separate numbers and a storage system can be good at one and poor at another.

Capacity is the sum of the inventory from item 2 plus growth. The two model builds take 99.8 GB, a year of quarterly fine-tuned models 399.2 GB, three checkpoints during a run 1.38 TB and the embedding table 41.04 GB before its index. The 200 TB contract store is the largest by far and has its own system.

Throughput is bytes a second for large sequential reads and writes. Model loading is a throughput job, and so is checkpointing. A checkpoint of about 458.7 GB written at 2 GB/s takes 229 seconds. Written every hour during a fine-tune, with training paused while it writes, that is 6.4% of every hour spent saving. At 10 GB/s it is 45.9 seconds, 1.3% of the hour.

IOPS, input and output operations a second, counts small random reads. The vector search behind the contract search reads small pieces of an index in no fixed order, and a training dataset made of many small files does the same. A system tuned for large sequential throughput can still answer small random reads slowly.

The owner's distributed inference deck draws AI data delivery as four S3 stages: ingestion uploads large objects, training data retrieval lists and downloads, model storage uploads trained models, and model retrieval downloads them to the servers. All four are S3 traffic with TLS, and the first two carry the heaviest performance demands. The same slide lists what the storage path must plan for: throughput, concurrency and latency targets, connection reuse, multipart upload and large-object streaming, the cost of TLS, and tenant quotas and rate limits so one team's burst does not starve another.

Backup follows the retention rules. Public weights can be downloaded again on a connected site, though not on an air-gapped one. Fine-tuned models and the embeddings cost GPU days to rebuild, so they are backed up. Checkpoints are kept only until the run is accepted.

Chapter 10 of 16

Item 7, AI platform and runtime: Kubernetes is optional

Drivers, runtimes, containers, orchestration, schedulers, model serving and model operations

The seventh item lists the software between the hardware and the model: drivers, runtimes, containers, orchestration, schedulers, model serving, and the operations around models and data. Each layer depends on the one below.

At the bottom is the NVIDIA driver, the kernel module that owns each card. Above it the CUDA libraries, which the model server's container brings with it. A container runtime with NVIDIA's container toolkit hands the cards to a container. On Kubernetes, a device plugin on each node announces the cards as the extended resource nvidia.com/gpu, and the scheduler places Pods that request it. The model server is vLLM, from the image vllm/vllm-openai, which serves an OpenAI-compatible API on port 8000. Model operations sit on top: which model versions exist, where their files are, which one each Deployment serves, and how a new fine-tuned version is promoted.

Kubernetes is not mandatory. vLLM's own documentation starts a server with one Docker command on one host: docker run --runtime nvidia --gpus all, a volume for the Hugging Face cache, -p 8000:8000, --ipc=host and the image with --model. One server running one model for one team can stay that simple. Training teams often use a batch scheduler such as Slurm instead. Kubernetes earns its place when there are many models, many teams, rolling updates, scaling on load and a gateway that picks the replica. Northlight runs Kubernetes because it has two workloads with different replica counts, a third that comes and goes, and the gateway and picker that the lessons From a Gateway to a Pod and Which GPU answers soonest describe.

Every layer carries a version, and the versions must agree. A driver too old for the CUDA version in the image stops the server at start. An upgrade of one layer is a change to all the layers above it, which is why item 9 asks for a lifecycle plan. The lesson A Pod asks for a GPU follows the request for a card through these layers.

Chapter 11 of 16

Item 8, resource operations: quotas, queues and placement

Who may hold which cards, who waits, and where a job may land

The eighth item covers provisioning, scheduling, queues, quotas, reservations and placement. These decide how the cards are shared once the hardware exists.

A quota caps how much one team may hold. Kubernetes supports quotas on extended resources by prefixing the name with requests., so a ResourceQuota in the namespace inference with requests.nvidia.com/gpu: 6 lets that namespace request at most six cards in total. A seventh Pod asking for a card is refused at creation by the API server, before the scheduler sees it. The data science team gets its own namespace and its own quota.

A queue holds work that cannot start yet. Inference cannot wait: its replicas run all the time. Training can, and it is the right place to queue. A job that needs eight cards waits until eight are free, instead of starting on three and holding them. The lesson Why a job waits while GPUs are free shows why a job can wait while cards sit idle, and how queueing systems for Kubernetes admit whole jobs at once.

A reservation sets capacity aside ahead of time. Northlight's quarterly fine-tune needs eight cards for several days, and its two inference workloads need six all day. With eight cards both cannot run. Either serving shrinks during the fine-tune, or the fine-tune runs elsewhere, for example in a reserved block of rented GPUs, as the lesson Private, public, on-prem or hybrid: where each workload runs describes.

Placement decides where a job lands within the cluster. A Pod lives on one node, so a request for four cards must find four free on the same node. If each node has two cards free, the cluster has four free and the Pod still cannot run. That is fragmentation. With single-card replicas it rarely bites. With the fine-tune's two 4-card halves it decides whether the job can start at all.

Chapter 12 of 16

Item 9, observability, reliability and lifecycle

Watch compute, network, storage, jobs and capacity, and plan the failure, the clean-up and the upgrade

The ninth item asks for monitoring of compute, network, storage, jobs and capacity, and a design for failure recovery, clean-up, upgrade and backup. It is the item most often left until after go-live, when it costs the most to add.

On each card, NVIDIA's DCGM exporter publishes metrics for Prometheus. In its 4.x releases the default set includes DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL, the share of time the card was busy, DCGM_FI_DEV_FB_USED, the memory in use in MiB, DCGM_FI_DEV_POWER_USAGE in watts, and DCGM_FI_DEV_XID_ERRORS, the code of the last Xid error the driver reported. An Xid is the driver's numbered report of a fault, and a non-zero value is the first sign that a card needs attention. The lesson Measure, don't assume reads those codes.

The model server tells the rest. vLLM's vllm:num_requests_running is the batch, vllm:num_requests_waiting the queue, and vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc the share of cache blocks in use. On a card serving Qwen3-32B-FP8, DCGM reports memory nearly full from the moment the server starts, because vLLM reserves its whole budget for cache at start. Card memory therefore says nothing about load. The cache usage and the queue do.

Reliability is what happens when a part fails. If gpu-node-2 stops, its replicas are recreated on gpu-node-1 only if gpu-node-1 has free cards, and each new replica needs time to load before it is Ready, about three minutes in this lesson's illustrative figure. Until then the remaining replicas carry the load. The lesson A node goes silent follows that timeline.

Lifecycle covers every planned change. A driver upgrade drains a node with kubectl drain gpu-node-2 --ignore-daemonsets, which evicts its Pods within the limits of their PodDisruptionBudgets, upgrades, reboots and returns it with kubectl uncordon. Clean-up removes old model versions, old checkpoints and finished jobs. Backup copies what item 6 marked as expensive to rebuild. Every one of these steps removes capacity for a while, which item 11 has to plan for.

Chapter 13 of 16

Item 10, security, access and governance

Identity, least privilege, boundaries, model and data access, keys, audit and artifact approval

The tenth item lists identity, least privilege, network boundaries, data and model access, keys, audit and artifact approval. Each one is a control the architecture has to make room for.

Identity means every person and every program that touches the platform has a name the system can check, from a single directory. Least privilege means each one holds only the rights its job needs. The fine-tune's service account may read the contract chunks and write checkpoints. The assistant's model server may read model weights and nothing in the contract store.

Network boundaries keep traffic to the paths the design allows. Model servers accept requests only from the gateway. Training nodes reach storage and each other, and nothing else. On an air-gapped site the transfer station is the only way in.

Data and model access is a separate question from network reach. A fine-tuned model trained on contracts carries some of what it read, so its files are as sensitive as the contracts. Keys encrypt data at rest and in transit, and someone has to own them, rotate them and decide who may use them.

Audit keeps a record of who did what: who downloaded a model, who changed a quota, who started a fine-tune on which data. Artifact approval decides what may run at all. A container image is allowed only if it was signed by a trusted builder. Model weights are allowed only if their checksums match the ones published with them, and only after someone has approved that model for that use. The lessons A policy says no and Who may talk to whom show two of these controls at work.

The checklist points to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, released on January 26, 2023 for voluntary use. Its core has four functions: govern, map, measure and manage. Govern is designed to run across the other three, and NIST says risk management should run through the whole lifecycle of an AI system. It also says its actions do not constitute a checklist. Trustworthy AI comes from a process kept up for the system's whole life, and no single technical control delivers it. Governance can be added later. Designing it in early costs less rework.

Chapter 14 of 16

Item 11, capacity: headroom, failure, lead time and new generations

Size for the day the hardware arrives, with one node out and one card broken

The eleventh item asks for current demand, growth, maintenance headroom, failure capacity, expansion lead time and new infrastructure generations. It is where the earlier items meet.

Current demand from chapter 3 is six H100 cards for the two inference workloads at peak. Northlight has eight. On a normal day that leaves two spare.

Maintenance headroom asks what is left when a node is out on purpose. Draining gpu-node-2 for an upgrade removes four cards, leaving four for a load that needs six. The upgrade has to wait for a quiet hour, or the assistant gets slow while it runs. To keep six cards serving with any one node drained, the cluster needs 6 + 4 = 10 cards, so a third 4-card node, twelve in all.

Failure capacity asks the same question for an unplanned loss. With twelve cards and one node in maintenance, eight remain. If one card then fails, seven remain, still above six.

Growth and lead time push the other way. If demand grows 4% a month and new servers take six months from order to service, the cluster must be sized for the demand of six months from now: 6 × 1.04^6 = 7.59, so eight cards at peak. Twelve cards with one node drained gives eight, exactly enough. With one card failed during that maintenance it gives seven, below the 7.59 needed. The plan works today and becomes tight within six months.

New generations change the unit of capacity. On H200, chapter 6 showed the same two workloads fit on three cards. A node of four H200 covers today's peak with one card spare, and the growth with it. Buying H100 now locks in the H100 arithmetic for the life of the servers. Waiting for the next generation has its own lead time. The checklist does not choose for Northlight. It makes the choice visible: a third H100 node, or a smaller number of larger cards, and either way the fine-tune runs somewhere else.

Chapter 15 of 16

Item 12, economics and who runs it over its life

Hardware is one line of many, and someone has to operate the rest for years

The twelfth item lists the costs to count: hardware, facilities, energy, networking, storage, software, platform engineering, operations, support and upgrades. It then asks one question that no spreadsheet answers by itself: who will operate the environment over its whole life.

Hardware is the line everyone sees first. Facilities are the space, the power feeds and the cooling. Energy is a running cost that grows with the fleet. At NVIDIA's rated maximum of 700 W, twelve H100 cards draw 8.4 kW, and over the 8,760 hours of a year that is 73,584 kWh for the cards alone. At $0.15 a kWh that is $11,037.60 a year before the CPUs, memory, network, storage and the cooling that removes all of that heat. Networking and storage carry their own hardware and support contracts. Software includes licences for the operating system, the platform and any enterprise support for the GPU stack.

The people lines are the ones most often missing. Platform engineering builds and upgrades the stack of item 7. Operations watches the metrics of item 9, answers the alerts and runs the drains and upgrades. Support answers the teams who use the service. Upgrades repeat for the life of the servers: drivers, CUDA, Kubernetes, vLLM and the model itself, each one a planned change that item 11 had to make room for.

The operating question decides the rest. If Northlight operates the environment itself, it needs a team that can run every layer, on call, for as long as the servers are in service. If a provider operates it, the price includes that team, and the contract has to say who does what. One critical gap outweighs ten green ticks

A diagnostic for this workload, and neither a scorecard nor a certification

It is tempting to read twelve items as a score out of twelve. The checklist rules that out. A single critical gap can matter more than ten completed items, and the goal is to find the requirements that are critical for this workload.

Northlight's pass through the list shows how that works. Ten items came out well: the workloads are written down, the data inventory is complete, the service model is internal, the compute sizing is done and exposed the collision between serving and the fine-tune, the platform and the observability are in place, the access rules and the cost sheet exist. Two items hold critical gaps. Capacity: two 4-card nodes cannot keep six cards serving while one is upgraded, and cannot hold the fine-tune at all. That is where item 4's collision lands as a gap. Network: the fine-tune across two boxes at 25 Gb/s would spend most of each step waiting for gradients. A score of ten out of twelve hides both, and either one would show up as a slow assistant or a fine-tune that never finishes.

The gaps lead to decisions, and the GPU is one of them. A third node, or a move to cards with more memory, closes the capacity gap for inference. Running the quarterly fine-tune in reserved rented capacity, with a fabric built for training, closes the second gap without building that fabric on site. Only now does the original question have an answer, and it is more precise than the one first asked for: which card, how many, in which node shape, for which workload, in which place.

The checklist's own answers to the common questions fit here. GPU selection usually comes after the workload is understood. Kubernetes is not mandatory. Not every enterprise needs on-premises AI. Governance can be added later, and designing it early lowers the risk of rework. The list is not an official certification checklist: it is a planning aid, much as NIST says of its own framework that its actions are not a checklist. Public, private, on-premises and hybrid each solve different workload and operating requirements, so the architecture follows the workload instead of a fixed preference.

What to keep
