Running a GPU cloud

Why a job waits while GPUs are free

Quota, gangs and fragmentation on twelve GPUs

Northlight Compute has twelve H100 GPUs in three servers of four. Tenant A holds six of them and six are free. In the next few minutes three requests will arrive that the scheduler cannot start, and each time free GPUs will be in plain sight on the board. The first is stopped by a quota. The second needs eight GPUs at the same moment and finds six. The third needs four GPUs inside one server and finds two in each of two servers. This lesson follows all three with exact numbers. Before them it shows why a queue of mixed requests served in arrival order leaves GPUs idle and urgent work waiting, where scheduling stops and orchestration begins, how self-service differs from on-demand, and the ways an operator can deliver its cloud. On the way it shows how Kubernetes, Kueue and Volcano express each check, what happens when a queue admits a job that the hardware cannot hold, when waiting work may take GPUs back by preemption, how packing and topology rules keep whole servers free, and what an operator reads in events and metrics while a job waits. It ends with the difference between a GPU that is allocated and a GPU that is doing work.

Chapter 1 of 16

What the scheduler is told

Seven inputs, and a free GPU answers only the first two

Finding a free GPU is only the first thing a scheduler does. Each request names a GPU type and a count. It arrives in a queue, which groups the waiting work of one tenant or one class of job, and it has a priority inside that queue. The tenant has a quota, a ceiling on what it may hold at once. Some capacity is under reservation, promised to one tenant for a period. And the request may state a topology: the GPUs must sit in one server, or in servers that are close together on the network.

The scheduler has to satisfy all seven at once against the twelve H100s that exist. When it can, it hands the result to the provisioner, which builds the machines. When it cannot, the request waits or is refused, whatever the free count says. The order of the checks matters. A quota is checked against the tenant before any server is looked at. A gang and a topology are checked against servers one by one. A priority only matters when two requests want the same GPUs.

The scheduler keeps its inventory per server, because a total hides where the free GPUs are. Right now gpu-srv-1 has 4 GPUs held by tenant A under a reservation, gpu-srv-2 has 2 held by tenant A on-demand and 2 free, and gpu-srv-3 has 4 free. That is 4 + 2 = 6 held and 2 + 4 = 6 free. The GPU type also matters even in a pool of one type. The owner's notes on Nebius describe one KAI scheduler shard per GPU type. The reason the Nebius team gave in its talk is that KAI's queue quotas, as they used them, count GPUs without saying which kind.

Chapter 2 of 16

A mixed queue, served in arrival order

Five requests, two GPU types, and three rules that give three very different pools

Northlight's pool has one GPU type, and most providers run several. Picture a larger Northlight with a fourth server, gpu-srv-4, that holds four NVIDIA L40S cards. An L40S has 48 GB of GDDR6 memory and an H100 has 80 GB. Qwen3-32B's FP8 build, 34.3 GB of weights, fits on one L40S with 48 − 34.3 = 13.7 GB left for the KV cache and the server's working memory. On one H100 it leaves 80 − 34.3 = 45.7 GB. So an inference service can run well enough on the cheaper card, while a training job, which also keeps gradients and optimizer state in memory, wants H100s. The H100 pool is as the board shows it: six held by tenant A, two free on gpu-srv-2 and four free on gpu-srv-3. All four L40S cards are free.

Five requests arrive within twenty seconds, from several teams that are each inside their quota, so quota plays no part here. At 09:00:00 mix-train-8 asks for eight H100s for a training run, as one gang. At 09:00:05 mix-infer-2 asks for two L40S cards for inference. At 09:00:10 mix-ft-1 asks for one H100 for a fine-tune, and at 09:00:15 mix-eval-1 for one H100 for an evaluation. At 09:00:20 mix-prod-4 asks for four H100s in one server for a production model server, at high priority.

Rule one is strict arrival order: nothing starts before everything that came earlier has started. mix-train-8 is first and needs eight H100s. Six are free, so it waits, and the four requests behind it wait with it. The L40S request waits too, although it wants a different card and all four of those are free. Result: 0 of 5 jobs running, and 6 + 4 = 10 GPUs idle. The production job waits behind a training run that may not start for hours.

Rule two is arrival order with each job placed as far as it will go, one worker at a time. mix-train-8 takes all six free H100s, two on gpu-srv-2 and four on gpu-srv-3, and waits for two more. mix-infer-2 starts on two L40S cards. mix-ft-1, mix-eval-1 and mix-prod-4 find 0 free H100s and wait. Result: 1 job doing work on 2 GPUs, 6 H100s held and doing nothing, and the high-priority job still waiting. This is the partial placement that the gang chapter returns to.

Rule three builds a policy from the inputs the first chapter listed. It sorts the requests by priority, because here they all want the same H100s, then matches the GPU type, then checks whether the whole request fits, then packs. mix-prod-4 has the highest priority and needs four H100s in one server. gpu-srv-3 has four free, so it starts there. The rest are tried in arrival order, and one that does not fit is passed over. mix-train-8 needs eight and finds two, so it waits as a gang and holds nothing. mix-infer-2 goes to gpu-srv-4 and takes two of its four L40S cards. mix-ft-1 and mix-eval-1 take the two free H100s on gpu-srv-2. Result: 4 of 5 jobs running, all 12 H100s allocated, 2 L40S cards still free, and one job waiting with nothing held.

Rule three has its own risk. mix-train-8 needs eight H100s free at once, and as long as one-GPU jobs keep arriving they will take every gap first, so it can wait for ever. That is starvation. Kueue lets an operator choose. Both of its queueing strategies order Workloads by priority first and creation time second. StrictFIFO lets an older Workload that cannot be admitted block newer ones even when they fit, which is rule one inside one queue. BestEffortFIFO, the default, lets the newer ones go ahead, which is rule three. A provider usually mixes them: reservations, a higher priority after a long wait, or a queue that runs strict order for large jobs.

The GPU type is the first filter in all of this, and Kubernetes knows it from labels. The Kubernetes docs show an administrator labelling nodes by hand, for example kubectl label nodes node1 accelerator=example-gpu-x100, and a Pod choosing a type with a nodeSelector or node affinity. NVIDIA's GPU Feature Discovery sets labels automatically, among them nvidia.com/gpu.product with the card's model name, nvidia.com/gpu.memory in MiB, and nvidia.com/gpu.count. In Kueue each type becomes a ResourceFlavor with spec.nodeLabels, and a ClusterQueue sets nominalQuota for nvidia.com/gpu separately for each flavor. Kueue gives a Workload the first flavor in the ClusterQueue's list that has enough unused quota and that matches the Pod's nodeSelector, so mix-infer-2 can only ever land on L40S nodes. A quota that counts GPUs without saying which kind would let a tenant spend its count on whichever card is scarcest. That is the reason the first chapter gave for one KAI shard per GPU type.

Chapter 3 of 16

Scheduling is one part of orchestration

The scheduler decides where and when, and other parts build, retry, scale and connect

Everything in this lesson so far is scheduling: deciding which work runs, where and when. The decision itself is small. For a two-GPU job it is a handful of facts: this job, gpu-srv-3, two H100s, now. Orchestration is the wider work around that decision. It creates the workload, runs steps that depend on each other in the right order, retries what fails, scales the number of copies up and down, carries the workload through its lifecycle from creation to deletion, and keeps the relationships between services, such as which address reaches which model server. A scheduler is one component inside an orchestration system. The two words are often used as if they meant the same thing, and they name different jobs.

Provisioning is a third word that gets mixed in. Provisioning prepares a resource for use: it allocates a bare-metal server or creates a VM, sets up drivers, connects the network, attaches storage and creates credentials. Scheduling places a workload on a resource that is ready. At Northlight the scheduler decides, then hands the decision to the provisioner, which does the building. Provisioning prepares the resource, and scheduling places the workload.

Kubernetes shows the split inside one cluster. kube-scheduler watches for newly created Pods that have no node assigned. For each one it filters the nodes down to the feasible ones, scores those, picks the highest score and tells the API server, a step called binding. That is all it does. It never creates a Pod. In the shared cluster northlight-eu, the Deployment qwen3 owns a ReplicaSet, and the ReplicaSet controller creates Pods. If Pod qwen3-6f7d9c8b4-m9q4t on gpu-node-2 is deleted, the ReplicaSet creates a replacement with the same hash and a new five-character suffix, because the revision has not changed. Only then does kube-scheduler place it, the kubelet on the chosen node starts it, vLLM loads the weights for about three minutes, and the Service qwen3 sends it traffic once it reports ready. Five different parts act, and one of them is the scheduler.

Retries are orchestration too. A Job's controller recreates failed Pods up to .spec.backoffLimit, 6 by default, waiting 10 s, then 20 s, then 40 s and so on, capped at ten minutes. When the limit is passed the Job fails with the reason BackoffLimitExceeded. Each recreated Pod goes back to the scheduler as a new placement, and on a full pool it can wait there like any other request. Scaling is the same: an autoscaler changes the replica count, a controller creates the Pods, and the scheduler only finds them homes.

Kubernetes' own overview page uses the word more narrowly. It defines orchestration as the execution of a defined workflow, first do A, then B, then C, and says Kubernetes instead runs independent control loops that keep driving the current state toward the desired state. Among the things those loops provide, the page lists service discovery and load balancing, storage orchestration, automated rollouts and rollbacks, bin packing, self-healing, batch execution, horizontal scaling, and secret and configuration management. In both readings the scheduler is one part.

The split tells an operator where to look. A job that never starts has a scheduling answer: a quota, a gang or a fit, with a FailedScheduling event or a pending Workload. A job that starts, fails and starts again has an orchestration answer: restart counts, back-off delays and controller events such as BackoffLimitExceeded.

Chapter 4 of 16

Reserved, on-demand and spot

The same H100, sold under three different promises

Northlight sells one kind of GPU under three sets of terms. A reservation promises capacity for a period. Tenant A has reserved all of gpu-srv-1 for a year, so those four GPUs are never offered to anyone else, even in an hour when tenant A runs nothing on them. On-demand means ask now, get it if it is there, and give it back at any time. Nothing is promised in advance, so a request can be turned away when the pool is full. Tenant A's two GPUs on gpu-srv-2 are on-demand.

Spot capacity is spare capacity sold on the condition that the provider may take it back. A spot job has to survive being stopped, which in practice means saving checkpoints often and starting again from the last one. The public clouds show how short the warning can be. AWS sends a Spot interruption notice two minutes before it stops or terminates the instance, readable from the instance metadata path /latest/meta-data/spot/instance-action. Google Cloud's Spot VMs get a notice of 0 seconds by default, or 120 seconds if asked for, followed by a best-effort shutdown of up to 30 seconds. A training step on eight GPUs that writes a large checkpoint may not finish inside either window, so the checkpoint has to be written before the notice comes.

To the scheduler these are three rules about who may be given a GPU, and who can lose one. A reserved GPU is placed only for its tenant and is never taken away. An on-demand GPU is kept until its tenant releases it. A spot GPU is the first thing a scheduler takes back when capacity runs short.

Chapter 5 of 16

Self-service is how you ask, on-demand is when

Two properties that usually travel together and can be had one without the other

NIST's definition of cloud computing, SP 800-145, names on-demand self-service as one of five essential characteristics: a consumer can unilaterally provision computing capabilities, such as server time and network storage, as needed automatically without requiring human interaction with each service provider. The phrase joins two separate ideas. Self-service is about how a customer requests and controls a service: through a portal or an API, without a provider employee acting on each request. On-demand is about whether capacity can be requested and released as needed, with no commitment made in advance. They often appear together, and each can exist without the other.

Tenant A's reservation shows the first case. Its engineers could order gpu-srv-1 for a year from the portal, with no call to sales, and that order would be self-service and reserved at once. Self-service also does not mean every request is approved. An on-demand request still passes entitlement, quota and capacity checks, and a provider may add an approval step for large shapes. The chapter Reason one, two chapters on, shows tenant A's on-demand request refused by its quota of six while six GPUs are free. An API is as much self-service as a portal. Northlight's POST /v1/gpus is the self-service path, and a script can call it.

A portal alone does not make a service self-service. Picture a Request button that opens a ticket. An engineer reads it, checks inventory by hand, sets up the network, creates credentials and emails the customer. The customer sees the same screen as at a provider whose systems do all of that, and the operating model behind it is manual. End-to-end self-service connects the request to policy, capacity, provisioning, service state, usage and release, so that the normal path runs without a person. People still own the policies, the exceptions, the capacity plan and the incidents.

Take one order to see the chain: one four-GPU server for seven days. The platform checks who the customer is and what it may order, checks its quota, finds a server with four free H100s, reserves it, provisions compute, network and storage, creates credentials, shows the customer a state, records usage from the moment of handover, and at the end of the seventh day terminates the server, cleans it and returns it to the pool. Seven days is 7 × 24 = 168 hours, so the record that billing reads holds 4 × 168 = 672 GPU-hours.

The hard part is the failure in the middle. Suppose the GPUs are reserved and the network step fails. The workflow has to decide whether to retry, whether to keep the reservation or release it, how to clean up what it half built, what state the customer sees, and that billing must not start. A customer should see a state such as requested, provisioning, active, terminating, failed or retrying, and never a server that is billed and unusable.

Neither property depends on the resource form. Dedicated bare metal can be both self-service and on-demand. OpenStack Ironic, for one, provisions physical machines through an API. On-demand also does not mean usable at once. A bare-metal server needs an image, a network, storage and, after the last tenant, a clean-up, and a model server on it then spends about three minutes loading Qwen3-32B's 34.3 GB of FP8 weights before it can answer.

Chapter 6 of 16

Public, dedicated, private, contract-based or hybrid

Who the cloud serves and on what contract, and where self-service starts to matter

An operator that turns GPUs into a cloud business has more than one way to deliver it, and a public cloud is only one of them. NIST SP 800-145 defines four deployment models by who the infrastructure is provisioned for. A private cloud is for exclusive use by a single organization comprising multiple consumers, such as business units, and may be owned, managed and operated by that organization, a third party or both, on or off premises. A community cloud is for exclusive use by a specific community of organizations with shared concerns, such as mission, security requirements, policy and compliance. A public cloud is for open use by the general public and exists on the premises of the cloud provider. A hybrid cloud is a composition of two or more distinct cloud infrastructures that remain separate but are bound together by technology that lets data and applications move between them, for example cloud bursting for load balancing between clouds.

Operators selling GPU capacity describe their offers in commercial terms that cut across those four. This lesson sorts those offers into five delivery models: a customer-facing GPU service can be public, dedicated, private, contract-based or hybrid. Public means anyone can sign up and order from the catalogue. Dedicated means specific hardware belongs to one customer inside a shared cloud: dedicated GPU instances, dedicated hosts, bare metal or a reserved cluster. Tenant A's reservation of gpu-srv-1 is dedicated in this sense, four H100s that serve no one else while Northlight stays a public cloud. Private means the whole environment serves one organization, run by that organization or by Northlight on its behalf, in Northlight's halls or in the customer's own. Contract-based means the capacity and its price are set by a negotiated agreement, such as a block of servers for a fixed term with a monthly invoice, and the commercial layer may be settlement, internal showback or a consortium's split instead of per-hour billing. Hybrid means a customer runs some work on its own or private capacity and some on Northlight, for example bursting extra inference replicas to Northlight at peak.

The scheduler sees each of these as a rule about who may be placed where. A dedicated or private server is placed only for its owner, like tenant A's reserved gpu-srv-1. A contract-based block is a reservation with a long term. A public on-demand request competes for whatever is unreserved and free. Hybrid adds a second pool the scheduler may not control at all.

Self-service is not required in every contract model. A one-year contract for a dedicated cluster can be negotiated by an account team and set up once by engineers, and nothing in that model breaks if it takes a week. Self-service becomes necessary when the same thing must happen many times a day. As an illustration, a pool that takes 50 on-demand orders a day at 20 minutes of manual work each spends 50 × 20 = 1,000 minutes, about 16.7 hours of staff time every day, and every order waits for a person before its server even starts provisioning. The reservation of gpu-srv-1 needed that work once. The more an offer depends on short, repeated, on-demand use, the more its operator needs the end-to-end self-service path from the previous chapter.

Chapter 7 of 16

Reason one: the tenant is at its quota

Six GPUs are free, and tenant A may not have one of them

Tenant A's quota is six GPUs. It holds six: four reserved on gpu-srv-1 and two on-demand on gpu-srv-2. An engineer asks for one more H100. Six are free, and every one of them is the right type. The scheduler adds the request to what the tenant holds, gets 6 + 1 = 7, compares that with six, and refuses. It never looks at a server. A quota is a rule about the tenant, and the state of the pool does not enter into it.

Kubernetes behaves the same way. A ResourceQuota with requests.nvidia.com/gpu set to 6 makes the API server reject a seventh GPU with 403 Forbidden while nodes sit empty. Only the requests. form is allowed for GPUs, because extended resources cannot be overcommitted. The message names the quota and three numbers: what was requested, what is used, and the limit. A quota only gates creation. Lowering it below current use stops nothing that is already running, and when two requests race for the last unit of quota, the first one wins.

The refusal is not always where the engineer looks. A bare Pod is refused at submission. A Deployment or a Job over quota is accepted, and its controller then fails to create the Pods, so the 403 appears in the events of the workload object and the engineer sees a Deployment with fewer Pods than asked. Quotas can also be split by priority with a scopeSelector, so a namespace may have, for example, a separate GPU ceiling for high-priority Pods. The fix in every case is a larger quota or a released GPU. Adding servers to the pool changes nothing for this request.

Chapter 8 of 16

Quota in a queue: Kueue and borrowing

A queue keeps the request instead of refusing it, and can lend unused quota

A queueing system turns the refusal into a wait. In Kueue each Job becomes a Workload in a LocalQueue, a namespaced queue that points to one ClusterQueue. The ClusterQueue holds the quota, as nominalQuota for each resource and each ResourceFlavor, and serves only the namespaces its namespaceSelector matches. A Workload over quota is not rejected. kubectl get workloads lists it with empty RESERVED IN and ADMITTED columns, and kubectl describe shows the condition Admitted False with reason Pending and the message workload didn't fit. kubectl get localqueues shows a PENDING WORKLOADS count for each queue.

Admission has two phases. First Kueue reserves quota in the ClusterQueue. Then any admission checks run, and the Workload is admitted when all of them are Ready. Inside a ClusterQueue the queue order is first by priority and then by creation time. The default strategy, BestEffortFIFO, lets a newer Workload that fits go ahead of an older one that does not. StrictFIFO makes the older one block everything behind it. An administrator can stop admission with stopPolicy Hold, or HoldAndDrain, which also evicts what was admitted.

ClusterQueues can join a cohort and lend each other unused quota. With no borrowingLimit, a ClusterQueue can borrow up to the sum of the nominal quotas in its cohort, and lendingLimit caps what it lends. The owner's notes on Nebius describe the same idea with the KAI scheduler: each workload gets a guaranteed quota that stays available, and demand above quota competes for spare GPUs by business priority. Before that, they managed the pool by hand, with a spreadsheet and an engineer evicting Pods. Borrowing lets a quota be a floor as well as a ceiling. If tenant A's ClusterQueue could borrow from tenant B's unused nominal quota, its seventh GPU would be admitted, and it could be taken back when tenant B needs it.

Chapter 9 of 16

Reason two: eight together, or none

Six of eight is worth nothing to a job that needs eight

Tenant B submits a training job with eight workers, one GPU each. The workers exchange results at every step, an all-reduce of their gradients, so the job makes progress only when all eight are running. Tenant B's quota is ten and it holds nothing, so quota is no obstacle: 0 + 8 = 8, within ten. Six GPUs are free: two on gpu-srv-2 and four on gpu-srv-3.

A scheduler that places workers one at a time would start six and leave two waiting. Those six GPUs would then be held by tenant B, counted against its quota, and do no work until two more appear, which could take hours. Nobody else could use them in the meantime. Worse, two such jobs can block each other. If job X holds five GPUs and waits for three, and job Y holds the other seven and waits for one, neither ever starts. That is a deadlock made of idle GPUs.

Gang scheduling is the rule that prevents this. The eight workers are treated as one group. The group is placed when all eight fit, and until then it holds nothing. The six free GPUs stay free, so a smaller job can use them. The cost is that a large job can wait a long time while small jobs keep taking the gaps it needs. Queue order, priorities and reservations exist partly to stop that starvation. The guarantee is about placement. Once placed, a gang can lose a member if a Pod is later deleted or evicted, and a training framework has to handle that on its own.

Chapter 10 of 16

How a batch scheduler admits the whole group

Volcano has a PodGroup, Kueue has a Workload, Kubernetes now has its own PodGroup

Kubernetes has gang scheduling of its own, in beta and disabled by default behind the GenericWorkload feature gate. The gate was alpha in 1.35 and 1.36 and is beta from 1.37. A PodGroup of API version scheduling.k8s.io/v1beta1 sets spec.schedulingPolicy.gang.minCount, and each Pod names its group in spec.schedulingGroup.podGroupName. The scheduler holds the Pods at PreEnqueue until the group exists and at least minCount Pods have been created, then makes one decision for the whole group. If fewer than minCount can be placed, none are, and they all move to the unschedulable queue.

In most clusters today gang scheduling still comes from an add-on. In Volcano the group is a PodGroup. Its field minMember says how many Pods must be able to run, and minResources adds a second gate on total resources. Volcano's enqueue action moves a group from Pending to Inqueue only when the cluster can meet its minimum, and its allocate action binds Pods only when the whole gang can be placed. A group that cannot fit carries an Unschedulable condition with the reason NotEnoughResources.

In Kueue the unit is a Workload, created for each Job. Kueue keeps the Job suspended, checks the whole Workload against the quota of its ClusterQueue, and admits it only when all of it fits in that quota. Only then does it unsuspend the Job, and the Pods are created. The Job's events tell the story in order: Suspended, CreatedWorkload, Started with Admitted by clusterQueue, a SuccessfulCreate for each Pod, Resumed and finally Completed.

Kueue's all-or-nothing is about quota, and quota is a number. Tenant B's quota is 10 and it holds 0, so 0 + 8 = 8 fits, and Kueue on its own would admit train-8 at once while only six GPUs are free. The eight Pods would then be created, six would be placed and two would stay Pending, holding six GPUs between them. That is exactly the partial placement that gang scheduling is meant to stop. Kueue closes the gap only when it is configured to. waitForPodsReady evicts and requeues a Workload whose Pods are not all ready in time, and Topology Aware Scheduling checks the real capacity of the nodes before admission. In this lesson train-8 waits in Northlight's own scheduler, which checks the hardware for the whole group, as Volcano and the native PodGroup do. So train-8 holds nothing.

Meanwhile tenant B sends eval-2, a job that needs two GPUs. Because train-8 holds nothing, it fits, and it starts.

Chapter 11 of 16

When admission meets real GPUs

A quota can say yes while the servers say no, and Kueue has a timer for that

Kueue's quota is a number, and real GPUs can run short of it. Tenant B now holds 2 and its nominal quota is 10, so 2 + 8 = 10 fits exactly. Without Topology Aware Scheduling, Kueue would admit train-8 on quota alone and unsuspend the Job. The Pods would be created, and the cluster's scheduler would find room for only 4 of them, two on gpu-srv-2 and two on gpu-srv-3. Four Pods would hold GPUs and do nothing, which is the partial placement gang scheduling was meant to stop.

waitForPodsReady is the backstop. When it is on, Kueue watches each admitted Workload. If its Pods are not all ready within the timeout, 30 minutes by default in current docs, Kueue cancels the admission, suspends the Job again, deletes its Pods and requeues the Workload. It waits before trying again, with a backoff that starts at backoffBaseSeconds, 60 by default, and doubles: about 60, 120, 240 seconds and so on, up to backoffMaxSeconds, 3600. With no backoffLimitCount the Workload is requeued without end. With one, it is deactivated when the limit is reached, and the eviction reason reads PodsReadyTimeout with the cause RequeuingLimitExceeded. The count and the next attempt are in .status.requeueState.

Two more settings change the picture. With blockAdmission set to true, Kueue admits Workloads one after another, so two half-placed gangs can never hold each other's GPUs. And a Job can declare the smallest size it accepts with the annotation kueue.x-k8s.io/job-min-parallelism. Kueue lowers the count only after borrowing and preemption have both been ruled out. train-8 cannot run with fewer than eight, so it does not set it.

Chapter 12 of 16

Preemption: when waiting work takes GPUs back

A higher priority can evict a lower one, and the defaults rarely let it

A waiting job can sometimes get its GPUs by evicting someone else. In kube-scheduler this follows Pod priority. A PriorityClass has a value from -2147483648 to 1000000000 for ordinary classes, while the two system classes sit at 2000000000 and 2000001000. A Pod without a priorityClassName gets 0 unless a class is marked globalDefault. When a Pod cannot be placed, the scheduler looks for lower-priority Pods whose removal would make room. If it finds them, it sets the waiting Pod's status.nominatedNodeName and evicts the victims, who get their normal grace period. The Pod is not guaranteed to land on that node in the end. PodDisruptionBudgets are respected on a best effort basis only. A class with preemptionPolicy Never queues ahead of lower priorities without evicting anyone.

Kueue adds its own rules at the ClusterQueue, and all three default to Never. reclaimWithinCohort lets a queue take back quota it lent. borrowWithinCohort lets a queue preempt while borrowing. withinClusterQueue lets a Workload evict a lower-priority one in its own queue. Victims are chosen from borrowing queues first, then the lowest priority, then the most recently admitted.

For train-8 none of this helps. Tenant A holds 6 of its own 6, borrows nothing, and is in another queue, so there is nothing to reclaim. The owner's Nebius notes show where preemption does pay: above-quota replicas run on spare GPUs and are evicted when a queue reclaims its guarantee. In the Nebius team's account, a preemption delay in their KAI setup waits so the autoscaler can add nodes first. An evicted model server stops getting new requests at once, while streams in flight get only the grace period, 30 seconds by default.

Chapter 13 of 16

Reason three: four free, two and two

The total is right and no server has room

Tenant B now wants to serve a model that is too large for one card. The model server is one process that spreads the model over four GPUs, and a process can only use the cards of the server it runs on. Those four cards exchange data at every layer over the server's own fast links, which do not reach the next server. So the request says four H100s in one server.

Quota passes: two held plus four is six, under ten. The total passes too, because four GPUs are free. They are two on gpu-srv-2 and two on gpu-srv-3, and no server has four. The request waits. This is fragmentation: free capacity that no request of this shape can use. It was made a minute ago, when eval-2 was put on the emptiest server.

The emptiest server was not chosen by accident. Northlight's scheduler spreads small jobs: it scores a server higher the more GPUs it has left. That spreads load and leaves headroom on every server, which suits web services. For GPU jobs it is the habit that makes 2 + 2.

kube-scheduler's defaults are often thought to do the same, and they do something narrower. Its default scoring strategy is LeastAllocated, which prefers the node with the most room left, but the default list of resources it scores is cpu and memory only, with a weight of 1 each. nvidia.com/gpu is not in that list, so the default score ignores how many GPUs a node has free. Under the defaults a GPU Pod lands where CPU and memory use, and other scorers such as BalancedAllocation and ImageLocality, point it, which can spread small GPU jobs or pack them by chance. A scheduler steers by GPU count only once nvidia.com/gpu is added to the scored resources, and that is exactly what the next chapter configures.

Packed next to tenant A's pair on gpu-srv-2, eval-2 would have left gpu-srv-3 whole, and serve-4 would start now. Schedulers call that choice bin packing. Defragmenting after the fact means moving a running job, which for a model server is a restart with a three-minute load of its weights, so the cheap moment to prevent fragmentation is at placement.

Chapter 14 of 16

Packing and topology, set before the jobs arrive

Score fuller servers higher, and let a request name the boundary it must stay inside

Bin packing is a setting. In kube-scheduler the NodeResourcesFit plugin takes a scoring strategy. MostAllocated scores a node higher the fuller it already is, and each resource gets a weight. The Kubernetes docs show two extended resources weighted 3 against 1 for cpu and memory, which gives them three times the influence on the score. Weighting nvidia.com/gpu that way makes the scheduler fill a server's GPUs before opening the next one. RequestedToCapacityRatio is the other strategy. It takes a shape, a list of points that map how full a node is to a score, so a provider can draw any curve it likes. Volcano has a binpack plugin with a weight for each resource, whose docs say it keeps room on idle machines for Pods with larger requests.

Topology needs its own rules, because a free count per server is not the whole story. Kueue's Topology Aware Scheduling, beta since v0.14 and on by default, uses a Topology object that lists levels such as block, rack and host, referenced from a ResourceFlavor's topologyName. A PodSet asks with an annotation. kueue.x-k8s.io/podset-required-topology keeps all Pods inside one domain at that level. podset-preferred-topology tries that level, then the next one up, and finally admits the Pods spread out. TAS counts only Ready nodes that are not cordoned, and subtracts what is already running there, including DaemonSets.

Inside one server the kubelet's Topology Manager can go further. Its policies are none, the default, best-effort, restricted and single-numa-node. Under single-numa-node a Pod whose GPUs and CPUs cannot come from one NUMA node is rejected at admission and ends Terminated, and the scheduler does not try it elsewhere. The owner's Nebius notes chose KAI partly for its NUMA-aware placement of GPUs and CPUs together.

Chapter 15 of 16

What an operator sees while a job waits

Every check that fails leaves a message, a metric or a queue position

When kube-scheduler cannot place a Pod it records a FailedScheduling event. The message follows a fixed form: 0/X nodes are available, then the reasons from the filter phase, then the result of trying preemption. A GPU shortage from the NodeResourcesFit plugin reads Insufficient nvidia.com/gpu, counted per node. For serve-4 that gives 0/3 nodes are available: 3 Insufficient nvidia.com/gpu, followed by a preemption part such as No preemption victims found for incoming pod. The scheduler retries an unschedulable Pod with a backoff that starts at 1 second and grows to at most 10. When it finally succeeds, the event is Scheduled with Successfully assigned and the namespace, Pod and node.

The metrics tell the same story for the whole cluster. scheduler_pending_pods is a stable gauge with a queue label: active, backoff, unschedulable and gated, plus incomplete and pending for pod groups. scheduler_schedule_attempts_total counts results, where unschedulable means a Pod could not be placed and error means the scheduler itself failed. Kueue reports kueue_pending_workloads by ClusterQueue and status. active means waiting to be tried, and inadmissible means an attempt failed and will not be retried until something in the cluster changes. Histograms such as kueue_admission_wait_time_seconds show how long admission takes, and kueue_evicted_workloads_total counts evictions by reason, among them Preempted and PodsReadyTimeout.

A tenant can ask where it stands. Kueue's visibility API returns the pending Workloads of a ClusterQueue with positionInClusterQueue and positionInLocalQueue, up to 1000 entries by default. Volcano writes its answer on the PodGroup as an Unschedulable condition with the reason NotEnoughResources. The useful reply to a customer quotes one of these and names the check that failed.

Chapter 16 of 16

Allocated is not the same as used

One number says who holds the GPUs, another says whether they work

Now count. Tenant A holds six GPUs and tenant B holds two, so eight of twelve are allocated, which is 67 percent. That number says who holds what. It says nothing about work. Tenant A's two on-demand GPUs belong to a notebook that someone left open, and their utilization gauge reads zero. Six GPUs are busy, which is 50 percent. Metering records allocation, because a GPU that is held cannot be sold to anyone else, and the bill follows that record.

Utilization itself is a blunt number. NVIDIA's management library defines GPU utilization as the percent of time, over the last sample period, during which one or more kernels was running. A GPU that runs one tiny kernel all the time reads 100 while most of its cores sit idle. dcgm-exporter publishes that gauge. From release 4.9.0 its default counters call it DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL_RATIO, a ratio from 0 to 1. Releases up to 4.8.4 called it DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL and reported percent, so a dashboard or alert written against one release's names or units can miss the series, or read a value a hundred times off, on the other. Check the release notes for names kept for compatibility. The exporter adds profiling ratios such as how often the tensor cores are active, DCGM_FI_PROF_TENSOR_UTIL_RATIO from 4.9.0 and DCGM_FI_PROF_PIPE_TENSOR_ACTIVE before it, and how busy the memory interface is, DCGM_FI_PROF_DRAM_UTIL_RATIO. Those finer numbers are the ones that show whether a model server is actually using its H100.

A scheduler can raise allocation by packing small jobs into gaps and keeping whole servers free for large ones. It cannot raise utilization inside a job. That depends on the customer's data pipeline, CPUs, storage, network and batch size. Two requests are still in the queue. The pool has four free GPUs, and neither request can use them. An idle allocated GPU is the customer's to waste. A free GPU that no queued request can use is the provider's loss, and it is the one a scheduler can do something about.

What to keep

Free is a count, and placing a job is a match

Three requests waited while GPUs were free, and each failed a different check. Tenant A was at its quota of six, so six free GPUs were out of its reach. Tenant B's job of eight found six, and a gang scheduler placed none of it so that the six stayed usable. Then a request for four GPUs in one server found two and two, left behind by Northlight's scorer, which spreads small jobs. A scheduler matches a request against type, count, queue, priority, quota, reservations and topology, all at once. The free count on a dashboard answers none of those questions alone. Served in plain arrival order, a mixed queue of five requests started none of them while ten GPUs sat idle, and the same queue under priority, type, gang and packing rules started four. The scheduler only places work. Provisioning, retries, scaling and lifecycle belong to the orchestration around it, and whether a customer can ask by itself, and on what terms, is decided by the service and its contract. When a customer asks why a job is waiting, the useful answer names the check that failed. And once a job does run, the number that the bill follows is allocation, whether the GPU works or idles.
